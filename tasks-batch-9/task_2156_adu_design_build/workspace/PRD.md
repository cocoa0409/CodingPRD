# ADU Design-Build API — Product Requirements Document

## 1. Introduction

California's accessory dwelling unit market has turned a formerly boutique remodeling niche into a high-volume production business, and the operational reality of that business is that no two parcels are governed by the same rules. A backyard cottage that sails through ministerial review in one city fails discretionary zoning in the next; a homeowner who can borrow comfortably against a capitalized rent projection cannot borrow at all against a sales-comparison appraisal that finds no ADU comps; a utility shutoff that is a footnote on a vacant lot is a habitability emergency when the family is still living in the front house. Design-build firms absorb all of this complexity in spreadsheets and email threads, and the cost of that absorption is measured in blown permit windows, unbudgeted change orders, and certificates of occupancy that arrive months after the borrower's rate lock expired.

This document specifies the backend service that replaces those spreadsheets. The service tracks an ADU project from the first feasibility screening through the certificate of occupancy, and it does so as an authoritative system of record rather than a passive datastore: it prices permit fees from the governing ordinance, scores the separate-service versus shared-metering decision, reconciles the contract ledger every time the homeowner changes their mind, blocks construction windows that would leave an occupied dwelling without water for more than a day, and refuses to issue a final certificate until every gate — energized utilities, completed phases, a passed final inspection signed by an inspector who has not already signed anything else on that project — is genuinely satisfied.

Two structural properties of the domain shape almost every rule that follows. First, a project advances along two independent axes at once: a design-and-build lifecycle and a funding lifecycle. A plan-check correction letter moves the first and leaves the second untouched; an approved change order moves the second and leaves the first untouched. Second, corrections and change orders both *loop* — plan check can bounce a package any number of times, and a homeowner can revise a change order until the project manager accepts it — so the service must remain correct on the second, third, and fourth pass through the same states, not merely on the happy path.

## 2. Global Specifications & System-Wide Principles

### 2.1. User and Role-Based Access Control

Every request to this API — without exception, including reads — must carry two headers: `X-User-ID`, identifying the caller, and `X-User-Role`, naming the capacity in which they act. If either header is absent or empty, the request is rejected with `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED` (HTTP 401). The same 401 and the same `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED` identifier are returned when `X-User-Role` carries a string that is not one of the seven roles below — an unrecognized role is treated as a failure to authenticate, not as a permission problem. Only once the role is recognized does the service ask whether that role is listed for the endpoint being called; when it is not, the answer is `ERR_ROLE_NOT_PERMITTED` (HTTP 403).

The seven roles are `PlatformAdmin`, `DesignLead`, `PlanChecker`, `ProjectManager`, `Homeowner`, `BuildingInspector`, and `FinanceAdvisor`.

A `PlatformAdmin` is the builder-platform operator who registers permitting jurisdictions and jurisdiction inspectors and who drives the service's logical clock. A `DesignLead` is the builder's designer of record: they run feasibility screenings, decide metering, prepare and file permit packages, and clear plan-check corrections. A `PlanChecker` is jurisdiction plan-check staff, recording plan-check outcomes and issuing the certificate of occupancy, and never touching contract money. A `ProjectManager` is the builder's construction manager: they sequence phases, book inspections, energize services, and decide change orders. A `Homeowner` is the property owner. A `BuildingInspector` is a jurisdiction field inspector. A `FinanceAdvisor` is the loan and appraisal advisor who prices financing options and commits the project to one.

Two of these roles are **entity-backed**, meaning `X-User-ID` names a stored row and ownership checks compare a resource's foreign key against it. When `X-User-Role` is `Homeowner`, `X-User-ID` is the `id` of the acting `Property`; ownership of any project therefore resolves through that project's `property_id`, and a `Homeowner` acting on a project whose `property_id` differs from `X-User-ID` is rejected with `ERR_NOT_PROJECT_OWNER` (HTTP 403). This check guards two operations: raising a change order against a project, and a homeowner's own submission of a change order for decision. When `X-User-Role` is `BuildingInspector`, `X-User-ID` is the `id` of the acting `Inspector`; recording a result on an appointment whose `inspector_id` is some other inspector is rejected with `ERR_NOT_ASSIGNED_INSPECTOR` (HTTP 403).

The remaining five roles — `PlatformAdmin`, `DesignLead`, `PlanChecker`, `ProjectManager`, and `FinanceAdvisor` — carry **opaque** identity. For each of them, `X-User-ID` is an operator identifier that is not tied to any persisted entity, and the service never compares it to any row's `id`. It may still be recorded: when a `ProjectManager` decides a change order, their `X-User-ID` is stored on that change order as `decided_by_user_id`. Likewise the `Homeowner` who creates a change order has their `X-User-ID` copied into `authored_by_user_id`, and the `BuildingInspector` who records an inspection outcome has theirs copied into `recorded_by_inspector_id`.

Segregation of duties is enforced structurally rather than by convention. A `Homeowner` authors change orders and may never decide one; a `ProjectManager` decides them and may never author one. An inspector who has already recorded any result on a project may not sign that project's `FINAL` inspection; the attempt is rejected with `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES` (HTTP 403).

### 2.2. API Response Structure

#### Standard Success Response Format

All successful API responses (those with 2xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format in their JSON body:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z",
    "limit": 10,
    "offset": 0,
    "total_count": 100,
    "has_more": true
  },
  "response_type": "object" | "array",
  "data": { }
}
```

- The `api_request_id` is a server-generated unique identifier for the request that starts with `req_`.
- The `api_request_timestamp` is a server-generated ISO 8601 UTC timestamp with three fractional digits.
- The `response_type` is either "object" for a single resource or "array" for a list of resources.
- The `data` field contains the resource object or an array of resource objects. For 2xx responses with no data, it contains an empty object. Unless otherwise noted, operations that create or modify a resource will return the created or updated resource object in the data field.
- Pagination-related fields in `meta` (`limit`, `offset`, `total_count`, `has_more`) are included for list endpoints.

#### Standard Error Response Format

All error API responses (4xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z"
  },
  "response_type": "error",
  "data": {
    "error_id": "UNIQUE_ERROR_IDENTIFIER_STRING",
    "message": "A human-readable explanation of the error."
  }
}
```

- The `meta` object for error responses contains only `api_request_id` and `api_request_timestamp`.
- The `error_id` is a unique, machine-readable string that identifies the specific error.
- The `message` provides a human-readable explanation of what went wrong.


### 2.3. Pagination

Every collection endpoint accepts two optional query parameters, `limit` and `offset`. `limit` defaults to 10 when omitted, may be no smaller than 1, and may be no larger than 100. `offset` defaults to 0 and may be no smaller than 0. A `limit` outside the range of 1 through 100, or a negative `offset`, is rejected with `ERR_VALIDATION` (HTTP 422) — the service does not silently clamp out-of-range pagination.

Unless a specific listing states otherwise, collections are returned sorted by `created_at` in ascending order, so the oldest row comes first. Wherever a narrative below says "oldest first", it is describing exactly this default. The `meta` block of a list response carries `limit` and `offset` as they were applied, `total_count` giving the number of rows matching the filters before the window was applied, and `has_more`, which is true when rows remain beyond the returned window.

### 2.4. Common Resource Properties, Versioning, and the Logical Clock

Every stored resource carries a server-minted `id`. Identifiers are UUID strings with no type prefix of any kind — an ADU project's identifier looks exactly like an inspector's. Clients never supply an `id`; supplying one has no effect.

Most resources also carry three bookkeeping fields. `created_at` and `updated_at` are ISO 8601 UTC timestamps with three fractional digits. `version` is an integer that starts at 1 when the row is created and increases by exactly 1 on every mutation of that row, whether the mutation was requested directly or arrived as a cascade from somewhere else. Any operation that changes a row also refreshes that row's `updated_at`.

Two entities are exceptions and carry only `id` and `created_at`: a feasibility screening (`FeasibilityScreening`) and an occupancy certificate (`OccupancyCertificate`). Both are immutable records of a decision frozen at a moment in time, so neither has an `updated_at` and neither has a `version`. Nothing in this API mutates them after creation, and no optimistic-concurrency field ever refers to them.

Separately from `created_at`, the service maintains a **logical clock**. Before any operator has moved it, the logical clock reads exactly `2026-08-01T00:00:00Z`, and it never falls back to real wall-clock time. A `PlatformAdmin` may move it to a supplied `to_iso8601` instant, and the response reports the new setting as `current_utc`. Every business date the service stamps is the date part of that clock: `submitted_on_date` and `approved_on_date` on a permit submittal, `correction_deadline_date` on a submittal awaiting corrections, `resolved_on_date` on a correction item, `energized_on_date` on a utility connection, `started_on_date` and `completed_on_date` on a construction phase, and `issued_on_date` on an occupancy certificate. All of these are plain calendar dates in `YYYY-MM-DD` form.

### 2.5. Path Resolution and Referential Integrity

Path identifiers are resolved strictly left to right, and the first segment that does not resolve determines the error. A request naming both a project and a nested resource under it therefore reports the missing project before it ever considers the nested identifier. Each entity has its own not-found identifier, all of them HTTP 404: `ERR_JURISDICTION_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_INSPECTOR_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_PROPERTY_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_PROJECT_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_SUBMITTAL_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_CORRECTION_ITEM_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_UTILITY_CONNECTION_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_FINANCING_SCENARIO_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_CHANGE_ORDER_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_PHASE_NOT_FOUND`, and `ERR_APPOINTMENT_NOT_FOUND`.

Foreign keys supplied inside a request body are resolved the same way and report the same identifiers. This covers `jurisdiction_id` when registering an inspector or a parcel, `property_id` when opening a project, `screening_id` when locking in a pathway, `inspector_id` when booking an inspection, `depends_on_phase_id` when adding a construction phase behind a predecessor, and `supersedes_change_order_id` when a change order replaces an earlier one. A `screening_id` gets its own identifier, `ERR_SCREENING_NOT_FOUND` (HTTP 404), which is returned both when no screening carries that identifier and when the screening exists but belongs to a different project than the one named in the path.

### 2.6. Optimistic Concurrency on Parcels and Projects

Three creates in this API are guarded by optimistic concurrency control, because in each case the create is legitimate from any state of its parent — it may supersede something that already exists — and so the parent's state alone cannot tell a fresh writer from a stale one. In each case the guarding field is a required, non-optional part of the request body carrying the parent `version` the client last observed. Endpoints not named here take no version field at all.

Opening an ADU project on a parcel — `POST /adu-projects` — requires `expected_property_version`, carrying the client's last observed `version` of that `Property`. A parcel may legitimately carry a redesigned project that supersedes an earlier one, so the create is valid from any parcel state; what the check stops is a writer working from an outdated view of the parcel's project set. If the supplied value differs from the parcel's current `version`, the request is rejected with `ERR_STALE_PROPERTY_VERSION` and HTTP 412, and nothing is created.

Pricing a financing scenario against a project — `POST /adu-projects/{id}/financing-scenarios` — requires `expected_adu_project_version`, carrying the client's last observed `version` of that `AduProject`. Because a new scenario supersedes the project's prior active one, a second create is legitimate from any project state; the version check rejects the writer whose observed project version predates a supersede that has already happened. A mismatch is rejected with `ERR_STALE_PROJECT_VERSION` and HTTP 412.

Raising a change order against a project — `POST /adu-projects/{id}/change-orders` — also requires `expected_adu_project_version`. A change order is legitimate from any project state that is not final and may supersede an earlier one, so the create cannot be refused on state alone; the version check rejects a homeowner whose observed project version predates a price-moving approval or supersede. A mismatch is likewise rejected with `ERR_STALE_PROJECT_VERSION` and HTTP 412.

### 2.7. Rejection Precedence

Many operations in this API can fail for several reasons at once, and the identifier a caller receives is not arbitrary: checks run in a fixed order and the **first** failure decides the response. Implementations must evaluate in the sequence below, and must not reorder checks to whatever is cheapest.

1. **Authentication.** Presence and non-emptiness of both headers, then recognition of the role name — both surfacing as `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED`.
2. **Endpoint authorization.** Whether the recognized role appears among the roles the endpoint permits — `ERR_ROLE_NOT_PERMITTED`.
3. **Path existence.** Every identifier in the URL, left to right, per §2.5.
4. **Body foreign-key existence.** Every foreign key inside the request body, per §2.5.
5. **Identity-bound authorization.** The homeowner-ownership check and the assigned-inspector check described in §2.1, yielding `ERR_NOT_PROJECT_OWNER` and `ERR_NOT_ASSIGNED_INSPECTOR` respectively. Note the ordering consequence: a homeowner acting on someone else's project learns that before any complaint about the shape of their payload.
6. **Request shape.** Required fields present and correctly typed; enum-typed fields carrying a member of their declared set; numeric, range, and format constraints satisfied; pagination bounds satisfied on list endpoints. Every failure here is `ERR_VALIDATION` (HTTP 422).
7. **Optimistic concurrency.** The version guards of §2.6.
8. **State preconditions.** Whether the target resource is in a state from which the requested action has a declared transition, and the specific gating conditions numbered from there.
9. **Safety, compliance, referential, uniqueness, temporal, and fiscal checks**, in the order each workflow section below sets out.

Within the shape checks of step 6, the concrete domain constraints are these: `proposed_sqft` is at least 150 and at most 1200; `proposed_height_ft` is strictly greater than 0 and at most 35; `bedrooms` is at least 0 and at most 4; `schedule_delta_days` is at least negative 30 and at most 120; `daily_capacity` is at least 1 and at most 12; a jurisdiction `code` is 2 to 8 characters drawn only from uppercase A through Z and the digits 0 through 9; every calendar date matches the `YYYY-MM-DD` pattern; `slot_date` equals the UTC date part of `slot_start`; and `slot_end` is strictly later than `slot_start`. Additional per-field constraints appear alongside the workflows that use them.

### 2.8. Money, Rounding, and Fee Precision

All monetary amounts in this API are integers denominated in **cents**, and every field carrying money says so in its name. There is no floating-point currency anywhere in a stored row or a response.

Two rounding disciplines are used. The first, applied to every fee and every builder markup, rounds half away from zero to whole cents — so a computed fee of 1250.5 cents becomes 1251, and a computed credit of negative 1250.5 cents becomes negative 1251. The second, applied to the cumulative change percentages on a change order, rounds half away from zero to two decimal places.

The order in which rounding is applied is part of the contract, not an implementation detail. Where a total is described as the sum of several rate-driven components, each component is rounded to whole cents **first** and the already-rounded components are then added. Computing the exact real-valued components, summing them, and rounding once at the end produces a different answer and is incorrect. One calculation in this document — the maximum lendable amount — deliberately uses truncation toward zero rather than half-up rounding; that exception is called out where it applies.

### 2.9. The Two Axes of a Project, and Cascading Consistency

An ADU project carries two independent state fields on the same row. `status` is the design, permit, and build lifecycle, taking values from `INTAKE`, `FEASIBILITY_CLEARED`, `PERMITTING`, `DESIGN_REVISION`, `PERMITTED`, `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION`, and `FINALED`; it begins at `INTAKE` and `FINALED` is terminal. `financing_state` is the funding lifecycle, taking values from `UNFUNDED`, `PRE_QUALIFIED`, `COMMITTED`, and `SHORTFALL`; it begins at `UNFUNDED` and has no terminal value. The two axes advance on unrelated triggers and never gate one another except where a rule below explicitly says so.

Several operations in this system are not local: a single request can move a chain of rows. Where that happens, every row in the chain is genuinely mutated — its own `version` increases by 1 and its own `updated_at` is refreshed — and every row is processed in `created_at` ascending order when more than one sibling is affected. The two longest chains are worth stating up front because everything in §3 assumes them.

The **plan-check correction chain** has four links. A corrections letter on a submittal under plan check creates the correction rows; because the submittal entered its corrections state, the owning project moves from `PERMITTING` to `DESIGN_REVISION`; because the project left `PERMITTING`, every construction phase of that project that was `SCHEDULED` moves to `BLOCKED` and gives up its window; and because each of those phases entered `BLOCKED`, every inspection appointment on them that was still `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL` is `CANCELLED`, releasing the inspector's slot.

The **change-order funding chain** has three links. Approving a change order stamps the markup and percentages on the change order itself; because the change order became `APPROVED`, the project's current contract price, approved-change day count, and approved-change count are all recomputed; and because the contract price moved, the project's single committed financing scenario is repriced, which can push `financing_state` from `COMMITTED` to `SHORTFALL`.

Voiding an approved change order is the exact compensating action for approving it: the contract ledger is recomputed over the change orders that remain approved, and the funding axis can move back from `SHORTFALL` to `COMMITTED`. Cancelling an inspection is likewise the compensating action for booking one — the inspector's slot count on that date returns to what it was before the booking.

### 2.10. Interval Semantics: Property Windows Versus Inspector Slots

Two different overlap regimes coexist in this API, and confusing them is the single most common source of wrong answers.

A construction phase's disruption window on a parcel is a **closed** interval of calendar dates: both `window_start_date` and `window_end_date` are inside the window, and a one-day window has its start and end on the same date. Windows that merely touch at their endpoints therefore collide with one another. On an occupied parcel a further habitability buffer of 2 calendar days is added on each side before the comparison is made, so two windows on an occupied parcel must be separated by more than two clear days to coexist.

An inspection appointment's slot is a **half-open** interval of instants: `slot_start` is inside the slot and `slot_end` is not, and no buffer is applied. Back-to-back bookings, where one appointment starts at precisely the instant another ends, are therefore accepted rather than rejected.

## 3. Business Logic & Workflows

### 3.1. Standing Up a Jurisdiction, Its Inspectors, and a Parcel

Before any project exists, a `PlatformAdmin` records the permitting jurisdictions the builder works in. A jurisdiction registration carries a human-readable `name` and a short `code` — 2 to 8 characters, uppercase letters and digits only — that must be unique across every jurisdiction on record; a second attempt to use a code that is already taken is rejected with `ERR_DUPLICATE_JURISDICTION_CODE` (HTTP 409). Alongside the identity fields, the registration captures the ordinance itself: `local_max_detached_sqft`, the largest detached ADU the city allows, which must be at least 150; `local_rear_setback_ft` and `local_side_setback_ft`, each of which may be zero or more; and `local_max_height_ft`, which must be strictly greater than zero.

The same registration carries the jurisdiction's fee schedule and rental posture. `plan_check_base_fee_cents` is the flat plan-check component and `plan_check_rate_per_sqft_cents` the per-square-foot component; `impact_fee_per_sqft_cents` is the development impact rate; `utility_capacity_fee_cents` is what the city charges to bring in a new separate service connection. All four are expressed in cents and may be zero or more. Four further fields have defaults and may be omitted: `correction_response_days`, the number of calendar days an applicant has to answer a corrections letter, defaults to 60 and must be at least 1; `separate_meter_required` defaults to false and is true only where the ordinance forbids shared metering for new detached units; `str_min_lease_days`, the shortest lease term the ordinance permits for an ADU, defaults to 30 and must be at least 1; and `str_prohibited` defaults to false and is true where short-term rental of an ADU is barred outright.

Jurisdiction inspectors are registered next, also by a `PlatformAdmin`, and each is tied to an employing jurisdiction through `jurisdiction_id`. An inspector row carries a `full_name` as it appears on inspection cards and a `certifications` array naming the disciplines they may sign off; the array must be non-empty and every member must be one of `STRUCTURAL`, `ELECTRICAL`, `PLUMBING`, `ENVELOPE`, or `FINAL`. Two optional fields round out the record: `daily_capacity`, the maximum number of appointments the inspector may hold on any one calendar date, which defaults to 4 and must lie between 1 and 12 inclusive; and `status`, which is either `ACTIVE` or `INACTIVE` and defaults to `ACTIVE`. Inspector listings may be narrowed by employing `jurisdiction_id` and by `status`. Anyone may also ask what an inspector's day looks like by naming a `slot_date`, and the answer reports that inspector's `inspector_id`, the `slot_date` asked about, their `daily_capacity`, a `booked_count` counting only appointments still in `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL`, and the `remaining_slots` left over.

A parcel is registered by a `PlatformAdmin` or a `DesignLead`, and it is the parcel row whose `id` doubles as the homeowner's identity, so registering the parcel is what brings a homeowner into existence. Each parcel names its `jurisdiction_id` and its `address_line`, and describes the physical lot with `lot_sqft` and `existing_dwelling_sqft`, both of which must be strictly greater than zero. Two capacity fields drive most of the feasibility and metering logic downstream: `electrical_service_amps`, the size of the existing main service, which must be exactly one of 100, 125, 150, 200, or 400; and `existing_water_meter_size_in`, the domestic meter size in inches, which must be exactly one of 0.625, 0.75, 1.0, or 1.5. Finally, `pre_adu_value_cents` records the appraised value of the property before the ADU and must be strictly greater than zero. Three optional fields carry defaults: `occupied`, true when the family is still living in the front house during construction, defaults to true; `in_str_overlay`, true when the parcel sits inside a short-term-rental restriction overlay, defaults to false; and `existing_mortgage_balance_cents`, the outstanding first-lien balance, defaults to 0 and may not be negative. Parcel listings may be narrowed by `jurisdiction_id`.

### 3.2. Opening a Project and Screening It for Feasibility

A `DesignLead` opens an ADU project against a registered parcel by naming its `property_id` together with the `expected_property_version` guard described in §2.6. The design itself is described at intake: `unit_type` is one of `DETACHED_NEW`, `ATTACHED`, `CONVERSION`, or `JADU`; `proposed_sqft` is the conditioned floor area; `proposed_height_ft` is the ridge height above grade; `rear_setback_ft` and `side_setback_ft` are the proposed setbacks, each zero or more; `bedrooms` counts the sleeping rooms; and `contract_base_price_cents` is the signed design-build price before any change order, which must be strictly greater than zero. The new project opens with `status` `INTAKE`, `financing_state` `UNFUNDED`, and `approved_pathway` null, with `max_allowed_sqft`, `approved_change_days`, `approved_change_order_count`, and `total_permit_fee_cents` all at 0, and with `contract_price_current_cents` seeded equal to `contract_base_price_cents`. Opening the project also counts as a mutation of the parcel, so the parcel's `version` rises by 1. Project listings may be narrowed by `property_id` and by lifecycle `status`.

Feasibility is not a single verdict but a comparison between two competing rule sets, and the same project may legitimately be screened under both. Each screening names a `pathway` — either `LOCAL_ORDINANCE`, meaning the homeowner accepts the city's discretionary ADU limits, or `STATUTORY_MINISTERIAL`, meaning they invoke the state's ministerial pathway — and freezes its entire result on the spot. A screening row is immutable: it records the `adu_project_id` it was run against and never changes afterwards, which is why the same project can carry a `FAIL` under the local ordinance and a `PASS` under the statutory pathway simultaneously. Screening listings for a project may be narrowed by `pathway`.

Under `LOCAL_ORDINANCE`, the requirement set is copied straight off the jurisdiction: `max_allowed_sqft` becomes the jurisdiction's `local_max_detached_sqft`, `required_rear_setback_ft` becomes its `local_rear_setback_ft`, `required_side_setback_ft` becomes its `local_side_setback_ft`, and `required_height_ft` becomes its `local_max_height_ft`. Under `STATUTORY_MINISTERIAL` the state floor takes over: `max_allowed_sqft` becomes whichever is larger of 800 square feet and the jurisdiction's `local_max_detached_sqft`, so a restrictive city can never push the ceiling below the state's always-allowed area; `required_height_ft` becomes a flat 16.0 feet regardless of local rules; and the setbacks become a flat 4.0 feet on both the rear and the side — except for a `CONVERSION` or a `JADU`, where converting existing space demands no new setback at all and both `required_rear_setback_ft` and `required_side_setback_ft` are 0.0.

Independently of pathway, the screening sizes the electrical load. `required_service_amps` is 100 when `proposed_sqft` is at most 750, 125 when `proposed_sqft` is greater than 750 but at most 1000, and 200 when `proposed_sqft` exceeds 1000. The minimum acceptable water meter is 0.75 inches once the unit reaches 800 square feet or more, and 0.625 inches below that threshold — note that this is the same 800-square-foot figure the statutory pathway uses as its floor-area floor, applied here to plumbing rather than zoning.

Four checks are then evaluated and each is recorded on the screening. `zoning_ok` is true when `proposed_sqft` is no greater than `max_allowed_sqft`. `setback_ok` is true when `rear_setback_ft` is at least `required_rear_setback_ft` **and** `side_setback_ft` is at least `required_side_setback_ft`. `height_ok` is true when `proposed_height_ft` is no greater than `required_height_ft`. `utility_capacity_ok` is true when the parcel's `electrical_service_amps` is at least `required_service_amps` plus the 100 amps reserved for the existing dwelling's own load, **and** the parcel's `existing_water_meter_size_in` is at least the minimum meter size just derived. Every one of these comparisons is inclusive at the boundary — a proposal that lands exactly on the ceiling passes.

Whichever checks failed are listed in `deficiency_codes`, drawn from `ZONING`, `SETBACK`, `HEIGHT`, and `UTILITY_CAPACITY` and sorted ascending in ASCII order, so `HEIGHT` precedes `SETBACK`, which precedes `UTILITY_CAPACITY`, which precedes `ZONING`. The `verdict` then follows a strict precedence: if zoning, setback, or height failed, the verdict is `FAIL`; otherwise, if only the utility capacity check failed, the verdict is `PASS_WITH_CONDITIONS`, because a service upgrade is a solvable problem where a zoning violation is not; and if all four checks passed, the verdict is `PASS`. A screening consults exactly one project, its parcel, and that parcel's jurisdiction — never any other screening.

Locking the project onto a pathway is what clears it out of intake, advancing `status` from `INTAKE` to `FEASIBILITY_CLEARED`. The `DesignLead` names the `screening_id` they are adopting. The project must still be in `INTAKE`; a second attempt to select a pathway on a project that has already left intake is rejected with `ERR_PROJECT_PHASE_INVALID` (HTTP 409). The named screening must belong to this project, or the request is rejected with `ERR_SCREENING_NOT_FOUND`. And the screening's `verdict` must be either `PASS` or `PASS_WITH_CONDITIONS` — adopting a `FAIL` screening is rejected with `ERR_FEASIBILITY_FAILED` (HTTP 409). On success the project's `approved_pathway` is set to the screening's `pathway`, its `max_allowed_sqft` is copied from the screening, and the project's `version` rises by 1.

### 3.3. Separate Service or Shared Metering

Each utility service the ADU needs gets its own record on the project — a `UtilityConnection` — opened by a `DesignLead` who names the `service_type` — `WATER`, `SEWER`, or `ELECTRIC`. A project may hold at most one record per service type; asking for a second `WATER` record on the same project is rejected with `ERR_DUPLICATE_UTILITY_SERVICE` (HTTP 409). A newly opened record starts in `PENDING_DECISION` with its `decision` null, its `separation_score` at 0, and its `separation_cost_cents` at 0. These listings may be narrowed by `service_type` and by `status`.

The metering study itself is a scored decision, run by the `DesignLead`, and it may only run on a record still in `PENDING_DECISION` — a record that has already been decided is rejected with `ERR_UTILITY_STATE_INVALID` (HTTP 409). It also may not run before the project has locked in a pathway: if `approved_pathway` is still null, the request is rejected with `ERR_PATHWAY_NOT_SELECTED` (HTTP 409).

Scoring starts from the same electrical tier table feasibility uses — 100 amps for a unit of at most 750 square feet, 125 amps above that up to and including 1000 square feet, and 200 amps beyond 1000 square feet — and subtracts both that figure and the 100 amps the existing dwelling already draws from the parcel's `electrical_service_amps`, giving the electrical headroom. The score then accumulates four positive contributions and one deduction. Forty points are added when the jurisdiction's `separate_meter_required` is true. Twenty-five points are added when the electrical headroom is negative, meaning the existing service genuinely cannot carry both dwellings. Twenty points are added when `unit_type` is `DETACHED_NEW`. Fifteen points are added when `bedrooms` is 2 or more. Ten points are then subtracted when the parcel's `existing_water_meter_size_in` is 1.0 inch or larger, since an oversized meter argues for sharing. The resulting total is clamped into the range 0 through 100 and stored as `separation_score`.

A score of 50 or more resolves the `decision` to `SEPARATE`; a score of 49 or below resolves it to `SHARED`. The threshold is inclusive: a score landing exactly on 50 is a separate service. An operator may supply an optional `override_decision` of `SEPARATE` or `SHARED` with the study; when present it replaces the resolved `decision` outright, but it never replaces `separation_score`, which is always stamped from the scored expression regardless. The two branches then diverge on cost. A `SEPARATE` decision costs the jurisdiction's `utility_capacity_fee_cents` plus a flat 850,000 cents of builder work for the new meter set, and moves the record from `PENDING_DECISION` to `SEPARATE_APPROVED`. A `SHARED` decision costs a flat 220,000 cents for the submeter and moves the record from `PENDING_DECISION` to `SHARED_APPROVED`. Either way `separation_cost_cents` is stamped with the chosen branch's figure and the record's `version` rises by 1 — and because that figure feeds the permit fee, the two branches produce materially different permits.

Once the permit is in hand, a `ProjectManager` records that the utility has energized the service, moving the record from either `SEPARATE_APPROVED` or `SHARED_APPROVED` to `ENERGIZED` and stamping `energized_on_date` with the logical clock date. Energizing requires that the project's most recently created permit submittal — the newest by `created_at` — carries `APPROVED` status; otherwise the request is rejected with `ERR_PERMIT_NOT_APPROVED` (HTTP 409). Attempting to energize a record still in `PENDING_DECISION` is an `ERR_UTILITY_STATE_INVALID` rejection.

One reversal exists on this entity. When the first change order on a project is approved and it carries a non-zero `cost_delta_cents`, the metering study is invalidated by the scope change: every utility record of that project sitting in `SEPARATE_APPROVED` reverts to `PENDING_DECISION`, its `decision` returns to null, its `separation_score` and `separation_cost_cents` return to 0, and its `version` rises by 1. The study must then be re-run. Records already `ENERGIZED` are unaffected, as are records in `SHARED_APPROVED`.

### 3.4. Pricing the Permit, Filing It, and Surviving Plan Check

Preparing a design package is a `DesignLead` action that names `design_sheet_count`, the number of sheets in the package, which must be at least 1, and `title24_compliant`, a flag asserting that the package includes a passing Title 24 energy compliance report. The new `PermitSubmittal` is created in `PREPARED` status with `cycle_number` 0, `design_revision` 1, and `submitted_on_date`, `correction_deadline_date`, and `approved_on_date` all null — and with its fee already priced. Submittal listings for a project may be narrowed by `status`.

The permit fee is the sum of four separately rounded components. `plan_check_component_cents` is the jurisdiction's `plan_check_base_fee_cents` plus its `plan_check_rate_per_sqft_cents` multiplied by the project's `proposed_sqft`, with a 7.5 percent technology surcharge applied to that subtotal — that is, the subtotal multiplied by 1.075 — and the result rounded half away from zero to whole cents. `impact_component_cents` is zero for any unit strictly smaller than 750 square feet; at 750 square feet and above it is the jurisdiction's `impact_fee_per_sqft_cents` multiplied by `proposed_sqft`, multiplied again by a proportionality factor that is the lesser of 1.0 and the ratio of `proposed_sqft` to the parcel's `existing_dwelling_sqft`, rounded half away from zero to whole cents. `school_component_cents` is zero for any unit strictly smaller than 500 square feet; at 500 square feet and above it is a statewide rate of 452 cents per square foot multiplied by `proposed_sqft`, carrying the same 7.5 percent technology surcharge, rounded half away from zero to whole cents. `utility_component_cents` is the sum of `separation_cost_cents` across every utility record of the project whose status is `SEPARATE_APPROVED`, `SHARED_APPROVED`, or `ENERGIZED`, enumerated in `created_at` ascending order; records still in `PENDING_DECISION` contribute nothing. `total_permit_fee_cents` is then the sum of those four already-rounded components, per the rounding-order rule in §2.8.

Filing the package with the jurisdiction moves the submittal from `PREPARED` to `UNDER_PLAN_CHECK`, sets its `cycle_number` to 1, and stamps `submitted_on_date` with the logical clock date. Four conditions guard the filing, evaluated in this order. The submittal must be in `PREPARED`, or the answer is `ERR_SUBMITTAL_STATE_INVALID`. The owning project must be in `FEASIBILITY_CLEARED`, `PERMITTED`, or `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION`, or the answer is `ERR_PROJECT_PHASE_INVALID` — the second and third of those states exist because deferred submittals and permit revisions are filed after the original permit has already issued. Every utility record of the project must have left `PENDING_DECISION`, or the answer is `ERR_UTILITY_DECISION_PENDING` (HTTP 409). And `title24_compliant` must be true, or the answer is `ERR_TITLE24_MISSING` (HTTP 409). On success the project also moves onto its permitting axis: from `FEASIBILITY_CLEARED` to `PERMITTING`, or from `PERMITTED` back to `PERMITTING`, or from `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION` back to `PERMITTING`, in each case raising the project's `version` by 1 alongside the submittal's own.

Plan check answers with one of two outcomes, recorded by a `PlanChecker` who supplies `outcome`. An `APPROVED` outcome closes the permit: the submittal moves `UNDER_PLAN_CHECK → APPROVED`, `approved_on_date` is stamped from the logical clock, the project moves `PERMITTING → PERMITTED`, and the submittal's `total_permit_fee_cents` is copied onto the project's own `total_permit_fee_cents` field. Approval is refused while any correction on the submittal has `severity` `BLOCKING` and `status` `OPEN`, with `ERR_BLOCKING_CORRECTIONS_OPEN` (HTTP 409).

A `CORRECTIONS_REQUIRED` outcome opens a correction round, and it must carry substance. The request supplies a `correction_items` array; if that key is omitted, or present but empty, the letter is rejected with `ERR_NO_CORRECTIONS_ON_RECORD` (HTTP 409) — a corrections letter carrying zero corrections is not a corrections letter. Each element is an object with `discipline`, one of `STRUCTURAL`, `ENERGY`, `FIRE`, `PLANNING`, or `UTILITY`; `severity`, either `BLOCKING` or `ADVISORY`; and `description`, the plan checker's text. One `CorrectionItem` row is created per element, in request order, each in `OPEN` status with `resolved_on_date` null and each pointing back at its parent through `permit_submittal_id`. The submittal moves `UNDER_PLAN_CHECK → CORRECTIONS_REQUIRED` and its `correction_deadline_date` is set to the logical clock date advanced by the jurisdiction's `correction_response_days`. From there the four-link cascade of §2.9 runs in full: the project moves `PERMITTING → DESIGN_REVISION`; every construction phase of the project sitting in `SCHEDULED` moves to `BLOCKED` with both `window_start_date` and `window_end_date` cleared to null, processed oldest first; and every inspection appointment on each of those phases that was `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL` moves to `CANCELLED`, releasing the inspector's slot on its `slot_date`, again processed oldest first.

A `DesignLead` clears individual corrections one at a time, moving a row `OPEN → RESOLVED` and stamping `resolved_on_date` from the logical clock. A row that is already `RESOLVED` is rejected with `ERR_CORRECTION_ITEM_STATE_INVALID` (HTTP 409), and a correction may only be cleared while its parent submittal is actually in `CORRECTIONS_REQUIRED` — otherwise the answer is `ERR_SUBMITTAL_STATE_INVALID`. Correction listings under a submittal may be narrowed by `severity` and by `status`.

Returning the corrected package to plan check moves the submittal `CORRECTIONS_REQUIRED → UNDER_PLAN_CHECK`, increases both `cycle_number` and `design_revision` by 1, clears `correction_deadline_date` back to null, and moves the project `DESIGN_REVISION → PERMITTING`. This is the loop: a package can go around `UNDER_PLAN_CHECK → CORRECTIONS_REQUIRED → UNDER_PLAN_CHECK` once per correction round, with `design_revision` counting the rounds. Two gates apply, in this order. First the deadline: if the logical clock date is later than `correction_deadline_date`, the submittal is first moved `CORRECTIONS_REQUIRED → EXPIRED` — a persisted transition that clears `correction_deadline_date` to null and raises the submittal's `version` — and the resubmit call is then rejected with `ERR_CORRECTION_WINDOW_EXPIRED` (HTTP 409). Expiry is evaluated lazily, at the moment a resubmit is attempted, and `EXPIRED` is terminal. Second, if any correction on the submittal still has `severity` `BLOCKING` and `status` `OPEN`, the resubmit is rejected with `ERR_BLOCKING_CORRECTIONS_OPEN`. A submittal carrying only `ADVISORY` corrections passes this check with none of them resolved.

### 3.5. Financing the Build, and the Appraisal Problem

A `FinanceAdvisor` prices a loan option as a **financing scenario** (a `FinancingScenario` row) against the project, supplying the `expected_adu_project_version` guard of §2.6 alongside four inputs: `product_type`, one of `HELOC`, `CASH_OUT_REFI`, `RENOVATION_LOAN`, or `CONSTRUCTION_LOAN`; `appraisal_approach`, either `SALES_COMPARISON` or `INCOME_CAPITALIZATION`; `comparable_adjustment_cents`, the contributory value an appraiser assigned to the ADU under the sales-comparison approach, which may not be negative; and `projected_monthly_rent_cents`, the projected market rent for the completed unit expressed as cents per month, which may also not be negative. Both value inputs are supplied on every scenario even though only one of them will be consulted, so that the two approaches can be compared side by side on identical inputs.

Creating a scenario supersedes whatever came before it: any scenario of the same project still in `ACTIVE` moves to `SUPERSEDED`, and the new row is created in `ACTIVE` with every derived field stamped. If the project's `financing_state` was `UNFUNDED`, it advances to `PRE_QUALIFIED`; from `PRE_QUALIFIED`, `COMMITTED`, or `SHORTFALL` the funding axis is left exactly where it was, because pricing another option is not itself a funding event. Either way the project's `version` rises by 1. Scenario listings for a project may be narrowed by `status` and by `appraisal_approach`.

The valuation itself is where the two approaches part company, and the gap between them is the reason this API exists. Under `SALES_COMPARISON`, `as_completed_value_cents` is the parcel's `pre_adu_value_cents` plus nothing more than `comparable_adjustment_cents` — the contributory value the appraiser could defend from comparable sales, which in most California submarkets lands far below what the unit cost to build. Under `INCOME_CAPITALIZATION`, `as_completed_value_cents` is the parcel's `pre_adu_value_cents` plus the capitalized net rent: take `projected_monthly_rent_cents`, annualize it by multiplying by 12, apply a vacancy factor of 0.92 and then an operating-expense haircut that retains 65 percent of what remains (that is, multiply by one minus an expense ratio of 0.35), divide the result by a capitalization rate of 0.055, round that capitalized figure half away from zero to whole cents, and add it to the pre-ADU value. For the same parcel and the same project cost, the income approach can exceed the sales-comparison adjustment by a large multiple, flipping a scenario from unfundable to fundable without a single physical change to the design.

From the as-completed value the borrowing capacity follows. `max_loan_cents` is `as_completed_value_cents` multiplied by a platform loan-to-value ceiling of 0.80, **truncated toward zero** rather than rounded — this is the one place in the API that floors instead of rounding half up. `available_cents` is `max_loan_cents` less the parcel's `existing_mortgage_balance_cents`, and it may legitimately be negative. On the other side of the ledger, `required_funds_cents` is the project's `contract_price_current_cents` as it stands at evaluation time, plus the project's `total_permit_fee_cents`, plus a soft-cost contingency of 10 percent of `contract_price_current_cents` rounded half away from zero to whole cents. `funding_gap_cents` is `required_funds_cents` less `available_cents`, floored at 0 so it is never negative. Finally `appraisal_shortfall` is true when the value the ADU actually added — `as_completed_value_cents` less the parcel's `pre_adu_value_cents` — is strictly less than `required_funds_cents`, which is the polite way of saying the homeowner will not get their money back on paper. A scenario reads only itself, its project, and that project's parcel; other scenarios on the same project, whether `ACTIVE` or `SUPERSEDED`, never contribute to its numbers.

Committing the project to a scenario moves that scenario `ACTIVE → COMMITTED` and moves the project's funding axis from `PRE_QUALIFIED` to `COMMITTED`, or from `SHORTFALL` to `COMMITTED` when a homeowner has re-shopped the loan after a change order opened a gap. The scenario must be in `ACTIVE`; a `SUPERSEDED` or already-`COMMITTED` scenario is rejected with `ERR_SCENARIO_STATE_INVALID` (HTTP 409). Its `funding_gap_cents` must be exactly 0, or the commit is rejected with `ERR_FUNDING_GAP_OPEN` (HTTP 409). On success, any other scenario of the same project already sitting in `COMMITTED` moves to `SUPERSEDED`, so a project holds at most one committed scenario at any moment — this invariant is what lets the change-order cascade speak of "the project's committed scenario" in the singular. Both the scenario's `version` and the project's `version` rise by 1.

### 3.6. Homeowner Change Orders and the Contract Ledger

A `Homeowner` raises a change order — a `ChangeOrder` row — against their own project, supplying the `expected_adu_project_version` guard, a `title` labelling the request, a `scope_items` array of free-text scope lines being added or removed, a `cost_delta_cents` figure that is signed so that negative values are credits, and a `schedule_delta_days` figure that is likewise signed and must lie between negative 30 and 120 inclusive. An optional `supersedes_change_order_id` names an earlier change order this one replaces. A change order must carry substance: one whose `scope_items` array is empty **and** whose `cost_delta_cents` is 0 **and** whose `schedule_delta_days` is 0 is rejected with `ERR_EMPTY_CHANGE_ORDER` (HTTP 422). The new row is created in `DRAFT` with `builder_fee_cents` at 0, both `cumulative_change_pct_before` and `cumulative_change_pct_after` at 0, `decided_by_user_id` null, and `authored_by_user_id` set to the acting `X-User-ID`; the project's `version` rises by 1. Change-order listings may be narrowed by `adu_project_id`, by `supersedes_change_order_id`, and by `status`.

Sending a change order for decision moves it `DRAFT → SUBMITTED`. The same homeowner action serves double duty in the revision loop: applied to a change order sitting in `REVISION_REQUESTED`, it moves the row **backwards** to `DRAFT` and clears `decided_by_user_id` to null rather than moving it forward. The full loop is therefore `DRAFT → SUBMITTED → REVISION_REQUESTED → DRAFT`, and it can run as many times as the parties need. From any other state the action is rejected with `ERR_CHANGE_ORDER_STATE_INVALID` (HTTP 409). Both raising and sending are ownership-guarded per §2.1.

A `ProjectManager` decides a submitted change order by supplying `decision`, one of `APPROVE`, `REJECT`, or `REQUEST_REVISION`; in all three cases the change order must be in `SUBMITTED` or the answer is `ERR_CHANGE_ORDER_STATE_INVALID`, and in all three cases `decided_by_user_id` is set to the deciding `X-User-ID` and the change order's `version` rises by 1. `REQUEST_REVISION` moves the row `SUBMITTED → REVISION_REQUESTED` and has no fiscal consequences whatsoever. `REJECT` moves the row `SUBMITTED → REJECTED`, which is terminal, and likewise leaves the project's `contract_price_current_cents`, `approved_change_days`, and `approved_change_order_count` completely untouched. Only `APPROVE` moves money.

Approval carries two gates beyond the state check. The project must be in `PERMITTED` or `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION` — approving against a project in any other lifecycle state is rejected with `ERR_PROJECT_PHASE_INVALID`. And the reconciliation below must not breach the cumulative change cap.

The reconciliation looks circular — the markup feeds the price and the tier is keyed on a percentage — so its evaluation order is pinned, and the percentage is **always** measured against `contract_base_price_cents`, never against `contract_price_current_cents`. First, the cumulative prior is the sum, over every change order of the same project that already carries `APPROVED` status excluding the one being decided, of that row's `cost_delta_cents` plus its `builder_fee_cents`, enumerated in `created_at` ascending order; rows in `DRAFT`, `SUBMITTED`, `REVISION_REQUESTED`, `REJECTED`, or `VOIDED` are excluded entirely. Second, `cumulative_change_pct_before` is that cumulative prior divided by `contract_base_price_cents`, expressed as a percentage by multiplying by 100, rounded half away from zero to two decimal places. Third, the markup tier is selected from that "before" percentage: strictly below 5.00 percent the builder takes 10 percent; at 5.00 percent and strictly below 15.00 percent the builder takes 15 percent; at 15.00 percent and above the builder takes 20 percent. The tier boundaries are strict in exactly this sense — a "before" percentage of exactly 5.00 selects the 15 percent rate, and exactly 15.00 selects the 20 percent rate. Fourth, `builder_fee_cents` is this change order's `cost_delta_cents` multiplied by the selected rate, rounded once, half away from zero, to whole cents; on a credit this rounds away from zero too, so a computed fee of negative 1250.5 cents becomes negative 1251. Fifth, the project's `contract_price_current_cents` becomes `contract_base_price_cents` plus the cumulative prior plus this change order's `cost_delta_cents` plus its `builder_fee_cents`. Sixth, `cumulative_change_pct_after` is the cumulative prior plus this `cost_delta_cents` plus this `builder_fee_cents`, divided by `contract_base_price_cents`, multiplied by 100, and rounded half away from zero to two decimal places. Seventh and last, once the fee is known, the cap is checked: if `cumulative_change_pct_after` is **strictly greater** than 25.00 percent, the approval is rejected with `ERR_CHANGE_CAP_EXCEEDED` (HTTP 409) and nothing at all is persisted — not the fee, not the percentages, not the project price. A result landing exactly on 25.00 percent is allowed through.

When the approval does go through, the three-link cascade of §2.9 runs. The change order itself receives `builder_fee_cents`, `cumulative_change_pct_before`, `cumulative_change_pct_after`, and `decided_by_user_id`. The project then takes the new `contract_price_current_cents`, grows `approved_change_days` by this row's `schedule_delta_days`, grows `approved_change_order_count` by 1, and raises its `version` by 1. Finally, because the contract price moved, the project's single `COMMITTED` financing scenario has its `required_funds_cents` and `funding_gap_cents` recomputed against the new price; if the recomputed `funding_gap_cents` is greater than 0, the project's `financing_state` moves `COMMITTED → SHORTFALL`. Separately from that chain, if `approved_change_order_count` was 0 immediately before this approval and this change order's `cost_delta_cents` is not 0, the metering reversal described in §3.3 fires as well.

Voiding an approved change order is the compensating action, available to a `ProjectManager` and refused with `ERR_PROJECT_PHASE_INVALID` once the project has reached `FINALED`. A change order not in `APPROVED` is rejected with `ERR_CHANGE_ORDER_STATE_INVALID`. On success the row moves `APPROVED → VOIDED`, its `builder_fee_cents`, `cumulative_change_pct_before`, and `cumulative_change_pct_after` all reset to 0, and the project's ledger is rebuilt over the change orders that remain `APPROVED`: `contract_price_current_cents` is recomputed, `approved_change_days` falls by the voided row's `schedule_delta_days`, and `approved_change_order_count` falls by 1. The project's committed financing scenario is repriced the same way it was on approval, and when the recomputed `funding_gap_cents` is 0 the project's `financing_state` moves `SHORTFALL → COMMITTED`, closing the funding cycle. Both the change order's `version` and the project's `version` rise by 1.

### 3.7. Sequencing Construction Around a Family That Never Moved Out

Once a permit is in hand, a `ProjectManager` lays out the build as a sequence of `ConstructionPhase` rows on the project. Each phase names its `phase_code` — one of `SITE_PREP`, `FOUNDATION`, `FRAMING`, `ROUGH_MEP`, `UTILITY_TIE_IN`, `INSULATION_DRYWALL`, or `FINISHES` — together with a `disruption_class` of `NON_INVASIVE`, `INVASIVE`, or `UTILITY_SHUTOFF` describing how badly it disturbs the front house, and a `sequence_index` of 0 or more giving the builder's intended ordering. An optional `depends_on_phase_id` points at a predecessor phase that must finish first. Phases are created in `PLANNED` with `window_start_date`, `window_end_date`, `started_on_date`, and `completed_on_date` all null. Phase listings may be narrowed by `adu_project_id`, by `depends_on_phase_id`, and by `status`.

Booking a phase's disruption window stamps `window_start_date` and `window_end_date` and moves the phase to `SCHEDULED`. A phase may be booked from `PLANNED` or from `BLOCKED` — the second case is what lets a project recover after a corrections letter blocked its schedule, closing the loop `SCHEDULED → BLOCKED → SCHEDULED` — and from any other state the answer is `ERR_PHASE_STATE_INVALID` (HTTP 409). The owning project must be in `PERMITTED` or `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION`, or the answer is `ERR_PROJECT_PHASE_INVALID`. The window must be non-empty in the sense that `window_end_date` is the same as or later than `window_start_date`.

Two conflict checks then guard the booking, in this order. The **habitability rule** comes first: when the parcel's `occupied` flag is true and the phase's `disruption_class` is `UTILITY_SHUTOFF`, the requested window may span no more than a single calendar day counting both endpoints, meaning `window_start_date` and `window_end_date` must be the same date; a longer shutoff on an occupied parcel is rejected with `ERR_OCCUPIED_HABITABILITY_VIOLATION` (HTTP 409). A family can camp out for one night without water; two is a displacement.

The **property window rule** comes second and applies to every phase regardless of disruption class. Windows are closed intervals as described in §2.10, and the buffer is 2 calendar days when the parcel's `occupied` flag is true and 0 days otherwise. The booking is rejected with `ERR_PHASE_WINDOW_CONFLICT` (HTTP 409) when any *other* construction phase belonging to a project on the same parcel is in `SCHEDULED` or `IN_PROGRESS` and its window, widened by the buffer on both sides, still overlaps the requested one — concretely, when the requested `window_start_date` is on or before that phase's `window_end_date` advanced by the buffer, and that phase's `window_start_date` is on or before the requested `window_end_date` advanced by the buffer. Because the comparison is inclusive at both ends, two windows that merely touch collide even when the buffer is 0. Note that the scope is the *parcel*, not the project: a second ADU project on the same parcel competes for the same windows.

Starting work moves a phase `SCHEDULED → IN_PROGRESS` and stamps `started_on_date` from the logical clock. Two conditions guard it. The project's most recently created permit submittal — newest by `created_at` — must be `APPROVED`, or the answer is `ERR_PERMIT_NOT_APPROVED`; this is the same freshness rule that governs energizing a utility, and it means a permit revision filed mid-build stops new phase starts until it clears. And if the phase names a `depends_on_phase_id`, that predecessor must have reached `COMPLETE`, or the answer is `ERR_PHASE_DEPENDENCY_INCOMPLETE` (HTTP 409); a phase with a null `depends_on_phase_id` skips this check entirely. The first phase to start on a project also advances the project itself, moving `status` from `PERMITTED` to `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION`; on later starts the project's status is already `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION` and is left alone.

Closing out a phase moves it `IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETE` and stamps `completed_on_date`. It is refused while any inspection appointment on that phase is still in `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL`, with `ERR_OPEN_INSPECTIONS_REMAIN` (HTTP 409) — a partial inspection is an open inspection, and the builder must either finish it or cancel it before the phase can close. `COMPLETE` is terminal for a phase.

### 3.8. Booking Inspections, and Who Is Allowed to Sign

A `ProjectManager` books a jurisdiction inspection against a specific construction phase, naming the `inspector_id` who will hold the slot, the `inspection_type` — one of `FOUNDATION`, `FRAMING`, `ROUGH_MEP`, `INSULATION`, or `FINAL` — the `slot_date`, and the half-open interval `slot_start` to `slot_end`. The resulting `InspectionAppointment` records the `construction_phase_id` it was booked against and copies the phase's project into its own `adu_project_id` at creation, so that project-wide queries need not walk the phase. New appointments are created in `SCHEDULED` with `result_notes` and `recorded_by_inspector_id` both null, and the booking consumes one of that inspector's slots on `slot_date`. Appointment listings may be narrowed by `adu_project_id`, `construction_phase_id`, `inspector_id`, `recorded_by_inspector_id`, `slot_date`, and `status`.

Four checks guard a booking, evaluated in this order. First, the inspector's `certifications` array must cover the requested `inspection_type`: a `FOUNDATION` or `FRAMING` inspection requires `STRUCTURAL`; a `ROUGH_MEP` inspection requires **both** `ELECTRICAL` and `PLUMBING`; an `INSULATION` inspection requires `ENVELOPE`; and a `FINAL` inspection requires `FINAL`. A gap is rejected with `ERR_INSPECTOR_NOT_CERTIFIED` (HTTP 409). Second, the inspector's `status` must be `ACTIVE`; booking against an `INACTIVE` inspector is rejected with `ERR_INSPECTOR_INACTIVE` (HTTP 409). Third comes the slot conflict: the booking is rejected with `ERR_INSPECTOR_SLOT_CONFLICT` (HTTP 409) when any other appointment held by the same inspector with status `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL` overlaps the requested interval — concretely, when the requested `slot_start` is strictly earlier than that appointment's `slot_end` and that appointment's `slot_start` is strictly earlier than the requested `slot_end`. Because both comparisons are strict, an appointment starting at precisely the instant another ends is accepted. Fourth comes the day's aggregate load: the count of appointments already held by that inspector on the same `slot_date` with status `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL` must be strictly less than the inspector's `daily_capacity` before the new row is written; at or beyond capacity the booking is rejected with `ERR_INSPECTOR_DAILY_CAPACITY_EXCEEDED` (HTTP 409). Note that these two checks measure different things — a day can be free of interval overlaps and still be full.

Recording an outcome is reserved to the `BuildingInspector` the slot was booked to. The appointment must be in `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL`; one already in `PASSED`, `FAILED`, or `CANCELLED` is rejected with `ERR_APPOINTMENT_STATE_INVALID` (HTTP 409). The acting `X-User-ID` must equal the appointment's `inspector_id`, per §2.1. The inspector supplies `outcome`, one of `PASS`, `FAIL`, or `PARTIAL`, and may attach `result_notes`; whichever branch is taken, `recorded_by_inspector_id` is set to the acting `X-User-ID`, `result_notes` is written, and the appointment's `version` rises by 1.

The three branches differ in what they do with the inspector's day. `PASS` moves the appointment to `PASSED` and releases the inspector's slot on `slot_date`. `FAIL` moves it to `FAILED` and likewise releases the slot; a failed inspection is terminal for that appointment, and the builder books a re-inspection as a brand-new appointment rather than reusing this one. `PARTIAL` moves it to `PARTIAL` and — unlike the other two — **keeps holding** the slot, because the inspector is coming back. From `PARTIAL`, a later `PASS` or `FAIL` on the same appointment moves it to `PASSED` or `FAILED` respectively, overwrites both `recorded_by_inspector_id` and `result_notes` with the newer values, and releases the slot at that point.

Segregation of duties applies to one branch specifically. When the `inspection_type` being recorded is `FINAL`, the service first checks whether any other appointment on the same `adu_project_id` already carries a `recorded_by_inspector_id` equal to the acting `X-User-ID`; if one does, the request is rejected with `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES` (HTTP 403). The inspector who signed the framing cannot also sign the final. No such restriction applies to the non-`FINAL` inspection types.

A `ProjectManager` may cancel a booked inspection that is still in `SCHEDULED` or `PARTIAL`, moving it to `CANCELLED` and returning the slot to the inspector's day so that the day's booked count falls back to exactly what it was before the booking. Cancellation is also what the corrections cascade of §2.9 performs automatically when a plan-check correction letter blocks a phase.

### 3.9. The Certificate of Occupancy and Rental Readiness

Issuing the certificate of occupancy is a `PlanChecker` action, and it is the last thing that happens to a project. It requires the project to be in `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION`, or the answer is `ERR_PROJECT_PHASE_INVALID`, and then four gates are checked in order. A project that already has a certificate is rejected with `ERR_CERTIFICATE_ALREADY_ISSUED` (HTTP 409). Every utility record of the project must be `ENERGIZED`, or the answer is `ERR_UTILITY_NOT_ENERGIZED` (HTTP 409). Every construction phase of the project must be `COMPLETE`, or the answer is `ERR_PHASES_INCOMPLETE` (HTTP 409). And the project must carry at least one inspection appointment whose `inspection_type` is `FINAL` and whose status is `PASSED`, or the answer is `ERR_FINAL_INSPECTION_MISSING` (HTTP 409).

The certificate is an immutable record, carrying only an `id` and a `created_at` alongside its business fields. It names the `adu_project_id` it certifies. Its `certificate_number` is the governing jurisdiction's `code`, a hyphen, and an issue sequence within that jurisdiction: the count of certificates already issued to projects sitting in that same jurisdiction, plus one — so the first certificate issued in a jurisdiction whose `code` is `SNVL` reads `SNVL-1`, the second `SNVL-2`. Its `issued_on_date` is the logical clock date. Its `final_inspection_passed_at` is the `slot_end` of the qualifying final inspection: among the project's appointments with `inspection_type` `FINAL` and status `PASSED`, the one with the latest `slot_end`, with ties broken by taking the oldest `created_at`. Issuing the certificate also moves the project `UNDER_CONSTRUCTION → FINALED` and raises the project's `version` by 1. Certificate listings for a project return an empty array before issuance and a single-element array afterwards.

The same act runs the rental-readiness and short-term-rental restriction check, and stamps its four outputs onto the certificate. Three conditions are tested and any that hold are collected into `blocking_reasons`. `JADU_OWNER_OCCUPANCY_REQUIRED` applies when the project's `unit_type` is `JADU` and the parcel's `occupied` flag is false, because a junior ADU is only lawful where the owner actually lives on the parcel. `OPEN_CHANGE_ORDERS` applies when the project has at least one change order still sitting in `DRAFT`, `SUBMITTED`, or `REVISION_REQUESTED`; rows in `APPROVED`, `REJECTED`, or `VOIDED` never block readiness. `STR_OVERLAY_RESTRICTED` applies when the parcel's `in_str_overlay` flag is true **or** the jurisdiction's `str_prohibited` flag is true. The resulting list is sorted ascending in ASCII order, so `JADU_OWNER_OCCUPANCY_REQUIRED` precedes `OPEN_CHANGE_ORDERS`, which precedes `STR_OVERLAY_RESTRICTED`.

The `rental_classification` then follows a strict precedence over those blockers. If `JADU_OWNER_OCCUPANCY_REQUIRED` is among them, the classification is `RENTAL_PROHIBITED` and `min_lease_days` is whichever is larger of the statewide 30-day floor and the jurisdiction's `str_min_lease_days`. Otherwise, if `STR_OVERLAY_RESTRICTED` is among them, the classification is `LONG_TERM_ONLY` and `min_lease_days` is again the larger of 30 days and `str_min_lease_days`. Otherwise, if the jurisdiction's `str_min_lease_days` is 7 days or fewer — a week or less being the practical definition of a short-term rental — the classification is `SHORT_TERM_PERMITTED` and `min_lease_days` is simply the jurisdiction's `str_min_lease_days`, without the statewide floor applied. In every remaining case the classification is `LONG_TERM_ONLY` and `min_lease_days` is the larger of 30 days and `str_min_lease_days`. Note that an `OPEN_CHANGE_ORDERS` blocker on its own never changes the classification — it only changes readiness.

Finally, `rental_ready` is true only when the classification is not `RENTAL_PROHIBITED` **and** `blocking_reasons` is completely empty. A unit with open change orders is therefore classified for rent but not yet ready to advertise, which is precisely the distinction the builder's marketing team needs.
