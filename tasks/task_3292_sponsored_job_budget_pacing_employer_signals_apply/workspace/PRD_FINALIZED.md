# Sponsored Job Pacing API — Product Requirements Document

## 1. Introduction

The Sponsored Job Pacing API is the marketplace layer that decides which job postings an employer's money buys, how much traffic those postings receive, and how much the employer is charged for it. Employers hand the platform both their raw inventory — job descriptions pulled from their own careers pages and from third-party applicant tracking systems — and their budget, in the form of campaigns that sponsor a chosen subset of that inventory. The platform's job is to keep the two in balance: never promote a posting the employer has not paid to promote, never charge for a placement that a policy violation, a duplicate, or an expired snapshot has invalidated, and never let a finite campaign budget be spent faster than the employer authorized for a single day.

The product exists because the naive alternative — charging for every click and application as it happens — behaves badly. Money runs out in the morning and the employer's postings go dark exactly when candidates are most active. Employers with a history of ignoring applicants keep winning placement over employers who reply quickly, because nothing in a raw auction measures follow-through. Fraudulent postings harvest applications until a human notices. The system described here addresses all three failure modes structurally: budget is reserved ahead of spend and released precisely on day rollover, distribution is tilted by an employer quality signal derived from real disposition behavior, and every posting snapshot is aged and scanned before it is allowed to serve.

This document is the complete specification read by the engineering team implementing the service. It covers account and posting ingestion, the sponsored auction and its pacing math, the budget ledger that every charge flows through, the apply-tracking flows that convert an impression into a billed outcome, the employer signal that reshapes distribution, alert delivery to job seekers, and the billing and refund operations that close the loop. Where a rule depends on the passage of time, the document refers to a logical clock that the platform controls rather than wall-clock time, so that every behaviour in the system is reproducible.

## 2. Global Specifications & System-Wide Principles

This section defines the contract-level rules that hold across every endpoint: who may call what, the exact shape of every response body, how lists are paged and ordered, how resources are identified and versioned, how money is represented, how the logical clock drives time-dependent behaviour, and the order in which request guards are evaluated.

### 2.1. Acting Roles and Ownership Boundaries

Every request to the API must carry two mandatory headers. `X-User-ID` identifies the acting party and `X-User-Role` declares the capacity in which they act. A request that omits either header, sends a malformed value, or sends a role that is not one of the four declared roles is rejected with `ERR_MISSING_OR_INVALID_USER_CONTEXT_HEADER` (HTTP 400).

The system recognizes exactly four roles, spelled as follows: `PlatformAdmin`, `TrustReviewer`, `Employer`, and `JobSeeker`. Operations are gated by role, and an attempt to invoke an operation from a role that is not authorized for it is rejected with `ERR_INSUFFICIENT_PERMISSIONS` (HTTP 403). Some operations are exclusive to a single role and some are shared between roles; §4 records, for every endpoint, the complete list of roles authorized to call it.

The role model is a peer model, not a hierarchy, and two of the four roles are bound to persisted entities while two are not. When `X-User-Role` is `Employer`, `X-User-ID` is the `id` of the acting `Employer`; ownership checks on any resource that carries an employer association compare that resource's employer foreign-key field to `X-User-ID`. Concretely, an `Employer` caller may only operate on campaigns, sponsored jobs, job postings, assessments and applications that belong to it, and an ownership mismatch is an `ERR_INSUFFICIENT_PERMISSIONS` failure rather than a not-found failure. When `X-User-Role` is `JobSeeker`, `X-User-ID` is the `id` of the acting `JobSeeker`; ownership checks on resources that carry a seeker association — applications and alert subscriptions — compare that association to `X-User-ID`. When `X-User-Role` is `PlatformAdmin`, `X-User-ID` is an opaque operator identifier not tied to any persisted entity; the server may record it as the actor on records it creates, but it does not compare it to any entity's `id`. The same is true of `TrustReviewer`: `X-User-ID` is opaque, so a `TrustReviewer` is never rejected on ownership grounds — only on the role gate for the operation itself. Because both administrative roles are opaque, an `Employer` or `JobSeeker` identifier supplied in `X-User-ID` under those roles is not validated against any account.

A few operations are readable by more than one role without ownership narrowing — for example both administrative roles and the owning `Employer` may read an employer account, and any authenticated party may read a job posting. Where a read is unrestricted by ownership, §4 lists every role that may call it.

### 2.2. API Response Structure

#### Standard Success Response Format

All successful API responses (those with 2xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format in their JSON body:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z",
    "limit": 10,
    "offset": 0,
    "total_count": 100,
    "has_more": true
  },
  "response_type": "object" | "array",
  "data": { }
}
```

- The `api_request_id` is a server-generated unique identifier for the request that starts with `req_`.
- The `api_request_timestamp` is a server-generated ISO 8601 UTC timestamp with three fractional digits.
- The `response_type` is either "object" for a single resource or "array" for a list of resources.
- The `data` field contains the resource object or an array of resource objects. For 2xx responses with no data, it contains an empty object. Unless otherwise noted, operations that create or modify a resource will return the created or updated resource object in the data field.
- Pagination-related fields in `meta` (`limit`, `offset`, `total_count`, `has_more`) are included for list endpoints.

#### Standard Error Response Format

All error API responses (4xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z"
  },
  "response_type": "error",
  "data": {
    "error_id": "UNIQUE_ERROR_IDENTIFIER_STRING",
    "message": "A human-readable explanation of the error."
  }
}
```

- The `meta` object for error responses contains only `api_request_id` and `api_request_timestamp`.
- The `error_id` is a unique, machine-readable string that identifies the specific error.
- The `message` provides a human-readable explanation of what went wrong.

### 2.3. Pagination

List endpoints accept two query parameters. `limit` bounds the number of items returned in a single page; when it is omitted it defaults to 10, and any supplied value below 1 is invalid and any value above 100 is invalid. `offset` is the number of leading items to skip and defaults to 0 when omitted. A page response echoes `limit`, `offset`, `total_count` — the total number of items matching the filter, ignoring pagination — and `has_more`, a boolean that is true exactly when further items remain after the returned page.

Unless a rule elsewhere in this document specifies otherwise, every list endpoint returns its items sorted by creation timestamp ascending. Filters narrow the result set before pagination is applied, so `total_count` reflects the filtered set rather than the unfiltered one. When a caller supplies a filter parameter that the endpoint does not define, or a value that is not a member of the relevant enumeration, the request fails general request-shape validation with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST` (HTTP 400).

### 2.4. Resource Identity, Mutable Timestamps and Version Bookkeeping

Every persisted resource carries a server-minted `id` formatted as a UUID string with no type prefix and no embedded meaning; two resources of different types may share no identifier space convention beyond the UUID format itself. Beyond that, resources fall into two families.

The first family — accounts, postings, campaigns, sponsored jobs, applications and their supporting records — carries the full mutable bookkeeping set. Every timestamp stamped from the logical clock is rendered with no fractional part at all when the instant falls exactly on a whole second (for example `2026-09-01T00:00:00Z` or `2026-09-01T18:00:00Z`) and with exactly three fractional digits otherwise (for example `2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z`); this governs `created_at` and `updated_at` on the mutable family and `created_at` on the append-only family, together with every other instant the server stamps from the logical clock, such as `posted_at`, `ingested_at`, `day_open_date`, `occurred_at`, `started_at`, `completed_at`, `responded_at`, `suspended_at` and `last_notified_at`. Each such resource has `created_at`, an ISO 8601 UTC timestamp recording when the resource was first created; `updated_at`, a timestamp in the same format recording when the resource was last modified; and `version`, an integer that starts at its creation value and is incremented by exactly one on every mutation of that resource. `updated_at` is refreshed on precisely the same occasions as `version` is incremented: whenever any field of the resource changes, whether the change was requested directly by a client or produced as a cascaded side effect of an operation on a different resource. This family comprises `Employer`, `JobSeeker`, `Campaign`, `SponsoredJob`, `JobPosting`, `JobPostingVersion`, `Assessment`, `Application`, `JobAlertSubscription`, `SponsorEvent`, and `PacingHold`.

The second family consists of two append-only record types: `ApplyEvent` and `BudgetLedger`. Neither is ever modified after creation, so neither carries an `updated_at` field and neither carries a `version` field. Both carry only `id` and `created_at`. A client that expects a `version` on an `ApplyEvent` or a `BudgetLedger` entry is reading the specification incorrectly.

### 2.5. The Immutable Budget Ledger and the Conserved Quantities

Money in this system is never tracked by mutating a single balance field. Every movement of campaign budget appends an entry to that campaign's `BudgetLedger`, and the ledger is the single source of truth from which the campaign's displayed budget fields are reconciled. A `BudgetLedger` entry records the `campaign_id` it belongs to, an `entry_type` drawn from `HOLD`, `COMMIT`, `RELEASE` and `REFUND`, an `amount_cents` that is signed — debits are positive and credits are negative — a `sponsor_event_id` identifying the sponsored event that produced the entry when there is one (it is null for adjustment entries that are not attributable to a single event), and a human-readable `reference_code`. The `reference_code` scheme is prefix-and-sequence: commitments carry a `CMT-` prefix, refunds a `RFD-` prefix, releases a `REV-` prefix and holds a `HLD-` prefix, each followed by a monotonically increasing sequence number.

The `total_budget_invariant` defines two conserved quantities, recomputed from the ledger after every one of those four entry types is appended, and they must always agree with the campaign's derived fields. The first is lifetime committed spend: `spent_cents` equals the sum of the `amount_cents` over all of the campaign's entries whose `entry_type` is `COMMIT`, plus the sum over all entries whose `entry_type` is `REFUND`. Because refund entries are negative, a refund reduces committed spend by exactly the refunded amount; entries of type `HOLD` and `RELEASE` do not appear in this sum at all. The second is reserved budget: `held_cents` equals the sum of `amount_cents` over every `PacingHold` whose `campaign_id` is this campaign and whose `status` is `ACTIVE`. A hold that has been committed or released leaves this sum immediately. Neither quantity is allowed to drift from the ledger; when the two disagree, the ledger is authoritative.

These invariants define how the four ledger entry types differ. A `HOLD` reserves budget without spending it, so it raises `held_cents` and leaves `spent_cents` untouched. A `COMMIT` converts reservation into spend, so it raises `spent_cents` and, when it consumes a reservation, lowers `held_cents` by the consumed amount. A `RELEASE` cancels a reservation that will not be spent and lowers `held_cents` while leaving `spent_cents` untouched. A `REFUND` returns previously committed money, so it lowers `spent_cents` through its negative amount.

The employer's own outstanding obligation is a separate quantity: `Employer.balance_cents` rises by the cleared cost of every billed sponsored event and falls by the cleared cost of every granted refund, and is settled separately by invoice. It is not derived from the ledger and is not subject to the two invariants above.

### 2.6. Money and Multiplier Precision

Monetary amounts are integers throughout. Every cents-valued field — budgets, bids, clearing prices, ledger amounts, balances and spend counters — is an integer number of cents, is computed with integer arithmetic, and is never returned as a fractional or string-encoded number. The one monetary operation that is not exact-by-construction is the auction clearing price, which is rounded up to the next whole cent, i.e. ceiling-rounded to integer cents.

Derived ratios are bounded and rounded. Both pacing multipliers, `scheduled_pacing_multiplier` and `realized_pacing_multiplier`, are computed as real numbers and are rounded half-up to exactly 3 decimal places, and both are clamped into the closed interval from 0.0 to 2.0 before rounding. The employer `quality_score` is an integer computed by rounding half-up and clamped into the closed interval from 0 to 100. The search `relevance_score` is an integer produced by summing fixed integer weights, so no rounding is involved at all. Posting freshness is expressed as a whole number of elapsed days by truncating the fractional part of the age in days.

### 2.7. The Logical Clock and Day Rollover

Time in this system is a controlled, monotonic value rather than the host's wall clock. The server's logical clock starts at exactly `2026-09-01T00:00:00Z` for every fresh process, and the server must never fall back to real wall-clock time under any circumstance. All age computations, freshness windows, refund windows and day boundaries are evaluated against the logical clock. The clock may only be moved forward by the administrative clock-setting operation documented in §4, and a request to move it to an instant that precedes the current logical clock value is rejected with `ERR_INVALID_CLOCK_ORDER` (HTTP 400). A request to set the clock to the current value or to a later instant succeeds and returns the new `current_utc` together with the derived `day_open_date`, a count of `campaigns_reset` and a count of `postings_rederived`. Both returned instants are stamped from the logical clock and follow the logical-clock timestamp format of §2.4, so a whole-second instant such as a midnight day boundary carries no fractional part.

The notion of a logical day is anchored to the clock's calendar date. A campaign's `day_open_date` is the instant `00:00:00Z` of the current clock date, and a campaign's `daily_spent_cents` counts only spend committed since that instant. When the clock crosses into a later calendar date, a rollover occurs: every campaign whose recorded `day_open_date` precedes the new logical day has its `daily_spent_cents` reset to 0 and its `day_open_date` advanced to the new day, and posting freshness is re-derived for every posting because the age of each snapshot has grown. Rollover is the mechanism by which daily budget is replenished, and it may be triggered either by the clock-setting operation or by any operation whose timestamps push the clock across midnight.

### 2.8. Graded Request Evaluation and Precedence

Request guards are not evaluated in an arbitrary order, and the order is observable because each failing guard produces a different error. When a request arrives, the following checks run in this fixed sequence, and the first one that fails terminates the request with its own error without later checks being attempted:

1. **Acting context.** Both identity headers must be present and well-formed, and the acting role must be one of the four declared roles. Otherwise `ERR_MISSING_OR_INVALID_USER_CONTEXT_HEADER` (400).
2. **Authorization and ownership.** The acting role must be permitted for the operation, and for the two entity-backed roles the caller must own the target resource. Otherwise `ERR_INSUFFICIENT_PERMISSIONS` (403).
3. **Referential existence.** Every resource identifier appearing in the request — in the path or in the payload — must resolve to an existing row. The identifiers are validated from the left of the path to the right, and the first unresolvable identifier determines the error: `ERR_EMPLOYER_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_JOB_SEEKER_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_CAMPAIGN_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_SPONSORED_JOB_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_JOB_POSTING_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_APPLICATION_NOT_FOUND`, `ERR_ASSESSMENT_NOT_FOUND` or `ERR_SPONSOR_EVENT_NOT_FOUND`, all HTTP 404.
4. **Lifecycle state.** The target resource must be in a state that permits the requested action. Failures here surface as `ERR_CAMPAIGN_NOT_ACTIVE`, `ERR_CAMPAIGN_TERMINAL`, `ERR_SPONSORED_JOB_NOT_ELIGIBLE`, `ERR_POSTING_NOT_ACTIVE`, `ERR_INVALID_APPLICATION_STATE`, `ERR_APPLICATION_ALREADY_TERMINAL`, `ERR_EMPLOYER_NOT_SUSPENDED` or `ERR_INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION`, all HTTP 409.
5. **Fraud and trust safety.** The acting employer must not be `SUSPENDED`, and submitted posting content must not contain a misleading-posting keyword. Failures surface as `ERR_EMPLOYER_SUSPENDED` or `ERR_FRAUD_KEYWORDS_DETECTED` (409).
6. **Ingestion, apply-mode, assessment and bid coherence.** Deduplication on ingestion, the coherence of the apply mode with its redirect destination, the shape of a screening assessment and its threshold, and the minimum bid are checked here. Failures surface as `ERR_DUPLICATE_INGESTION`, `ERR_INVALID_REDIRECT_URL`, `ERR_UNSUPPORTED_ASSESSMENT_QUESTIONS`, `ERR_ASSESSMENT_SCORE_BELOW_THRESHOLD` or `ERR_BID_BELOW_MINIMUM` (400 or 409).
7. **Logical clock ordering.** The requested instant must not precede the current logical clock. Otherwise `ERR_INVALID_CLOCK_ORDER` (400).
8. **Budget and billing capacity.** Daily and lifetime budget capacity, pacing-hold capacity, and the requirement that the billing account is not frozen are checked here. Failures surface as `ERR_DAILY_BUDGET_EXCEEDED`, `ERR_TOTAL_BUDGET_EXCEEDED` or `ERR_INSUFFICIENT_DAILY_BUDGET` (409).
9. **Refundability and hold referential rules.** The event under review must be a refundable billed event, and a pacing hold must not overlap an existing hold. Failures surface as `ERR_EVENT_NOT_REFUNDABLE`, `ERR_REFUND_WINDOW_EXPIRED`, `ERR_DUPLICATE_REFUND_CLAIM` or `ERR_PACING_HOLD_OVERLAP` (409).
10. **Optimistic concurrency.** Where a write requires a version precondition, the expected-version field must be present and must match, producing `ERR_MISSING_EXPECTED_VERSION` (400) or `ERR_STALE_VERSION` (412).
11. **General request shape.** Required fields must be present, types must be correct and enumerations must carry valid members. Otherwise `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST` (400).

Two consequences of this ordering deserve emphasis because they are easy to get wrong. First, a request whose path is malformed and whose payload is also missing required fields fails on the missing-required-field rule only after every preceding tier has passed, so an application whose posting does not exist will report a posting-not-found failure rather than a request-shape failure. Second, where two different tiers could plausibly own a mismatch, the earlier tier wins: a submission of an assessment score below the assessment's threshold against an application that is already terminal reports the application-state failure at tier 4, not the score failure at tier 6.

### 2.9. Optimistic Concurrency on Campaign Writes

Two write operations — the recording of a sponsored event and the granting of a refund on a sponsored event — require the caller to prove it has an up-to-date view of the affected campaign. Both must carry a non-optional request field named `expected_campaign_version` holding the value of `version` that the caller last observed on the campaign. If the field is absent, the request fails with `ERR_MISSING_EXPECTED_VERSION` (HTTP 400). If the field is present but does not equal the campaign's current `version` — because any other write has advanced the campaign since the caller last read it — the request is rejected with `ERR_STALE_VERSION` (HTTP 412), and no event is recorded, no refund is granted, and no ledger entry is appended.

This rule exists because both operations compute corrective or incremental ledger effects from the campaign's current budget posture, and a caller working from a superseded posture would produce a wrong entry. Every other endpoint in the system neither requires nor accepts `expected_campaign_version`; a write to a campaign that does not carry the field is handled without any version comparison, and a stray value supplied to such an endpoint is not used.

### 2.10. Nested Resource Resolution and Child Sub-resources

The API's resource graph is not uniformly nested in its URLs. Some resources are addressed only through their parent — a job posting's versions, a job posting's assessments, a seeker's alert subscriptions, an application's apply events, a sponsored job's and a campaign's collections — while others are addressed directly by their own identifier even though they belong to a parent, because they are created and cited across several parents. When a path does contain more than one identifier, existence is validated strictly from left to right, and the first identifier that does not resolve produces its own not-found error before any later identifier is examined; the ordering rule in §2.8 tier 3 governs in every such case.

Reading a resource is never gated on the state of its parent, but mutating one frequently is. Where a mutation is conditional on a parent's state, the condition is stated with the workflow that owns it in §3 rather than repeated here, and the failure is a tier-4 lifecycle failure.
## 3. Business Logic & Workflows

### 3.1. Employer Onboarding, Feed Ingestion and Posting Integrity

An employer account is seeded by a `PlatformAdmin` with a `legal_name`, a `domain` that keys the employer's applicant-tracking-system feed, and an optional `ats_provider`. When `ats_provider` is omitted it takes the value `NONE`; when supplied it must be one of `WORKDAY`, `GREENHOUSE`, `LEVER`, `TALEO` or `NONE`. A newly created employer begins with `status` set to `ACTIVE`, `quality_score` at 100, `response_rate_bps` at 0, `balance_cents` at 0 and `billing_status` set to `CURRENT`; its `last_invoice_at`, `suspended_at` and `suspension_reason` are all null, and all three become populated only as the account is used or disciplined. Job seekers are seeded the same way, by an administrative call carrying an `email` that must be unique across all seekers, a `full_name`, and an optional `location`; a seeker's `status` is `ACTIVE` for the entire lifetime of the account, and the only lifecycle event a seeker account can undergo in this system is none at all.

Employers publish work by creating a job posting and, at the same time, its first feed snapshot. A posting is created with the owning `employer_id`, a `title`, a free-text `location`, a `description_text` body, an `apply_mode` of either `HOSTED` or `ATS_REDIRECT`, and an optional `apply_redirect_url` and `external_posting_id`. The apply mode decides how candidates finish their application, and the two modes have a hard coherence requirement: when `apply_mode` is `ATS_REDIRECT`, `apply_redirect_url` must be present and must be an absolute `https` URL, and a posting that violates either part is rejected with `ERR_INVALID_REDIRECT_URL` (HTTP 400). When `apply_mode` is `HOSTED`, an `apply_redirect_url` is never required and is not consulted. On creation the posting runs an immediate duplicate check; a created posting stamps `posted_at` from the logical clock, initialises `apply_started_count` and `apply_completed_count` to 0, and starts in the `DRAFT` state, moving to `ACTIVE` as soon as its first snapshot is `FRESH`.

Every posting snapshot is a `JobPostingVersion` record. A snapshot carries the `external_posting_id` from the employer's or ATS's side, an `ingested_at` timestamp stamped from the logical clock, and a set of content fields — `title`, `description_text`, `apply_mode` and `apply_redirect_url` — each of which is optional at ingestion time and, when omitted, is inherited from the posting's newest existing snapshot. Two derived values are computed for each snapshot. The first is `content_hash`, a hash of the case-folded `title` concatenated with `description_text`; it exists to detect a feed replay, because a snapshot whose `content_hash` matches the hash of any version of the same posting ingested within the preceding 30 days is not a new fact about the posting and the ingestion is rejected as a duplicate with `ERR_DUPLICATE_INGESTION` (HTTP 409). The second is `freshness`, derived from `ingested_at` against the logical clock: a snapshot is `FRESH` while its age is less than 30 whole days, `STALE` once its age reaches 30 whole days and up to 59 whole days, and `EXPIRED` once its age is 60 whole days or more. Age is measured by subtracting `ingested_at` from the current logical instant, dividing by 86,400 seconds and truncating the fractional part, so exactly 30 days and 30 days and 12 hours both compute to an age of 30 days. The posting does not aggregate freshness across its history: `current_freshness` is the `freshness` of the single newest `JobPostingVersion` by `ingested_at`, and when two versions share an `ingested_at` the newer-created one wins, breaking by `created_at` descending.

The posting's own distribution state is derived, not set, and the `posting_freshness` rule that derives it applies a strict precedence. A posting that is already `SUSPENDED` stays `SUSPENDED` regardless of freshness — suspension is sticky and outranks every other derived value. Otherwise, if `current_freshness` is `EXPIRED` the posting's `state` is `EXPIRED`; if `current_freshness` is `STALE` the state is `STALE`; and if `current_freshness` is `FRESH` the state is `ACTIVE`. The state `DRAFT` exists only before a posting has any snapshot at all, so a posting leaves `DRAFT` for `ACTIVE` on its first `FRESH` snapshot and never returns to `DRAFT`. Each of these derivations runs on every snapshot ingestion and on every clock advance, and each transition has a consequence. A posting that goes `ACTIVE` → `STALE` is immediately excluded from the sponsored placement candidate pool, so no new sponsored impressions or clicks can be served against it. A posting that goes `STALE` → `EXPIRED`, or directly `ACTIVE` → `EXPIRED` when a single re-derivation skips the intermediate freshness, is terminal for distribution, and every `SponsoredJob` sponsoring that posting is moved to `PAUSED` in the same response.

Deduplication extends beyond a single posting's history. Each posting carries a `dedupe_key` built from the case-folded `title`, a normalised form of its `location`, and its `external_posting_id`. Two postings for the same employer that produce the same key are the same job advertised twice across feeds, and the second ingestion is rejected with `ERR_DUPLICATE_INGESTION`. This is a per-employer uniqueness rule; two different employers may legitimately advertise identically titled roles in the same city.

Posting content is also scanned for fraud at ingestion time. If the submitted `title` or `description_text` carries any misleading-posting keyword — the canonical examples being an instruction to send a wire transfer, a demand for an upfront fee, or a request to pay to start — the ingestion or posting creation is rejected with `ERR_FRAUD_KEYWORDS_DETECTED` (HTTP 409). The same guard blocks an employer whose `status` is `SUSPENDED` from creating postings, campaigns, sponsored jobs or events at all, in each case with `ERR_EMPLOYER_SUSPENDED` (HTTP 409); this check sits below the lifecycle checks in the ordering of §2.8 and above the ingestion checks, so a suspended employer's malformed redirect URL is reported as a suspension rather than a URL problem.

Screening assessments are authored by an employer against a posting it owns. An assessment is created with a `job_posting_id`, an ordered `questions` array and a `pass_threshold`, and its `status` is `PUBLISHED` for its entire lifetime. Each element of `questions` is an object with a `prompt` string, a `kind` drawn from `BOOLEAN`, `SINGLE_CHOICE` or `NUMERIC`, and a `required` boolean. An assessment whose `questions` array is empty, or whose `pass_threshold` is greater than the number of its required questions, is rejected with `ERR_UNSUPPORTED_ASSESSMENT_QUESTIONS` (HTTP 400). A `pass_threshold` of zero is legal and means the assessment never blocks completion.

When an employer is disciplined, the consequences are broad and fully specified. A `TrustReviewer` may suspend an employer whose `status` is `ACTIVE`, supplying a `reason`; the `reason` is recorded as the employer's `suspension_reason`, `suspended_at` is stamped from the logical clock, `status` becomes `SUSPENDED`, and `billing_status` becomes `FROZEN`. In the same response, every `JobPosting` belonging to that employer moves to `SUSPENDED`, every `Campaign` belonging to that employer moves to `PAUSED`, every `SponsoredJob` inside those campaigns moves to `PAUSED`, and every `PacingHold` that was `ACTIVE` on any of those sponsored jobs moves to `RELEASED`, returning its reserved budget. Note that the sponsored jobs are paused directly by the suspension rather than by their campaign's pause, because the suspension cascade reaches them in a single hop from the employer; the campaign pause would have paused only the sponsored jobs that were `ELIGIBLE`, whereas the suspension pauses all of them. Reinstate works in the opposite direction but is deliberately not a mirror image. Reinstatement requires the employer to be `SUSPENDED` — a request against an `ACTIVE` employer is rejected with `ERR_EMPLOYER_NOT_SUSPENDED` (HTTP 409) — and on success the employer's `status` returns to `ACTIVE` and `billing_status` to `CURRENT`. Crucially, reinstatement re-derives every one of the employer's postings from `current_freshness` — a posting whose newest snapshot is `EXPIRED` becomes `EXPIRED`, and every other posting becomes `ACTIVE` — but it does not resume the employer's sponsored jobs. A sponsored job that was paused by a suspension stays `PAUSED` until an employer explicitly resumes it, and a campaign stays `PAUSED` until it is explicitly resumed.

### 3.2. Campaign Budget, Pacing Mathematics and Day Rollover

A campaign is an employer's budget envelope. An `Employer` (or an administrator on its behalf) creates one with the owning `employer_id`, a display `name`, a `pricing_model` of either `CPC` or `CPA`, a `daily_budget_cents` limit and a `total_budget_cents` limit. A new campaign begins in `DRAFT` with `spent_cents`, `daily_spent_cents` and `held_cents` all at 0, and with `day_open_date` set to `00:00:00Z` of the current logical day. A campaign only becomes economically meaningful when it is activated. Activation is an employer action permitted only while the owning employer's `status` is `ACTIVE`, and on success the campaign moves to `ACTIVE` and its `spent_cents` is re-derived from the sum of its ledger's `COMMIT` entries — which for a campaign that has never served an event is 0.

The pacing multiplier is the mechanism that prevents an employer's daily budget from being consumed in the first hour of the day, and there are two of them — `campaign_pacing_multiplier` and `realized_pacing_multiplier` — because they answer two different questions. Both are computed for a campaign operating on the current logical day and both are sold to the selection system as a number between 0.0 and 2.0 inclusive, rounded half-up to three decimal places.

Consider first the scheduled, a-priori multiplier, which projects forward. It measures how much of the logical day has elapsed. The elapsed fraction is the number of seconds between the campaign's `day_open_date` and the current logical instant, divided by 86,400, clamped into the closed interval from 0.0 to 1.0. If that elapsed fraction is less than 0.05 — the first 72 minutes of the logical day, which is 5 percent of 86,400 seconds — the multiplier is exactly 1.0 and no further arithmetic is done, because the system will not front-load spend during the warm-up window. Otherwise the elapsed fraction is treated as the target fraction of the day's budget that ought to have been spent by now, and the actual fraction is computed as `daily_spent_cents` divided by `daily_budget_cents`. If the actual fraction is at or below the target fraction the campaign is behind pace, and the raw multiplier is 1.0 plus the difference between the target and the actual; if the actual fraction exceeds the target the campaign is ahead of pace, and the raw multiplier is 1.0 minus the difference between the actual and the target. The raw value is then clamped into the closed interval from 0.0 to 2.0 and rounded half-up to three decimal places. The behaviour this produces is deliberate: a campaign that has spent nothing halfway through the day receives a multiplier near 1.5, which makes its bids more competitive and catches it up, while a campaign that has spent its entire daily budget by midday receives a multiplier near 0.0, which removes it from contention for the rest of the day. The named constants behind this are the 0.05 warm-up fraction, the day length of 86,400 seconds, and the 0.0 and 2.0 bounds, and the campaign scope is every campaign that is `ACTIVE` or `PACING_HOLD` whose `day_open_date` equals the current logical day.

The realized multiplier answers the retrospective question: how fast is this campaign actually spending, measured against how fast it should have spent by now? It recomputes on every committed spend and on every day rollover. Its elapsed fraction is computed identically to the scheduled case, by clamping the seconds elapsed since `day_open_date` divided by 86,400 into the closed interval from 0.0 to 1.0. The expected spend so far is then `daily_budget_cents` multiplied by that elapsed fraction with the fractional part truncated toward zero to a whole number of cents. If the expected spend is zero — which happens when elapsed time rounds down to nothing, at the very start of a day — the multiplier is exactly 1.0 by convention, because there is no baseline to measure against. Otherwise the multiplier is `daily_spent_cents` divided by the expected spend, clamped into the closed interval from 0.0 to 2.0 and rounded half-up to three decimal places. The two multipliers move in opposite directions for the same data, and this is intentional rather than a defect: a campaign that is behind pace gets a scheduled multiplier above 1.0 to push it forward and a realized multiplier below 1.0 because it has in fact delivered less than its even-schedule share. The realized multiplier is evaluated for campaigns that are `ACTIVE`, `PACING_HOLD` or `COMPLETED` on the current logical day, so a campaign that has just finished still reports the pacing it delivered.

A day rollover resets the daily clock. The rollover fires whenever the logical clock crosses into a calendar date later than a campaign's recorded `day_open_date`, and it does three things for each affected campaign: `daily_spent_cents` is reset to 0, `day_open_date` is advanced to the new logical day, and any campaign sitting in `PACING_HOLD` returns to `ACTIVE` because it now has a fresh daily budget to spend. A campaign returns from `PACING_HOLD` to `ACTIVE` only on this rollover condition, which is tested by `day_open_date` strictly preceding the current logical day. The rollover also re-derives sponsored-job eligibility, described in §3.3. The same re-derivation pass refreshes posting freshness, because a day added to the clock adds a day to every snapshot's age.

Daily exhaustion is what puts a campaign into `PACING_HOLD` in the first place. When `daily_spent_cents` reaches or exceeds `daily_budget_cents`, the campaign leaves `ACTIVE` for `PACING_HOLD`, and every `SponsoredJob` in that campaign that is currently `ELIGIBLE` moves to `BUDGET_HOLD`. A campaign in that state accepts no further billed spend for the remainder of the logical day. Lifetime exhaustion is a stronger and terminal condition: when a committed spend causes `spent_cents` to reach or exceed `total_budget_cents`, the campaign moves to `COMPLETED` in the very same request that reached the total, every `SponsoredJob` in the campaign moves to `PAUSED`, and every `ACTIVE` `PacingHold` in the campaign moves to `RELEASED`. `COMPLETED` is the campaign's only terminal state — no transition leaves it — and any later attempt to hold or spend against it is rejected with `ERR_CAMPAIGN_TERMINAL` (HTTP 409). The same terminal code covers a campaign in `PAUSED` that a caller attempts to spend against, and a campaign that is simply not `ACTIVE` when an operation requires it fails with `ERR_CAMPAIGN_NOT_ACTIVE` (HTTP 409).

An employer may pause and resume its own campaign. Pausing is permitted from `ACTIVE` or from `PACING_HOLD` and moves the campaign to `PAUSED`, taking every `ELIGIBLE` `SponsoredJob` in the campaign to `PAUSED` along with it; a sponsored job already in `BUDGET_HOLD` is not touched by the pause. Resuming is permitted only from `PAUSED`, and only when the campaign's `spent_cents` is still strictly below its `total_budget_cents` and the owning employer's `status` is `ACTIVE`. On resume the campaign returns to `ACTIVE` and every `PAUSED` `SponsoredJob` in it that sponsors a posting currently in the `ACTIVE` state returns to `ELIGIBLE`; a sponsored job whose posting is no longer `ACTIVE` stays `PAUSED`, because a campaign cannot make an unpromotable posting promotable. Both pause and resume mutate the owning employer's account only in the sense that the campaign's version advances; the campaign's own `version` is incremented by one on each such write.

### 3.3. Sponsored Job Attachment, Eligibility and the Candidate Pool

An employer attaches a posting to a campaign by creating a sponsored job, supplying the `campaign_id`, the `job_posting_id`, and both bid amounts. The `cpc_bid_cents` is the cost-per-click bid and the `cpa_bid_cents` is the cost-per-application bid; which of the two is actually used is decided by the campaign's `pricing_model`, and both must nonetheless be supplied and both must be at least one cent, because a bid below one cent is rejected with `ERR_BID_BELOW_MINIMUM` (HTTP 400). A newly created sponsored job begins in `ELIGIBLE`, with `impressions`, `clicks` and `applications` all at 0, and with both `scheduled_pacing_multiplier` and `realized_pacing_multiplier` initialised to 1.0. Those multipliers are recomputed continuously thereafter: the scheduled multiplier tracks the campaign's scheduled pacing as described in §3.2, and both are recomputed whenever the employer's `quality_score` changes, because a change in employer quality changes the ranking of every sponsored job the employer owns. One bound on the recomputation is worth stating because it is response-visible: the pacing formulas of §3.2 are scoped to campaigns that are `ACTIVE` or `PACING_HOLD`, so a sponsored job on a campaign that is still `DRAFT` keeps `scheduled_pacing_multiplier` pinned at 1.0 and is not advanced by elapsed time at all.

Eligibility for the sponsored placement pool is a conjunction, and it is worth stating exactly because four different conditions can each remove a sponsored job from contention. In order to be a candidate, a sponsored job must have `status` `ELIGIBLE`, its `JobPosting` must be in the `ACTIVE` state, and its campaign must be `ACTIVE`. A sponsored job in any other status — `DRAFT`, `PAUSED` or `BUDGET_HOLD` — is not a candidate, and an operation that requires a serving sponsored job and finds it in one of those states fails with `ERR_SPONSORED_JOB_NOT_ELIGIBLE` (HTTP 409). Correspondingly, an operation that requires an `ACTIVE` posting and finds the posting `DRAFT`, `STALE`, `EXPIRED` or `SUSPENDED` fails with `ERR_POSTING_NOT_ACTIVE` (HTTP 409).

Between the two budget states there is a real distinction. A campaign that has exhausted its daily budget moves to `PACING_HOLD` as described above and takes its still-`ELIGIBLE` sponsored jobs to `BUDGET_HOLD`, which removes them from the candidate set without stopping the campaign; they are expected to come back. A sponsored job can also be put into `BUDGET_HOLD` directly, by the system, when the campaign's remaining daily capacity — computed as `daily_budget_cents` minus `daily_spent_cents` minus `held_cents` — falls below 1,000 cents. The 1,000-cent figure is the floor below which there is not enough unreserved daily budget left to serve a placement worth serving, and it is the same threshold in both directions: a sponsored job returns from `BUDGET_HOLD` to `ELIGIBLE` either when the logical day advances and the daily budget resets, or when an inbound refund raises the campaign's remaining daily capacity back to 1,000 cents or more. In both directions the guard is the same comparison against the campaign's remaining daily capacity, and neither direction requires the employer to act.

A sponsored job leaves play when the posting beneath it does. Whenever a posting becomes `EXPIRED`, or is suspended, every `SponsoredJob` sponsoring it moves to `PAUSED`, and whenever an employer is suspended every one of its sponsored jobs moves to `PAUSED` as described in §3.1. A campaign pause moves every `ELIGIBLE` sponsored job in the campaign to `PAUSED`. In every one of these pause paths, an additional cascade runs: any `PacingHold` on the paused sponsored job that is currently `ACTIVE` is moved to `RELEASED`, and its reserved amount is returned to the campaign. This is the mechanism that prevents budget from being stranded on a sponsored job that can no longer serve.

A `PAUSED` sponsored job is recoverable, but the resume is guarded by three conditions evaluated together, and it is an employer action. The sponsored job may be resumed only when its campaign's `status` is `ACTIVE`, when the sponsored job's `JobPosting` state is `ACTIVE`, and when the campaign's owning employer is not `SUSPENDED`. If any of those fails the attempt is an invalid lifecycle action for the sponsored job's current situation and is rejected with `ERR_INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION` (HTTP 409). A successful resume returns the sponsored job to `ELIGIBLE` and re-admits it to the candidate set. The version of the sponsored job is incremented by one on resume.

### 3.4. Clearing the Auction and Pricing an Event

The price of a sponsored placement is settled by `auction_clearing_price`, a generalized second-price auction with a quality tilt, and the same clearing logic is used both when a bid quote is requested ahead of time and when a billable event is actually recorded. The settlement currency depends on the campaign's `pricing_model`: a `CPC` campaign clears on `cpc_bid_cents` and bills one charge per click, while a `CPA` campaign clears on `cpa_bid_cents` and bills one charge per application, recording click events without billing them.

The candidate pool for a settlement is every sponsored job with `status` `ELIGIBLE` whose `JobPosting` is in the `ACTIVE` state, drawn from campaigns whose `status` is `ACTIVE`. Candidates are ranked by a score that fuses price with employer quality. For each candidate the rank is its effective bid multiplied by the employer's `quality_score` divided by 100. The effective bid is the candidate's relevant bid — `cpc_bid_cents` for a `CPC` campaign and `cpa_bid_cents` for a `CPA` campaign — multiplied by that candidate's `scheduled_pacing_multiplier`. The quality tilt means that two candidates bidding identically can be separated by their employers' `quality_score`, and the pacing tilt means the same campaign bids differently at different times of day.

Candidates are ordered by rank in descending order, then by `cpc_bid_cents` in descending order, then by `created_at` in ascending order. The ranking expression can produce a tie between two candidates — the first two keys can both match — so the creation timestamp ascending is the explicit and final tie-break, and it guarantees a deterministic ordering.

When fewer than two candidates exist, the settlement is first-price: the single bidder is charged its own bid, the full relevant bid amount, with no second-price discount and no quality tilt applied. When two or more candidates exist, the runner-up determines the price. The ranking score of the candidate immediately behind the bidder is taken, divided by the bidding job's employer's `quality_score`, and multiplied by 100 — which effectively recovers the runner-up's pacing-adjusted bid as the floor the winner must beat, stripped of the winner's own quality tilt. One cent is then added to that floor, and the result is rounded up to the next whole cent. Finally the rounded amount is bounded below by one cent and above by the bidder's own relevant bid amount, so the clearing price is never below one cent and never above the bidder's bid. The named constants are the one-cent minimum increment and the quality scale of 100.

A worked example makes the two models concrete and shows why the quality tilt matters. Suppose a `CPC` campaign's sponsored job has a `cpc_bid_cents` of 100, its employer has a `quality_score` of 100, and its `scheduled_pacing_multiplier` is 1.0, so its rank is 100. Suppose the runner-up has a `cpc_bid_cents` of 80, an employer `quality_score` of 50 and a multiplier of 1.5, so its rank is 80 times 1.5 times 0.5, which is 60. The floor is 60 divided by 100, multiplied by 100, which is 60; adding the one-cent increment gives 61; and 61 lies between 1 and the bidder's own 100, so the click clears at 61 cents. Now change only the runner-up's pacing multiplier to 2.0 while keeping its bid at 80: its rank becomes 80 times 2.0 times 0.5, which is 80, so the floor is 80, the price becomes 81 cents, and the winner pays twenty cents more for the same click purely because a rival's pacing behaviour improved its standing. This sensitivity is the point of the design and it applies symmetrically under the `CPA` model, where the same arithmetic is performed on `cpa_bid_cents` and the resulting price is charged once per application rather than once per click.

### 3.5. Recording Sponsored Events and the Budget Commit Split

Recording a sponsored event is an administrative operation that names the `campaign_id`, the `sponsored_job_id`, the `event_type`, a `device_fingerprint`, an `ip_hash`, and the `expected_campaign_version` required by §2.9. The `event_type` is one of `IMPRESSION`, `CLICK` or `APPLICATION`. The campaign identified must be in `ACTIVE` status — anything else fails with `ERR_CAMPAIGN_NOT_ACTIVE` — and the sponsored job must be in a serving state, failing with `ERR_SPONSORED_JOB_NOT_ELIGIBLE` otherwise. The `occurred_at` timestamp is stamped from the logical clock, and it is not merely decorative: it anchors the 24-hour refund window described in §3.8.

Whether the event costs anything is decided by two rules acting together. An event is billable when it is a `CLICK` recorded against a `CPC` campaign, or when it is an `APPLICATION` recorded against a `CPA` campaign. Every other combination is non-billable: a `CLICK` on a `CPA` campaign is recorded and counted but never charged, an `APPLICATION` on a `CPC` campaign is recorded and counted but never charged, and an `IMPRESSION` is never billable under either model. A non-billable event carries a `cost_cents` of 0 and does not touch the ledger or the campaign's spend counters at all; it exists to be counted toward the sponsored job's `impressions`, its `clicks` or its `applications`, and to leave a record of what was served.

For a billable event the cost is the clearing price from §3.4, and that cost must be paid out of the campaign in a way that respects the employer's existing reservations. This is the purpose of the `budget_commit_split`, whose evaluation order is pinned because the order determines the ledger entries that are produced. The cleared cost is placed in a remaining-amount accumulator that starts at the full cleared amount. The campaign's `PacingHold` records whose `status` is `ACTIVE` are then consumed in ascending `created_at` order — oldest reservation first, with the record `id` never used as a tie-break — and for each hold a take amount is computed as the smaller of that hold's `amount_cents` and the remaining accumulator. If the take amount equals the hold's full amount, the hold's `status` becomes `COMMITTED` and it is consumed whole; if the take amount is smaller, the hold's `amount_cents` is reduced by the take amount and the hold stays `ACTIVE` with its remainder. Either way the take amount is subtracted from the remaining accumulator, and as soon as the accumulator reaches zero the loop stops consuming further holds. Holds that are already `COMMITTED` or `RELEASED` are skipped entirely and are never considered for consumption.

Once the reservations have been consumed, the ledger is written. One `COMMIT` entry is appended for each hold that was fully or partially consumed, and if the remaining accumulator is still greater than zero after every active hold has been exhausted — including the case where the campaign had no active holds at all — a final `COMMIT` entry is appended for that remainder, representing unreserved budget spent directly. The take from a hold is recorded against the campaign as a commitment whether the hold was consumed whole or in part, because the money is spent either way. The campaign's `spent_cents` rises by exactly the full cleared amount, no more and no less, regardless of how it was distributed across holds; and `held_cents` falls by the sum of the amounts taken out of holds, whether those holds were fully consumed or merely reduced. In the degenerate case where no active hold exists, the entire cleared amount becomes a single direct commitment with nothing released, and `held_cents` is unchanged. The ledger entries produced by this pass carry `CMT-` reference codes and, where they arise from a hold, the associated event's identifier in `sponsor_event_id`.

Committing spend has three further effects in the same response, all of them immediate. The campaign's `daily_spent_cents` rises by the cleared amount alongside `spent_cents`. The owning employer's `balance_cents` rises by the cleared amount, because the employer now owes that money. And the campaign is re-tested against its lifetime total: if `spent_cents` has now reached or exceeded `total_budget_cents`, the campaign is moved to `COMPLETED` in this same request, every sponsored job in it is moved to `PAUSED`, and every `ACTIVE` hold in it is moved to `RELEASED`. The campaign's `version` is incremented by one by the write, which is what makes the `expected_campaign_version` precondition of §2.9 meaningful for the next writer.

Two capacity guards can reject a billable event before any of this happens, and they are distinct. An event whose cleared cost would push `daily_spent_cents` above `daily_budget_cents` is rejected with `ERR_DAILY_BUDGET_EXCEEDED` (HTTP 409). An event whose cleared cost would push `spent_cents` above `total_budget_cents` is rejected with `ERR_TOTAL_BUDGET_EXCEEDED` (HTTP 409). These are checks against the campaign's capacity, not against its reservations, and they are evaluated after the fraud and time checks of §2.8 rather than before them.

The `SponsorEvent` record itself captures the outcome. Its `status` begins at `RECORDED` for every event; a billable event that clears is moved to `BILLED` in the same write, and it is only from `BILLED` that a refund is ever possible. Its `billable` flag records the billing decision described above, its `cost_cents` records the cleared amount for a billed event and 0 otherwise, and its `sponsored_job_id` names the sponsored job that served the placement. That field is a resolvable reference rather than a declared parent link: the sponsored job can be located through the serving campaign's sponsored jobs, and the same is true of `PacingHold.campaign_id`, which is resolvable through the sponsored job that owns the hold.

Reservations are created by an employer through the pacing-hold operation, which names the target sponsored job and an `amount_cents` that must be a positive integer number of cents and at least the campaign's minimum hold unit. Creating a hold appends a `HOLD` entry to the campaign's ledger and raises the campaign's `held_cents` by the reserved amount; it does not touch `spent_cents`. Three rules govern the operation. First, a sponsored job may hold only one reservation per logical day: if the sponsored job already has an `ACTIVE` hold for the current logical day, the new request is rejected with `ERR_PACING_HOLD_OVERLAP` (HTTP 409). Second, the reservation must fit inside the day's remaining allowance: if `held_cents` plus `daily_spent_cents` plus the requested amount would exceed `daily_budget_cents`, the request is rejected with `ERR_INSUFFICIENT_DAILY_BUDGET` (HTTP 409). Third, the hold must belong to a campaign that can still spend, so a hold against a `COMPLETED` campaign is rejected with `ERR_CAMPAIGN_TERMINAL`. A hold that is later consumed by a commit gains the consuming event's identifier in its `sponsor_event_id`, which is null until the hold commits.

### 3.6. Apply Flows, Screening and Apply-Routing Events

An apply flow begins when a `JobSeeker` creates an application against a posting, supplying the `job_posting_id`, their own `job_seeker_id`, and optionally an `assessment_id` together with `assessment_answers`. The application begins in `STARTED` with `started_at` stamped from the logical clock, its `completed_at` and `responded_at` both null, and its `disposition` set to `NONE`. Creating it increments the posting's `apply_started_count` by one. When an `assessment_id` is supplied and the referenced assessment is attached to that posting, the application's `assessment_score` is computed at creation from the supplied `assessment_answers` as the number of correctly answered required questions; when no `assessment_id` is supplied the field is null and the application is never gated on screening. An `assessment_id` that does not resolve is a referential failure and produces `ERR_ASSESSMENT_NOT_FOUND`.

Completion is the transition that turns an in-flight apply flow into a submitted application, and it may be driven either by the seeker submitting a hosted application or by the employer's tracking system calling back a completion pixel. The application must be in `STARTED`; any other state fails with `ERR_INVALID_APPLICATION_STATE` (HTTP 409). When the posting carries a `PUBLISHED` assessment whose `pass_threshold` is greater than zero, completion additionally requires that the application's `assessment_score` — whether it was computed at creation or recomputed from an `assessment_answers` payload supplied with the completion request — is at least the `pass_threshold`; a score below the threshold is rejected with `ERR_ASSESSMENT_SCORE_BELOW_THRESHOLD` (HTTP 409). Note that this score check is a tier-6 guard in the ordering of §2.8, so an application that is not in `STARTED` fails on the state check before its score is ever examined, and an assessment with a `pass_threshold` of zero never blocks a completion at all.

A successful completion sets the application's `status` to `COMPLETED` and stamps `completed_at` from the logical clock. It increments the posting's `apply_completed_count` by one. It appends an `ApplyEvent` whose `kind` is `COMPLETED`, whose `occurred_at` is the logical clock at emission, and whose `source` is `HOSTED` when the posting's `apply_mode` is `HOSTED` and `REDIRECT` when it is `ATS_REDIRECT`; an `ApplyEvent` belongs to its application through `application_id`. And it may record a billable sponsored outcome: when the posting is sponsored by a sponsored job that is `ELIGIBLE` under a `CPC` or `CPA` campaign, a `SponsorEvent` of type `APPLICATION` is recorded against that campaign, and on a `CPA` campaign that event is billable and its cost flows through the commit split of §3.5 exactly as any other billable event would. On a `CPC` campaign the `APPLICATION` event is recorded and counted but is not billable, so it produces no charge.

An application can also be closed. Closing from `STARTED` is the abandoned-flow path and is available to the applicant; it moves the application to `CLOSED` and — a point worth stating precisely because the incoming side of the counter behaves differently — it does not decrement `apply_started_count`, because the abandoned start remains counted against the posting even though the flow never completed. Closing from `COMPLETED` is the employer's path for an application it has not answered; the application moves to `CLOSED` with its `disposition` left at `NONE` and its `responded_at` left null, and that non-response is deliberately counted against the employer's response signal as described in §3.7. Closing from `RESPONDED` is the employer's path for an application it has already answered; it moves the application to `CLOSED` and the application is then terminal. `CLOSED` is the application's only terminal state, and any further transition attempted against a closed application is rejected with `ERR_APPLICATION_ALREADY_TERMINAL` (HTTP 409). The `ApplyEvent` kinds include `INVALID_CLICK` alongside `STARTED` and `COMPLETED`, and the event `source` is one of `HOSTED`, `REDIRECT` or `PIXEL`.

Disposition is the employer's feedback channel and it is deliberately decoupled from the application's status, because disposition evolves on its own dimension. An employer records a `disposition` on an application that is `COMPLETED`, choosing one of `REVIEWED`, `INTERVIEW`, `REJECTED` or `HIRED`; a disposition of `NONE` is not accepted as a write of new feedback. The disposition value is stored on the application, and when it is one of the four responding values the application's `status` moves to `RESPONDED`. The `responded_at` timestamp is stamped from the logical clock the first time a responding disposition is recorded and is never moved afterwards, so a subsequent change of disposition from `REVIEWED` to `HIRED` leaves the original first-response timestamp in place. Recording a responding disposition is what triggers the employer signal recomputation of §3.7, and it also recomputes the pacing multipliers and the candidate-set standing of every sponsored job the employer owns. One cross-entity caveat applies here: the application transition is permitted regardless of the employer's own `status`, so a disposition may be recorded even while the employer is `SUSPENDED`; but the quality recomputation fires only while the employer's `status` is `ACTIVE`, because a suspended account carries no distribution value to recompute.

### 3.7. Employer Quality Signals and How They Reshape Distribution

The employer `quality_score` exists to make follow-through a first-class ranking input, and the `employer_quality_signal` that produces it recomputes every time an application of that employer receives a responding disposition or is closed without one. Two quantities are produced from the same population of applications, and the population is defined carefully. A dispositioned application is one whose `disposition` is anything other than `NONE`, together with one whose `status` is `CLOSED` while its `disposition` is `NONE` — the second clause is what makes an unanswered but closed application count as a signal rather than vanishing. The responding subset of that population is the applications whose `disposition` is `REVIEWED`, `INTERVIEW`, `REJECTED` or `HIRED`. The employer's `response_rate_bps` is then the number of responding applications multiplied by 10,000, divided by the number of dispositioned applications, with the fractional part truncated toward zero to a whole number of basis points; when there are no dispositioned applications at all the rate is 0, and an employer who never answers anything therefore sits at a response rate of 0 rather than being undefined.

The score is built in two parts. The first is a tier selected from the response rate, and its boundaries are inclusive at the bottom: a rate of at least 6,000 basis points earns the high tier of 100, a rate of at least 3,000 basis points but below 6,000 earns the middle tier of 70, and anything lower earns the base tier of 40. The second part is a disposition-mix delta computed over the same population and using counts of the `HIRED`, `INTERVIEW` and `REJECTED` dispositions specifically. The delta adds 30 times the number of hired applications divided by the dispositioned count, plus 10 times the number of interviewed applications divided by the dispositioned count, minus 10 times the number of rejected applications divided by the dispositioned count, and the whole expression is rounded half-up to an integer. The 30, 10 and 10 in that expression are basis-point-scaled weights: because the count is divided by the same denominator as the response rate, a workforce that hires one third of its applicants contributes one third of 30 points. The two parts are summed and the total is clamped into the closed interval from 0 to 100, which is the score.

Two consequences follow and both are observable. First, an employer whose history is entirely non-responding dispositions scores exactly its tier with a delta of zero, so an employer with three responses out of four but no hires scores less than an employer with the same three-of-four response history and one hire. Second, at any fixed response rate, the score diverges by the mix of the dispositions: a hire is worth three times an interview and a rejection subtracts a full unit, so two employers with identical response rates can be separated by what they did with the people they answered. The score starts at 100 for a brand-new employer, which is the highest possible tier, and the field can only be moved by disposition activity. Because the score is the quality tilt in the auction rank of §3.4 and a multiplier in the sponsored-jobs' priority, a drop in employer quality immediately and automatically makes that employer's sponsored placements less competitive, without any explicit enforcement step.

### 3.8. Refunds, Duplicate Invalid Clicks and Billing Posture

Refunds exist to compensate employers for charges that should never have been assessed — most often a click that was invalid, duplicated, or produced by an automated client — and the operation is deliberately narrow. A refund is requested against a specific sponsored event by a `TrustReviewer` or a `PlatformAdmin`, carrying a `reason` and the `expected_campaign_version` required by §2.9. Four guards must all pass before a refund is granted.

The first is the event's own status: only an event whose `status` is `BILLED` is refundable, and an event that is still `RECORDED` or has already been refunded fails with `ERR_EVENT_NOT_REFUNDABLE` (HTTP 409). The second is the refund window: the event's `occurred_at` must be no more than 24 hours before the current logical clock, and an event whose `occurred_at` is more than 24 hours in the past fails with `ERR_REFUND_WINDOW_EXPIRED` (HTTP 409). The third is duplicate-claim protection, and it is keyed on the device rather than the event: if a refund has already been granted for another event carrying the same `device_fingerprint` within the invalid-click window, the new claim fails with `ERR_DUPLICATE_REFUND_CLAIM` (HTTP 409), which prevents an automated client from harvesting a refund for every one of its repeated clicks. The fourth is the concurrency precondition of §2.9, which rejects a caller whose view of the campaign is stale with `ERR_STALE_VERSION` (HTTP 412); this guard matters especially here because a refund is a compensation of a still-unrefunded billed event, and a campaign view in which a second billable event has since committed is not a trustworthy basis for computing the corrective ledger entry. These four guards sit at tier 9 and tier 10 of the ordering in §2.8 — the referential and refundability checks precede the concurrency check — so an event that is simply not refundable reports that fact rather than reporting a version mismatch.

A granted refund moves the sponsored event's `status` to `REFUNDED`. It appends a `REFUND` ledger entry whose `amount_cents` is negative, so the campaign's `spent_cents` falls by the refunded amount through the invariant of §2.5. The campaign's `daily_spent_cents` falls by the same amount, restoring daily capacity that the day's earlier spend had consumed — and that restored capacity is itself meaningful, because returning enough of it can lift a sponsored job out of `BUDGET_HOLD` as described in §3.3. The employer's `balance_cents` falls by the refunded amount, reducing what the employer owes. The campaign's `version` is incremented, which is what makes a second refund against the same campaign subject to a fresh version precondition.

Separately from event-level refunds, the employer's billing posture is tracked through `billing_status` and `balance_cents`. A healthy employer account is `CURRENT`; an employer that is suspended has its `billing_status` set to `FROZEN` as part of the suspension described in §3.1, and reinstatement returns it to `CURRENT`. The `last_invoice_at` timestamp records the most recent invoice settlement and is null until the first settlement occurs. The relationship between an employer's outstanding `balance_cents`, its `billing_status` and the budget checks of §3.5 is that the balance is a receivable rather than a spending limit: the campaign's daily and total budgets are what bound spend, and the billing posture governs whether the account is permitted to transact at all.

### 3.9. Job Alerts, Matching and Relevance Ranking

A job seeker subscribes to alerts by creating a subscription naming their own `job_seeker_id`, a comma-separated `keywords` filter and a `location` filter. Both filters are matched case-insensitively, and the keyword filter is matched against the posting `title` while the location filter is matched against the posting `location`. A new subscription begins with `status` `ACTIVE`, an empty `notified_posting_ids` list and a null `last_notified_at`; the subscription's `status` remains `ACTIVE` for its entire lifetime in this system.

Ranking a posting for a seeker, whether for the on-demand matches view or for alert delivery, uses the `relevance_score`, a fixed-weight score summed from three components. The keyword component contributes 40 points when the subscription's keywords match the posting's title fully, 20 points when they match partially, and 0 points when they do not match. Matching is exact-token, case-insensitive, whitespace-split and deduplicated, so the comparison is over distinct tokens rather than over raw substrings. The location component contributes 35 points when the seeker's location matches the posting's location exactly, 15 points when the posting's location begins with the seeker's location as a prefix, and 0 points otherwise. The freshness component contributes 25 points when the posting's `current_freshness` is `FRESH`, 10 points when it is `STALE`, and 0 points when it is `EXPIRED`. The score is the sum of the three and is therefore an integer with a maximum of 100 for a fully matching, fresh posting. Two degenerate readings are worth ruling out: a seeker who supplies no location receives a location component of 0 and can therefore attain at most 65 points, and two postings that are identical on keywords and freshness can still be separated by up to 35 points on location. Only postings whose `state` is `ACTIVE` or `STALE` are ever ranked — expired and suspended postings are excluded from the pool entirely, so they can never be matched or delivered regardless of how well they would score.

Alert delivery is an administrative capability that walks the subscriptions and delivers candidate postings to each. When a specific `subscription_id` is supplied the delivery is limited to that subscription; when it is omitted every subscription is considered. For each subscription, the selector ranks the eligible postings by their relevance score exactly as above and delivers the highest-scoring ones first. A posting that already appears in the subscription's `notified_posting_ids` is excluded, which is what makes delivery non-repeating: the seeker is never alerted twice about the same posting through the same subscription. A delivery stamps `last_notified_at` from the logical clock and extends `notified_posting_ids` with everything delivered in that pass. The operation reports a `delivered_count` with the number of postings delivered, a `subscription_ids` array naming the subscriptions that received something, a `job_posting_ids` array naming what was delivered, and a `delivery_id` identifying the delivery run; a run that matches nothing reports a count of zero and empty arrays rather than failing.

The on-demand matches view answers the same ranking question for a single seeker without touching any subscription state. It is available to the seeker and to administrators, accepts optional `keywords` and `location` filters that override the seeker's stored profile for that request, and pages with `limit` and `offset` in the ordinary way. Because it is a read, it neither stamps `last_notified_at` nor extends `notified_posting_ids`; only an actual alert delivery does either.
