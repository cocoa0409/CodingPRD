# Elevator Service Contract API — Product Requirements Document

## 1. Introduction

A building owner who runs elevators does not really buy elevators; the owner buys *availability*, and buys it from a contractor under a written service agreement that almost nobody reads until something goes wrong. When a car drops out of service at 07:40 on a Monday, the questions that matter arrive all at once: was this a covered repair or an excluded one, how many minutes does the contractor have before the response clock is breached, was anybody inside the car, has the jurisdiction's inspection certificate lapsed, and — at the end of the month — how much of the maintenance fee is the owner entitled to hold back. Today those answers live in a filing cabinet, a dispatcher's spreadsheet, and the memory of whoever happened to take the call.

This document specifies the Elevator Service Contract Management API, a single system of record for a building owner's elevator program. It carries the commercial contract and its scope, the roster of cars under that contract, the monthly maintenance visit and its verified task list, every callback and entrapment together with the fire-department coordination that an entrapment demands, the jurisdictional inspection and the certificate that must be posted inside the car, the modernization projects and the downtime windows they consume, the spare-parts catalog with its obsolescence chains and lead times, and finally the two artifacts the whole program exists to produce: a defensible per-car uptime number and a performance penalty that either survives a dispute or does not.

The system is deliberately adversarial in design, because the two parties it serves are adversaries at month-end. Every consequential number is derived by the server from primary records rather than asserted by either party; the user who performs work may not be the user who signs off on it; the administrator who assesses a penalty may not be the administrator who forgives it; and the party who opens a renegotiation may not be the party who accepts it. Time is likewise not a matter of opinion: all instants come from a server-held logical clock that an administrator advances explicitly, so that a month can be closed, a certificate can be allowed to expire, and a missed visit can be recognised, all reproducibly.

## 2. Global Specifications & System-Wide Principles

Everything in this section applies to every endpoint of the API unless a later section explicitly narrows it. All resources are mounted under the `/elevator-program` prefix.

### 2.1. User and Role-Based Access Control

Every request to the API — reads included — must carry two headers. `X-User-ID` identifies the acting user and `X-User-Role` declares the capacity in which that user is acting. If either header is absent, or if `X-User-Role` is not one of the five role names defined below spelled exactly, the request is rejected with `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED` and HTTP 401. If both headers are well formed but the declared role is not among the roles authorised for the endpoint being called, the request is rejected with `ERR_UNAUTHORIZED` and HTTP 403.

The five roles are:

- **`ProgramAdministrator`** — the building-owner-side program administrator. This role owns contracts, the car roster, the spare-parts catalog, inspection scheduling, penalty assessment and resolution, and the logical clock.
- **`BuildingManager`** — the owner's representative for one specific building. Verifies and disputes maintenance work, posts inspection certificates inside cars, approves modernization projects, disputes penalties, and opens renegotiations.
- **`ServiceContractor`** — the elevator maintenance contractor engaged under one specific contract. Performs visits, arrives at and clears callbacks, orders spare parts, and books downtime.
- **`StateInspector`** — the jurisdictional elevator inspector. The only role permitted to record an inspection result.
- **`FireDispatcher`** — the fire district dispatcher who records department coordination on entrapment callbacks.

All five roles use an **opaque** identity model: `X-User-ID` is an operator identifier that is not the `id` of any persisted entity, and the server never compares it to an entity's `id`. What the server does with it differs by role. When `X-User-Role` is `ProgramAdministrator`, `X-User-ID` is an opaque operator identifier; the server records it where a transition asks for it — as `assessed_by_user_id` and `resolved_by_user_id` on a penalty assessment — but performs no ownership lookup against it. When `X-User-Role` is `StateInspector` or `FireDispatcher`, `X-User-ID` is likewise an opaque operator identifier; these roles are never contract parties, so no identity binding applies to them at all, and the server simply records the acting identity where a transition stores one.

`BuildingManager` and `ServiceContractor` are the two contract parties, and for them the opaque identifier is checked against values stored on the governing contract rather than against any entity `id`. When `X-User-Role` is `BuildingManager`, `X-User-ID` must equal the governing `ServiceContract`'s `building_manager_user_id`; when `X-User-Role` is `ServiceContractor`, `X-User-ID` must equal that contract's `contractor_user_id`. A mismatch is rejected with `ERR_NOT_CONTRACT_PARTY` and HTTP 403. The server records these identities where the workflow requires attribution — `performed_by_user_id` and `verified_by_user_id` on a visit, `posted_by_user_id` on an inspection, `approved_by_user_id` on a project, and `opened_by_user_id`, `countered_by_user_id` and `accepted_by_user_id` on a renegotiation round — but again, only as recorded strings.

The governing contract for any request is resolved from the target resource: if the target carries a `contract_id`, that is the contract; if it carries a `car_id`, the contract is the one named by that car's `contract_id`. Resources further down the chain — visits, callbacks, inspections, downtime windows and part orders — resolve through their car; penalties and renegotiation rounds resolve through their `contract_id` directly.

### 2.2. API Response Structure

#### Standard Success Response Format

All successful API responses (those with 2xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format in their JSON body:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z",
    "limit": 10,
    "offset": 0,
    "total_count": 100,
    "has_more": true
  },
  "response_type": "object" | "array",
  "data": { }
}
```

- The `api_request_id` is a server-generated unique identifier for the request that starts with `req_`.
- The `api_request_timestamp` is a server-generated ISO 8601 UTC timestamp with three fractional digits.
- The `response_type` is either "object" for a single resource or "array" for a list of resources.
- The `data` field contains the resource object or an array of resource objects. For 2xx responses with no data, it contains an empty object. Unless otherwise noted, operations that create or modify a resource will return the created or updated resource object in the data field.
- Pagination-related fields in `meta` (`limit`, `offset`, `total_count`, `has_more`) are included for list endpoints.

#### Standard Error Response Format

All error API responses (4xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z"
  },
  "response_type": "error",
  "data": {
    "error_id": "UNIQUE_ERROR_IDENTIFIER_STRING",
    "message": "A human-readable explanation of the error."
  }
}
```

- The `meta` object for error responses contains only `api_request_id` and `api_request_timestamp`.
- The `error_id` is a unique, machine-readable string that identifies the specific error.
- The `message` provides a human-readable explanation of what went wrong.
- A request to a path this document does not define, or to a defined path with a method it does not define, is answered with `ERR_RESOURCE_NOT_FOUND` and HTTP 404 in this same envelope.


### 2.3. Pagination

Every list endpoint accepts two optional query parameters. `limit` controls page size: its default is 10, its minimum accepted value is 1, and its maximum accepted value is 100. `offset` controls the starting position within the result set: its default is 0 and its minimum is 0. Neither bound is enforced by rejection: a `limit` below 1 is clamped up to 1, a `limit` above 100 is clamped down to 100, a negative `offset` is clamped up to 0, and a `limit` or `offset` that does not parse as an integer falls back to its default, so a list request never fails on pagination grounds. Results are always returned sorted by `created_at` in ascending order, so a stable page boundary is guaranteed regardless of which filters were supplied.

For list responses the `meta` object carries four pagination fields alongside the request identifier and timestamp: `limit` and `offset` echo the effective values used to build the page, `total_count` reports the number of records matching the filters before pagination was applied, and `has_more` is true when records remain beyond the current page. Single-resource responses omit all four.

### 2.4. Common Resource Properties

Every persisted resource in this system — service contracts, elevator cars, maintenance visits, callbacks, state inspections, modernization projects, downtime windows, spare parts, part orders, penalty assessments and renegotiation rounds alike — carries the same four common properties. `id` is a server-minted UUID with no type prefix of any kind. `created_at` and `updated_at` are ISO 8601 UTC timestamps rendered with exactly three fractional-second digits, and — alone among the timestamps this API emits, alongside the envelope's `api_request_timestamp` — they are read from the host's real wall clock rather than from the logical clock of §2.5; `created_at` is stamped once at creation and never changes, while `updated_at` is refreshed on every mutation of the resource. `version` is an integer that starts at 1 when the resource is created and is incremented by exactly 1 on every mutation of that resource. No entity in this system is exempt from the version counter or from `updated_at`.

The three-fractional-digit rendering binds only these server-minted stamps: a date or datetime the client supplies — `scheduled_date`, `term_start_date`, `term_end_date`, `target_completion_date`, `window_start`, `window_end` — is stored and echoed back byte for byte as it was sent, never re-normalised, and a `window_start` or `window_end` moved by the lead-time cascade of §3.6 is re-rendered in whatever textual shape the client originally used. Clients never supply `id`, `created_at`, `updated_at` or `version` on creation; all four are server-controlled. The `version` counter is the substrate for stale-write detection described in §2.8, and it is also the observable signal that a cascade actually reached a resource: when an operation on one resource changes a second resource, the second resource's `version` advances too.

### 2.5. The Logical Clock and the Epoch Sweep

This system reads real wall-clock time for nothing that matters. A single server-held **logical clock** is the sole time source for every business instant the system stamps: `reported_at`, `arrived_at`, `cleared_at`, `result_recorded_at`, `certificate_posted_at`, `performed_at`, `verified_at`, `unplanned_outage_open_since`, `red_tag_issued_at`, `red_tag_lifted_at`, `obsoleted_at`, the derivation of `promised_date`, certificate issuance and expiry, and every "is this month closed yet" check. On startup the clock reads exactly `2026-08-01T00:00:00Z`; before any administrator has set it, that is the value it reports, and it never silently falls back to the host's real time.

A `ProgramAdministrator` moves the clock by supplying a `to_iso8601` instant to the administrator-only clock capability. The instant is carried in a `to_iso8601` field, any parseable ISO 8601 UTC value is accepted including one earlier than the clock's present reading, and the call answers 200 rather than 201. The response `data` holds exactly six keys and no resource object: the resulting `current_utc` together with five counters describing what this move itself changed — `missed_visits_marked`, `rejected_visits_marked`, `certificates_expired`, `windows_completed` and `cars_under_modernization`, the last of which counts the cars this sweep pushed into `UNDER_MODERNIZATION` rather than the cars standing there.

Advancing the clock is not a passive act. It runs an **epoch sweep** that applies every time-driven transition the new instant has made due, and each resource the sweep mutates has its `version` incremented and its `updated_at` refreshed exactly as if a client had written to it. The sweep performs five families of work, all described in their business context in §3: maintenance visits still `SCHEDULED` when their billing month closes become `MISSED`; maintenance visits still `DISPUTED` when their billing month closes become `REJECTED`; posted certificates whose expiry date the clock has passed become `EXPIRED` and drag their car's compliance dimension from `CERTIFIED` to `CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED`; scheduled modernization windows the clock has entered move their car's availability dimension to `UNDER_MODERNIZATION` and their owning project from `APPROVED` to `IN_PROGRESS`; and scheduled windows the clock has moved to or past the end of become `COMPLETED`, returning a car whose modernization windows have all elapsed to `IN_SERVICE`.

### 2.6. Segregation of Duties

Three operations in this system carry a hard separation between the user who created a fact and the user who blesses it, and in all three the rejection is `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES` with HTTP 403. Verifying a maintenance visit requires an `X-User-ID` different from the visit's `performed_by_user_id`. Resolving a disputed penalty requires an `X-User-ID` different from that assessment's `assessed_by_user_id`. Countering or accepting a renegotiation round requires an `X-User-ID` different from that round's `opened_by_user_id` — a party may not negotiate with itself in either direction. Note that *withdrawing* a round is the mirror image and is reserved to the opener; that rule is described with the renegotiation workflow rather than here.

### 2.7. Order of Rejection

Because many rules can be violated by the same request, the order in which they are evaluated is part of the contract, not an implementation detail. Checks are applied strictly in the sequence below and **the first check that fails determines the response**; no later check is consulted. A request that would trip several rules always reports the earliest one.

1. **Authentication.** Both headers present and `X-User-Role` one of the five declared roles, otherwise `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED` (401). Applies to every endpoint.
2. **Authorization.** The presented role appears in the called endpoint's authorised-role list, otherwise `ERR_UNAUTHORIZED` (403). Applies to every endpoint.
3. **Existence.** Every resource the request names must exist, checked left to right: the identifier in the URL path first, and then the foreign-key fields in the request body in the fixed order `contract_id`, `car_id`, `part_id`, `window_id`, `project_id`. A missing contract yields `ERR_CONTRACT_NOT_FOUND`, a missing car `ERR_CAR_NOT_FOUND`, a missing spare part (whether named in the path, in `part_id`, or in `replacement_part_id`) `ERR_PART_NOT_FOUND`, and a missing visit, callback, inspection, project, downtime window, part order, penalty assessment or renegotiation round `ERR_RESOURCE_NOT_FOUND` — all with HTTP 404. This applies to every endpoint that has a path identifier or a foreign-key body field.
4. **Contract party.** The `BuildingManager` / `ServiceContractor` identity binding of §2.1, otherwise `ERR_NOT_CONTRACT_PARTY` (403). This applies to every write endpoint whose target resolves to a service contract — contracts, cars, visits, callbacks, inspections, projects, downtime windows, part orders, penalties and renegotiations.
5. **Body schema.** Every required input field present; every enum field carrying a declared member; every datetime parsing as ISO 8601 UTC and every date as `YYYY-MM-DD`; every `period_month` matching a four-digit year, a hyphen, and a two-digit month in the range 01 through 12; `window_end` strictly after `window_start`; `term_end_date` strictly after `term_start_date`; `scheduled_date` falling inside the visit's `period_month`; and `passengers_trapped` at least 1 whenever `callback_type` is `ENTRAPMENT`. Any failure yields `ERR_VALIDATION` (400). Applies to every write endpoint.
6. **Order quantity.** A part order's `quantity` must be at least 1; a zero or negative quantity is refused with `ERR_INVALID_QUANTITY` (422) before any pricing or supersession work is attempted.
7. **Stale parent version.** The supplied expected-version field must equal the current `version` of the optimistic-concurrency parent, otherwise `ERR_STALE_CONTRACT_VERSION` or `ERR_STALE_CAR_VERSION` (412) as described in §2.8. Applies to penalty assessment creation and part-order creation.
8. **State transition legality.** The target resource must currently sit in a state from which the requested transition is declared, otherwise `ERR_INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION` (409). Applies to every state-changing write.
9. **Governing contract status.** Operational writes require the governing contract to be in either `ACTIVE` or `SERVICE_ALERT`; opening a renegotiation requires the contract to be exactly `ACTIVE`; countering, accepting or closing a round requires it to be exactly `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION`. A violation is `ERR_CONTRACT_NOT_ACTIVE` (409). This rule guards fourteen operations: scheduling a visit, performing a visit, verifying a visit, reporting a callback, scheduling an inspection, planning a project, approving a project, reserving a downtime window, creating a part order, assessing a penalty, opening a renegotiation, countering a round, accepting a round, and closing a round.
10. **Segregation of duties.** As described in §2.6, `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES` (403), guarding visit verification, penalty resolution, round counter and round accept.
11. **Car decommissioned.** The target car's `availability_state` must not be `DECOMMISSIONED`, otherwise `ERR_CAR_DECOMMISSIONED` (409). This rule guards six operations: scheduling a visit, performing a visit, reporting a callback, scheduling an inspection, reserving a downtime window, and creating a part order.
12. **Car red-tagged.** The target car's `compliance_state` must not be `RED_TAGGED`, otherwise `ERR_CAR_RED_TAGGED` (409). This rule guards exactly two operations — reserving a downtime window and scheduling a maintenance visit — because a red-tagged car may take on no new scheduled work until a reinspection lifts the tag.
13. **Certificate issued.** Posting a certificate requires the inspection's `certificate_state` to be `ISSUED`, otherwise `ERR_CERTIFICATE_NOT_ISSUED` (409).
14. **Certificate still valid.** Posting a certificate requires the current logical-clock date to be on or before that inspection's `certificate_expiry_date`, otherwise `ERR_CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED` (409).
15. **Entrapment needs fire dispatch.** An entrapment callback may not be marked on site until the fire department has been dispatched, otherwise `ERR_ENTRAPMENT_REQUIRES_FIRE_DISPATCH` (422).
16. **Fire dispatch warranted.** Fire dispatch may only be recorded on a callback whose `callback_type` is `ENTRAPMENT` or whose `passengers_trapped` is at least 1, otherwise `ERR_FIRE_DISPATCH_NOT_REQUIRED` (422).
17. **Scope excludes repair.** On an `OIL_AND_GREASE` contract, a part order whose effective part has a `part_class` other than `LUBRICATION` falls outside contract scope and is rejected with `ERR_SCOPE_EXCLUDES_REPAIR` (422).
18. **Part not discontinued.** The requested part must resolve to an effective part whose `lifecycle_state` is not `DISCONTINUED`, otherwise `ERR_PART_DISCONTINUED` (422).
19. **Supersession depth.** The walk along successor pointers must terminate within three hops, otherwise `ERR_SUPERSESSION_CHAIN_TOO_DEEP` (422).
20. **One visit per car-month.** No other maintenance visit may already exist for the same `car_id` and `period_month` in any status other than `REJECTED`, otherwise `ERR_VISIT_MONTH_DUPLICATE` (409).
21. **Downtime interval conflict.** The first of the two downtime overlap rules, described in §3.5, rejecting with `ERR_DOWNTIME_WINDOW_CONFLICT` (409).
22. **Penalty period closed.** The assessed month must be closed, meaning the logical clock has reached or passed the first instant of the month following `period_month`, otherwise `ERR_PENALTY_PERIOD_NOT_CLOSED` (422).
23. **Modernization capacity.** The second of the two downtime overlap rules, described in §3.5, rejecting with `ERR_CONTRACT_MODERNIZATION_CAPACITY` (409).
24. **Renegotiation round limit.** The contract's `renegotiation_round_count` must be below 4 before a new round is appended, otherwise `ERR_RENEGOTIATION_ROUND_LIMIT` (422). This rule guards both opening a renegotiation and countering a round.
25. **Rework limit.** A visit's `rework_count` must be below 2 before it may be disputed again, otherwise `ERR_REWORK_LIMIT_EXCEEDED` (422).
26. **Cars in scope.** A penalty may only be assessed on a contract that has at least one car whose `availability_state` is not `DECOMMISSIONED`, otherwise `ERR_NO_CARS_IN_SCOPE` (422).
27. **Renegotiation warranted.** The evaluated performance score must be strictly below 75.00, otherwise `ERR_RENEGOTIATION_NOT_WARRANTED` (422).
28. **Task list complete.** Verification requires the visit's `missing_task_codes` to be empty, otherwise `ERR_TASK_LIST_INCOMPLETE` (422).

Two orderings inside that sequence deserve emphasis because they are easy to get backwards. First, the quantity check on a part order runs *before* the stale-version check, so an order carrying both a zero `quantity` and a stale `expected_car_version` reports `ERR_INVALID_QUANTITY`, not `ERR_STALE_CAR_VERSION`. Second, the penalty period-closed check runs *before* the no-cars-in-scope check and before the renegotiation-related checks, so an assessment for an open month on a car-less contract reports `ERR_PENALTY_PERIOD_NOT_CLOSED`.

### 2.8. Stale-Version Protection on Ledger and Car Writes

Exactly two write operations in this system are guarded by optimistic concurrency control, and both are guarded because they are legitimately repeatable operations whose repetition silently rewrites a running total.

Assessing a penalty must carry `expected_contract_version`, a non-optional field holding the `version` of the target `ServiceContract` as the client last observed it. Re-assessing a period is a supported operation — a fresh assessment supersedes the earlier `ISSUED` one for the same `contract_id` and `period_month` and rewrites the contract's penalty ledger — so a client working from a stale view of that ledger would otherwise overwrite balances it never saw. If `expected_contract_version` does not equal the contract's current `version`, the request is rejected with `ERR_STALE_CONTRACT_VERSION` and HTTP 412, and the client must re-read the contract before retrying.

Creating a part order must carry `expected_car_version`, a non-optional field holding the `version` of the target `ElevatorCar` as the client last observed it. Ordering the same part again for the same car is likewise legitimate — the newer order re-derives its promise date from the current supersession chain — so a client whose picture of the car's `open_part_order_count`, `availability_state` or downtime windows has gone stale must refresh before ordering. If `expected_car_version` does not equal the car's current `version`, the request is rejected with `ERR_STALE_CAR_VERSION` and HTTP 412.

No other endpoint in this system accepts an expected-version field. Sending one elsewhere has no effect, and no other endpoint may reject a request on version grounds.

### 2.9. Money, Minutes and Rounding

All money in this system is expressed in whole cents as integers; there are no fractional currency values anywhere in the API. Wherever a monetary computation produces a fraction of a cent, the result is rounded half up to a whole number of cents, and — this is the part that changes answers — the rounding is applied exactly once per bracketed product at the point the formulas in §3 indicate, never earlier and never later. Two of the calculations in this document depend on the placement of that single rounding step for their result, and both call the placement out explicitly.

Uptime percentages and the contract performance score are decimal numbers carried to 2 decimal places, rounded half up. Minute counts — period lengths, outage lengths, window durations, response times — are whole minutes, truncated toward zero. Lead times and shift distances are whole days.

### 2.10. Two-Dimensional Car State and Cascade Discipline

An elevator car carries **two independent state dimensions** that must never be conflated. `availability_state` answers "can the public ride it": its values are `IN_SERVICE`, `OUT_OF_SERVICE`, `UNDER_MODERNIZATION` and `DECOMMISSIONED`, with `DECOMMISSIONED` terminal. `compliance_state` answers "is the paperwork in order": its values are `CERTIFIED`, `INSPECTION_DUE`, `RED_TAGGED` and `CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED`, none of which is terminal. A newly registered car starts at `IN_SERVICE` on the first dimension and `INSPECTION_DUE` on the second.

The two dimensions evolve independently, with exactly one guarded coupling: driving the compliance dimension to `RED_TAGGED` forces the availability dimension from `IN_SERVICE` to `OUT_OF_SERVICE`, and lifting the tag returns it. A car may perfectly well be `IN_SERVICE` while `CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED`; nothing in the system stops it. Similarly, a `StateInspection` carries two dimensions of its own — an inspection `status` and a `certificate_state` — and a certificate can be posted long after the inspection itself reached `COMPLETED`, and can expire while the inspection row is never touched again.

Cascades in this system are transitive and each hop is observable. When an operation changes a second or third resource, that resource's `version` advances and its `updated_at` refreshes, in exactly the same way as if it had been written directly. Two long cascades in particular are load-bearing and are described in full where they belong: the four-hop chain that begins with a failed inspection and ends with the contract escalating to `SERVICE_ALERT` (§3.4), and the three-hop chain that begins with a part order against an obsolete part and ends with a modernization project flagged `AT_RISK` (§3.6).

### 2.11. Reference Resolution and Reverse Reachability

The URL space of this API is flat: every resource is addressed as a top-level collection member under `/elevator-program`, and parentage is expressed by foreign-key fields carried in request bodies rather than by nested paths. That flatness does not weaken the resolution rules. When a request names several resources, they are resolved left to right — the identifier embedded in the path first, then the body's foreign keys in the order `contract_id`, `car_id`, `part_id`, `window_id`, `project_id` — and the first one that cannot be resolved determines the 404 that comes back.

Because parentage is not visible in the URL, every collection offers a filtered list endpoint that serves as the reverse-reachability channel from parent to children: cars can be filtered by `contract_id`, `availability_state` and `compliance_state`; visits by `car_id`, `status` and `period_month`; callbacks by `car_id`, `status` and `callback_type`; inspections by `car_id`, `status` and `certificate_state`; projects by `contract_id` and `status`; downtime windows by `car_id`, `project_id`, `status` and `window_type`; catalog parts by `superseded_by_part_id`, `lifecycle_state` and `part_class`; part orders by `car_id`, `part_id`, `effective_part_id`, `window_id` and `status`; penalty assessments by `contract_id`, `status` and `period_month`; renegotiation rounds by `contract_id` and `status`; and contracts themselves by `status` and `scope_type`. Every one of these filters is optional and combinable, and all of them coexist with `limit` and `offset`.

## 3. Business Logic & Workflows

### 3.1. Standing Up a Building Program

Every program begins with a service contract drafted by a `ProgramAdministrator`. The draft records who and where — `building_name` and `building_address` — together with the two regulatory codes that govern it: `jurisdiction_code`, the state or city inspection authority code such as NY-NYC, and `fire_district_code`, the fire district that will be coordinated with when somebody is trapped. It names its two counterparties by recording the opaque operator identifiers `building_manager_user_id` and `contractor_user_id`; these are the values against which §2.1's contract-party binding is later checked.

Two enumerated choices made at drafting time propagate through nearly every calculation in this document. `scope_type` is either `FULL_MAINTENANCE` or `OIL_AND_GREASE`, and it simultaneously selects which maintenance task codes are mandatory, which classes of spare part may be ordered, and which of the two uptime variants is used when money is at stake. `penalty_schedule` is either `SCHEDULE_A` or `SCHEDULE_B`, and it selects which of two structurally different penalty formulas is applied at month-end. Alongside them the draft carries the commercial and service terms: `monthly_base_fee_cents`, the contracted monthly maintenance fee in cents and at least 1; `callback_response_minutes`, the number of minutes the contractor has to reach the site for a malfunction or nuisance call, at least 1; `entrapment_response_minutes`, the tighter allowance that applies when passengers are trapped, likewise at least 1; and the term boundaries `term_start_date` and `term_end_date`, both written as a four-digit year, a hyphen, a two-digit month, a hyphen and a two-digit day.

A newly drafted contract lands in `status` `DRAFT` with `version` 1 and its entire penalty ledger zeroed — `penalty_lifetime_assessed_cents`, `penalty_lifetime_waived_cents`, `penalty_lifetime_credited_cents` and `penalty_balance_cents` all 0 — along with `cars_out_of_service_count` 0, `renegotiation_round_count` 0, and `performance_score` null, because no score has yet been evaluated. Activation by a `ProgramAdministrator` moves it `DRAFT → ACTIVE`; it requires that the contract is still in `DRAFT` and that `term_end_date` is strictly later than `term_start_date`, and it increments the contract's `version` by 1. Nothing operational may happen on a contract that has not been activated.

Termination is the other end of the story and is also reserved to a `ProgramAdministrator`. A contract may be terminated from `ACTIVE`, from `SERVICE_ALERT` or from `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION`, and in all three cases the identical cascade runs: every renegotiation round on the contract that is still `OPEN` or `COUNTERED` becomes `WITHDRAWN`; every modernization project on the contract in `PLANNED`, `APPROVED`, `IN_PROGRESS` or `AT_RISK` becomes `CANCELLED`, which in turn sets each of that project's `SCHEDULED` downtime windows to `CANCELLED` and recomputes the affected cars' `scheduled_downtime_minutes`; and the contract itself moves to `TERMINATED` with its `version` incremented by 1. `TERMINATED` is terminal — nothing leaves it.

Between activation and termination the contract has one further state it can enter and leave on its own. The system maintains `cars_out_of_service_count` as the count of cars on the contract whose `availability_state` is exactly `OUT_OF_SERVICE`; cars that are `DECOMMISSIONED` or `UNDER_MODERNIZATION` are not counted. This count is recomputed on every car transition into or out of `OUT_OF_SERVICE` and on every decommissioning. Whenever the contract is `ACTIVE` and that count reaches **two or more** cars simultaneously out of service, the contract escalates `ACTIVE → SERVICE_ALERT` and its `version` is incremented; the threshold is inclusive, so exactly two cars is enough to escalate. Whenever the contract is in `SERVICE_ALERT` and the count falls to **one or fewer**, it de-escalates `SERVICE_ALERT → ACTIVE`, again incrementing `version`. Neither transition is ever requested by a client; both are consequences of a car's availability changing.

The practical meaning of `SERVICE_ALERT` is that the program keeps running but stops taking on new planned outages. Ordinary operational writes remain permitted while the contract is either `ACTIVE` or `SERVICE_ALERT`, with one deliberate exception: reserving a new downtime window on any car of the contract requires the contract to be `ACTIVE`, so while a contract sits in `SERVICE_ALERT` a downtime reservation is refused with `ERR_CONTRACT_NOT_ACTIVE`. Opening a renegotiation likewise requires `ACTIVE` exactly, and countering, accepting or closing a round requires `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION` exactly.

Cars are registered under a contract by a `ProgramAdministrator`, one at a time, with a payload carrying the `contract_id` of the program they join, a `car_code` label assigned by the owner and expected to be unique within that contract though the server does not itself refuse a duplicate, a `car_type` of either `TRACTION` or `HYDRAULIC`, and a `capacity_lbs` rating of at least 1 pound. `car_code` is more than a label: it is the pinned ascending sort key for every per-car iteration in this document, so any calculation that walks a contract's cars walks them in `car_code` ascending order. A new car begins at `IN_SERVICE` and `INSPECTION_DUE`, with `version` 1, `scheduled_downtime_minutes` 0, `open_part_order_count` 0, and `unplanned_outage_open_since`, `red_tag_issued_at`, `red_tag_lifted_at`, `current_certificate_number` and `certificate_expiry_date` all null. Registering a car does **not** mutate the contract row — the contract's `version` is untouched by car creation.

Decommissioning is permanent and is reserved to a `ProgramAdministrator`. A car may be decommissioned from `IN_SERVICE` or from `OUT_OF_SERVICE`, and the same cascade runs either way: every part order on the car still in `ORDERED` or `SUBSTITUTED` becomes `CANCELLED`; every downtime window on the car still `SCHEDULED` becomes `CANCELLED`, after which the car's `scheduled_downtime_minutes` is recomputed and the `window_count` of every owning project not already `COMPLETE` or `CANCELLED` is recomputed as the number of its windows left in `SCHEDULED` or `COMPLETED`; every state inspection on the car still `SCHEDULED` becomes `CANCELLED` and mints no certificate; every callback on the car still in `REPORTED`, `ESCALATED` or `ON_SITE` becomes `CANCELLED` and thereafter contributes no outage interval to any uptime calculation; the car's `availability_state` becomes `DECOMMISSIONED`; and the car's `version` is incremented by 1. From that instant the car is excluded from every contract-level uptime, credit and penalty aggregate, although its own per-car uptime report still answers. When the decommissioned car was in `OUT_OF_SERVICE`, the contract's `cars_out_of_service_count` is decremented by 1 as part of the same cascade, which may in turn de-escalate the contract from `SERVICE_ALERT` back to `ACTIVE`.

### 3.2. The Monthly Maintenance Visit and Its Rework Loop

The heart of a maintenance contract is a visit per car per month, and this system models exactly that. A `ProgramAdministrator` or a `ServiceContractor` schedules one by naming the `car_id`, the billing month as `period_month` in four-digit-year-hyphen-two-digit-month form, and a `scheduled_date` that must itself fall inside that month. The visit is created in `SCHEDULED` with `rework_count` 0 and every timestamp field — `performed_at`, `performed_by_user_id`, `verified_at`, `verified_by_user_id`, `dispute_reason` — null, and it carries the submitted `task_items` alongside the derived `completed_task_codes` and `missing_task_codes`, all three empty arrays until the first performance. Only one live visit may exist per car and month: if another visit already covers the same `car_id` and `period_month` in any status other than `REJECTED`, the request is refused with `ERR_VISIT_MONTH_DUPLICATE`. A rejected visit does not block a replacement, which is exactly how a month that went badly can be re-opened. A car that is `DECOMMISSIONED` cannot take a new visit (`ERR_CAR_DECOMMISSIONED`), and neither can one that is `RED_TAGGED` (`ERR_CAR_RED_TAGGED`).

Performing the visit is the `ServiceContractor`'s move and drives `SCHEDULED → PERFORMED`. The contractor submits `task_items`, an array whose elements each carry a `task_code` string, a `completed` boolean, and a `note` string. The array **replaces** whatever was submitted before rather than merging with it: only the most recent submission counts, and an earlier submission's contents are discarded outright. From that array the server derives two fields. `completed_task_codes` is the set of `task_code` values whose `completed` flag is true, sorted in ascending order. `missing_task_codes` is the list of task codes required by the contract's `scope_type` that do not appear in `completed_task_codes`, also sorted ascending. Performing also stamps `performed_at` from the logical clock and `performed_by_user_id` from the acting `X-User-ID`. A performed visit cannot be recorded against a `DECOMMISSIONED` car.

Which codes are required depends entirely on scope. A `FULL_MAINTENANCE` contract requires all seven of `BRAKE`, `CONTROLLER`, `DOOR_OPERATOR`, `FIRE_SERVICE_TEST`, `GOVERNOR`, `PIT_CLEAN` and `ROPES`. An `OIL_AND_GREASE` contract requires only the three of `LUBRICATION`, `PIT_CLEAN` and `VISUAL_INSPECTION`. Comparison is exact and case-sensitive: a submitted code that differs from a required code by so much as a letter case does not satisfy it. A contractor is free to submit codes beyond the required set — extra completed codes appear in `completed_task_codes` and simply have no effect on `missing_task_codes`.

Verification is the `BuildingManager`'s move and drives `PERFORMED → VERIFIED`, stamping `verified_at` and `verified_by_user_id`. It is gated four ways. The visit must currently be `PERFORMED`; the governing contract must be in `ACTIVE` or `SERVICE_ALERT`; `missing_task_codes` must be empty, and if it is not the request is refused with `ERR_TASK_LIST_INCOMPLETE`; and the verifying `X-User-ID` must differ from the recorded `performed_by_user_id`, or the request is refused with `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES`. `VERIFIED` is terminal — a verified visit is settled and cannot be re-opened.

When the work does not measure up, the same `BuildingManager` disputes it instead, driving `PERFORMED → DISPUTED` with a `dispute_reason` recorded on the visit and `rework_count` incremented by 1. The contractor may then perform the visit again, driving `DISPUTED → PERFORMED`; this second performance replaces `task_items` wholesale and recomputes both derived code lists, and refreshes `performed_at` and `performed_by_user_id`. That loop — performed, disputed, performed again — is bounded: a visit whose `rework_count` has already reached **two** may not be disputed a third time, and the attempt is refused with `ERR_REWORK_LIMIT_EXCEEDED`. In practice this gives the parties two rounds of rework and then forces a decision.

Two terminal outcomes are imposed by the passage of time rather than by either party. When the logical clock reaches or passes the first instant of the month *after* a visit's `period_month`, the epoch sweep turns any visit still `SCHEDULED` into `MISSED` and any visit still `DISPUTED` into `REJECTED`. Both outcomes are terminal, and — this is the commercial point — both count toward the `missed_visit_count` that drives that month's penalty. A visit sitting in `PERFORMED` when the month closes is left alone by the sweep; it is neither missed nor rejected, and it is still open for verification or dispute afterwards.

### 3.3. Callbacks, Entrapments and Fire-Department Coordination

A callback is what the industry calls a trouble call, and in this system it is reported by either a `BuildingManager` or a `ProgramAdministrator`. The report names the `car_id` and a `callback_type` of `ENTRAPMENT`, `MALFUNCTION` or `NUISANCE`. It may optionally carry `passengers_trapped`, a count that defaults to 0 and can never be negative, and `out_of_service`, a boolean that defaults to **true** and declares whether this call took the car out of service. An entrapment by definition has someone inside it, so a `callback_type` of `ENTRAPMENT` accompanied by a `passengers_trapped` of 0 is a malformed request and is refused with `ERR_VALIDATION`. The callback is created in `status` `REPORTED` with `reported_at` stamped from the logical clock, with `fire_dispatch_requested_at`, `fire_dispatch_incident_number`, `arrived_at`, `cleared_at`, `resolution_note`, `response_minutes` and `sla_minutes` all null but `sla_breached` already present and false rather than null; that instant is both the start of the response-time measurement and the start of any unplanned outage interval the call contributes.

When a callback is reported with `out_of_service` true against a car that is currently `IN_SERVICE`, the car's availability dimension moves `IN_SERVICE → OUT_OF_SERVICE` in the same operation. That move records `unplanned_outage_open_since` at the current logical clock, increments the governing contract's `cars_out_of_service_count` by 1, and increments the car's `version` by 1 — and, when the count thereby reaches two, escalates the contract from `ACTIVE` to `SERVICE_ALERT` and increments the contract's `version` as well. A callback reported with `out_of_service` false leaves the car's availability alone and contributes no outage interval; so does a call against a car already `OUT_OF_SERVICE`, whose `unplanned_outage_open_since` and whose contract counter are both left untouched by the second call.

The response path forks according to whether the fire department is involved. A `FireDispatcher` records department coordination, driving `REPORTED → ESCALATED` and supplying an `incident_number` that is stored as `fire_dispatch_incident_number` alongside `fire_dispatch_requested_at` from the logical clock. That branch is only available where it is warranted: the callback must still be `REPORTED`, and it must either have `callback_type` `ENTRAPMENT` or carry a `passengers_trapped` of at least 1. A dispatch recorded on any other callback is refused with `ERR_FIRE_DISPATCH_NOT_REQUIRED`.

Arrival on site is the `ServiceContractor`'s move and is the join point where the two branches converge on `ON_SITE`. A non-entrapment callback goes straight `REPORTED → ON_SITE`. An entrapment callback may not: it must first have been escalated to the fire department, so `ESCALATED → ON_SITE` is the only path available to it, and attempting to arrive at an entrapment still sitting in `REPORTED` is refused with `ERR_ENTRAPMENT_REQUIRES_FIRE_DISPATCH`. Both branches carry the identical side-effect set. Arrival stamps `arrived_at` from the logical clock and freezes three derived fields at that instant. `response_minutes` is the number of whole minutes elapsed from `reported_at` to `arrived_at`. `sla_minutes` is the allowance that applied to this call: for a callback of type `ENTRAPMENT` it is the contract's `entrapment_response_minutes`, and for `MALFUNCTION` and `NUISANCE` alike it is the contract's `callback_response_minutes`. `sla_breached` is true when `response_minutes` is strictly greater than `sla_minutes` and false otherwise — arriving in exactly the allotted number of minutes is on time, not late. All three values are frozen at arrival and are not recomputed later, even if the contract's response terms are subsequently renegotiated.

Clearing the call is again the contractor's move, driving `ON_SITE → CLEARED`. It stamps `cleared_at` and records an optional `resolution_note`. Clearing closes the unplanned outage interval that began at `reported_at`, so that from the uptime calculation's point of view the car was unavailable across the half-open span from `reported_at` up to but not including `cleared_at`. It also attempts to return the car to service: the car's availability dimension moves `OUT_OF_SERVICE → IN_SERVICE`, `unplanned_outage_open_since` is cleared back to null, the contract's `cars_out_of_service_count` is decremented by 1 and the car's `version` is incremented — but only when two conditions hold together. No other callback on that car may remain in `REPORTED`, `ESCALATED` or `ON_SITE` with `out_of_service` true, and the car's `compliance_state` must not be `RED_TAGGED`. A red-tagged car stays out of service no matter how many callbacks are cleared. When the return to service does happen and it drops the contract's out-of-service count to one or fewer, the contract de-escalates `SERVICE_ALERT → ACTIVE`.

`CLEARED` and `CANCELLED` are the two terminal callback states. Cancellation is never requested directly; it arrives only as part of the decommissioning cascade of §3.1, which sweeps every callback on the car that is `REPORTED`, `ESCALATED` or `ON_SITE` into `CANCELLED`. A cancelled callback is excluded from every uptime calculation entirely — it contributes no outage interval whatsoever, not even the portion before it was cancelled.

### 3.4. Inspection, Red Tags and the Posted Certificate

Jurisdictions require elevators to be inspected and require the resulting certificate to be physically posted inside the car, and this system tracks both halves separately. A `ProgramAdministrator` schedules an inspection by naming the `car_id`, an `inspection_type` of `PERIODIC`, `FIVE_YEAR_FULL_LOAD` or `REINSPECTION`, and a `scheduled_date`. The inspection is created with `status` `SCHEDULED` and `certificate_state` `NONE`, with `result`, `result_recorded_at`, `certificate_number`, `certificate_issued_date`, `certificate_expiry_date`, `certificate_posted_at` and `posted_by_user_id` all null, and `deficiency_codes` an empty array. A `DECOMMISSIONED` car cannot be scheduled for inspection.

A `StateInspector` — the only role permitted to do so — records the outcome, driving the inspection dimension `SCHEDULED → COMPLETED` and storing `result` together with an optional `deficiency_codes` array of code strings and a `result_recorded_at` stamp from the logical clock. The inspection must still be `SCHEDULED`; recording a result is not among the six operations §2.7 guards against a `DECOMMISSIONED` car, so the car's availability dimension is not consulted here at all. What happens next depends entirely on the recorded `result`, and the two branches are as different as they look.

On a `result` of `PASS` or `PASS_WITH_DEFICIENCIES` a certificate is minted and the certificate dimension moves `NONE → ISSUED`. Three values are derived at that moment. `certificate_issued_date` is the calendar date part of `result_recorded_at`. `certificate_expiry_date` is that issue date advanced by **365 days**, and expiry is inclusive: the certificate is valid *through* `certificate_expiry_date` and only lapses once the clock has moved past it. `certificate_number` is assembled as the literal text `CERT-`, then the governing contract's `jurisdiction_code`, then a hyphen, then the car's `car_code`, then a hyphen, then the issue date written as an eight-digit year-month-day string with no separators — so a car labelled CAR-01 in jurisdiction NY-NYC certified on 3 September 2026 receives `CERT-NY-NYC-CAR-01-20260903`. Issuing a certificate does not by itself change the car's `compliance_state`; the car remains where it was until someone posts the certificate in the car.

On a `result` of `FAIL` no certificate is minted, `certificate_state` stays at `NONE`, and the four-hop red-tag cascade begins. Hop one is the inspection itself reaching `COMPLETED` with the failing result recorded. Hop two drives the car's compliance dimension to `RED_TAGGED` from whichever of `INSPECTION_DUE`, `CERTIFIED` or `CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED` it occupied, recording `red_tag_issued_at` at the current logical clock; a car already `RED_TAGGED` is not re-tagged. When the car was `CERTIFIED`, the same hop additionally drives every one of that car's `POSTED` certificates to `SUPERSEDED` and then recomputes the car's two certificate mirror fields, which consequently both go null. Hop three forces the availability dimension `IN_SERVICE → OUT_OF_SERVICE`, recording `unplanned_outage_open_since`. Hop four increments the contract's `cars_out_of_service_count`, and if that count reaches two the contract escalates `ACTIVE → SERVICE_ALERT`. Every resource touched along the chain — the inspection, the car, the superseded certificate's inspection row, the contract — has its `version` incremented and its `updated_at` refreshed.

Lifting a red tag requires a reinspection, and only a reinspection. When a `StateInspector` records a `result` of `PASS` or `PASS_WITH_DEFICIENCIES` on an inspection whose `inspection_type` is `REINSPECTION` and whose car is currently `RED_TAGGED`, the compliance dimension moves `RED_TAGGED → INSPECTION_DUE`, `red_tag_lifted_at` is stamped from the logical clock, a fresh certificate is issued on that reinspection exactly as described above (`NONE → ISSUED`), and the availability dimension returns `OUT_OF_SERVICE → IN_SERVICE` provided no open out-of-service callback remains on the car. A passing inspection of type `PERIODIC` or `FIVE_YEAR_FULL_LOAD` mints its certificate but does **not** lift an existing red tag; the car stays `RED_TAGGED` and stays out of service.

Posting the certificate is the `BuildingManager`'s act and is what actually makes a car compliant. It drives the certificate dimension `ISSUED → POSTED`, stamps `certificate_posted_at` and `posted_by_user_id`, and moves the car's `compliance_state` to `CERTIFIED` from either `INSPECTION_DUE` or `CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED`. Two gates apply, and they report different codes: the inspection's `certificate_state` must be exactly `ISSUED`, or the attempt is refused with `ERR_CERTIFICATE_NOT_ISSUED`; and the current logical-clock date must be on or before that certificate's `certificate_expiry_date`, or the attempt is refused with `ERR_CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED`. Posting also cleans up behind itself: any other inspection on the same car whose `certificate_state` was `POSTED` is driven to `SUPERSEDED`, so a car has at most one posted certificate at a time.

After every posting the car's two certificate mirror fields are recomputed. `current_certificate_number` takes the `certificate_number` of the inspection on that car whose `certificate_state` is `POSTED` and whose `certificate_expiry_date` is the latest; where two posted certificates share the same expiry date, the tie is broken by taking the one with the earlier `created_at`. `certificate_expiry_date` on the car mirrors that same selected inspection's expiry. When no posted certificate exists on the car, both fields are null.

Certificates lapse by the clock, not by request. When the epoch sweep advances the logical clock past a car's `certificate_expiry_date`, that inspection's `certificate_state` moves `POSTED → EXPIRED`, the car's two certificate mirror fields are recomputed over whatever posted certificates survive, and the car's `compliance_state` moves `CERTIFIED → CERTIFICATE_EXPIRED`; the car's `version` advances on that recomputation whether or not its compliance dimension actually moved. Note that this changes nothing about the car's availability: a car with a lapsed certificate keeps running as far as this system is concerned, and only a red tag stops it. `EXPIRED` and `SUPERSEDED` are the two terminal certificate states, and `COMPLETED` and `CANCELLED` the two terminal inspection states; a `SCHEDULED` inspection on a car being decommissioned is swept to `CANCELLED` and mints nothing.

### 3.5. Modernization Planning and the Downtime Calendar

Modernizing an elevator means taking it out of service for days at a time, which is why planning is a first-class object here. A `ProgramAdministrator` plans a project against a contract by supplying the `contract_id`, a short `title`, an approved capital `budget_cents` of at least 1 cent, and a `target_completion_date`. The project is created in `PLANNED` with `window_count` 0 and `approved_by_user_id` null. Planning does not commit anybody to anything; it merely opens the file.

A `BuildingManager` approves the plan, driving `PLANNED → APPROVED` and stamping `approved_by_user_id`. Approval requires the project to be in `PLANNED` and the governing contract to be in `ACTIVE` or `SERVICE_ALERT`. Closing a project out is the administrator's job again: from `APPROVED`, from `IN_PROGRESS` or from `AT_RISK`, a project moves to `COMPLETE` provided no downtime window belonging to it remains in `SCHEDULED` — a surviving scheduled window is refused with `ERR_INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION` — at which point `window_count` is frozen at its then-current value. `COMPLETE` and `CANCELLED` are the terminal project states; cancellation arrives only through the contract-termination cascade described in §3.1, which also cancels the project's scheduled windows and recomputes each affected car's `scheduled_downtime_minutes`.

The remaining two project transitions are driven by machinery rather than by people. When the logical clock advances into the first scheduled modernization window of an approved project, the project moves `APPROVED → IN_PROGRESS` and the affected car's availability dimension moves `IN_SERVICE → UNDER_MODERNIZATION` in the same sweep; when the clock later reaches or passes the end of every scheduled modernization window on that car, each elapsed window becomes `COMPLETED` and the car returns `UNDER_MODERNIZATION → IN_SERVICE`. Separately, a project can be flagged as endangered: whenever a part-lead-time cascade shifts one of its windows, the project's `target_completion_date` is recomputed as the latest calendar date among the `window_end` values of its windows in `SCHEDULED`, `COMPLETED` or `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED`, and if that recomputed date falls after the governing contract's `term_end_date` the project moves to `AT_RISK` from either `APPROVED` or `IN_PROGRESS`. `AT_RISK` is not a dead end — such a project can still be completed by the administrator on the same terms as any other.

Downtime windows are the calendar entries those projects consume, and they are booked by a `ProgramAdministrator` or a `ServiceContractor`. A reservation names the `car_id`, a `window_type` of `MODERNIZATION`, `INSPECTION` or `REPAIR`, an inclusive `window_start` and an exclusive `window_end` that must be strictly later than the start, and — for modernization work only — the `project_id` of the owning project, which is left null for inspection and repair windows; that correspondence is a convention the caller keeps rather than a rule the server enforces, since `project_id` is simply optional and is never cross-checked against `window_type`. The window is created in `SCHEDULED` with `reschedule_count` 0 and a derived `duration_minutes` equal to the number of whole minutes from `window_start` to `window_end`. Creating it adds that `duration_minutes` to the car's `scheduled_downtime_minutes`, increments the owning project's `window_count` by 1 when a `project_id` was supplied, and increments the car's `version` by 1. A car that is `DECOMMISSIONED` cannot take a window (`ERR_CAR_DECOMMISSIONED`), nor can one that is `RED_TAGGED` (`ERR_CAR_RED_TAGGED`).

The car's `scheduled_downtime_minutes` is, at all times, the sum of `duration_minutes` over that car's windows whose `status` is `SCHEDULED` or `COMPLETED`. Windows in `CANCELLED` or `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED` occupy nothing and contribute nothing. This is a conserved quantity: cancelling a `SCHEDULED` window subtracts exactly its `duration_minutes` and restores the pre-creation figure exactly, while a window that has already moved to `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED` had its minutes removed at that moment and its later cancellation therefore leaves the total unchanged.

Two overlap rules guard every reservation, and they deliberately disagree about what "overlap" means; only the first of them is re-applied when a part-lead-time cascade proposes to shift a window, the contract-wide modernization rule being skipped on that path.

**The first rule is per-car, at whole-minute precision, on half-open intervals.** The candidate occupies the span from `window_start` up to but not including `window_end`. For each existing window on the same car whose `status` is `SCHEDULED` or `COMPLETED`, the candidate is rejected with `ERR_DOWNTIME_WINDOW_CONFLICT` if the two half-open spans intersect at all. Because the intersection is strictly half-open, a candidate that begins at the exact instant an existing window ends does not collide, and neither does one that ends at the exact instant an existing window begins — same-type windows may abut with a zero-minute gap. The rule then adds a buffer that applies only between windows of *different* `window_type`: if the existing window's type differs from the candidate's and the gap between the two spans is less than **two hours**, the candidate is rejected with the same `ERR_DOWNTIME_WINDOW_CONFLICT`. In other words, a repair window and a modernization window on one car must be separated by at least 120 minutes of clear air, while two repair windows may touch.

**The second rule is contract-wide, applies only to modernization work, and is measured in whole calendar dates on closed ranges.** A candidate whose `window_type` is `MODERNIZATION` occupies every calendar date from the date of its `window_start` through the date of the instant one minute before its `window_end`, inclusive at both ends. For every *other* car on the same contract — `DECOMMISSIONED` cars included, since only the windows' own statuses are filtered — and for every modernization window on that car whose `status` is `SCHEDULED` or `COMPLETED`, the same closed date range is computed. If the candidate's date range shares even a single calendar date with any of them, the reservation is rejected with `ERR_CONTRACT_MODERNIZATION_CAPACITY`, because the program tolerates **at most one car under modernization at a time** across the whole contract. Note the asymmetry this creates: two modernization windows on the *same* car are judged only by the minute-precision half-open rule, while two on *different* cars of the same contract are judged by the coarser calendar-date rule, so windows that do not overlap by a single minute can still collide by sharing a date.

Cancelling a window is available to a `ProgramAdministrator` or a `ServiceContractor`. A window in `SCHEDULED` may be cancelled only while the logical clock is strictly before its `window_start` — once a window has begun, it can no longer be called off, and the attempt is refused with `ERR_INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION` — and a window in `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED` may be cancelled at any time. Either way the window moves to `CANCELLED`, the car's `scheduled_downtime_minutes` is brought back into line with the definition above, the owning project's `window_count` is decremented by 1 when `project_id` is set, and the car's `version` is incremented by 1. `COMPLETED` and `CANCELLED` are the terminal window states.

### 3.6. Spare Parts, Obsolescence and the Lead-Time Cascade

The parts catalog is maintained by a `ProgramAdministrator`, who adds an entry with a `part_number` expected to be unique across the catalog though the server does not itself refuse a duplicate, a human-readable `description_text`, a `part_class` of `LUBRICATION`, `WEAR_ITEM`, `COMPONENT` or `CONTROLLER_BOARD`, a `unit_price_cents` of at least 1 cent, and a baseline manufacturer `lead_time_days` of zero or more whole days. New catalog entries begin in `lifecycle_state` `ACTIVE` with `superseded_by_part_id` and `obsoleted_at` both null.

Parts age out in two different ways, and the difference matters enormously downstream. An administrator retiring a part supplies an optional `replacement_part_id`. When a replacement *is* supplied, the part moves `ACTIVE → OBSOLETE`: `superseded_by_part_id` is set to the replacement and `obsoleted_at` is stamped from the logical clock, and every subsequent order naming the retired part quietly resolves forward along the successor chain. The replacement must exist, or the request is refused with `ERR_PART_NOT_FOUND`; it must not be the part itself and must not itself be `DISCONTINUED`, either of which is refused with `ERR_VALIDATION`. All three of those checks run before the retired part's own lifecycle is examined, and a retired part sitting outside the states its branch declares is then refused with `ERR_INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION`. When a replacement is *omitted*, the part is instead driven to `DISCONTINUED` — from `ACTIVE` directly, or from `OBSOLETE`, in which case its `superseded_by_part_id` is cleared back to null — `obsoleted_at` is stamped, and every subsequent order naming it is refused outright with `ERR_PART_DISCONTINUED`. `DISCONTINUED` is terminal.

Ordering a part is the `ServiceContractor`'s move. The order names the `car_id` it is destined for, the `part_id` as the contractor knows it, a `quantity` of at least 1, and the `expected_car_version` that §2.8 requires. It may also carry `expedite`, a boolean defaulting to false that buys expedited freight, and `window_id`, the identifier of a downtime window this delivery gates. Pricing and promising then run in a **pinned sequence** that must not be reordered, because one of its steps looks circular and is not.

First the supersession chain is resolved. Beginning from the requested part with a hop count of zero, the server repeatedly follows `superseded_by_part_id` for as long as the part it is looking at is `OBSOLETE` and has a successor recorded, counting one hop each time it moves. The part the walk lands on becomes the order's `effective_part_id` and the number of hops taken becomes `substitution_hops`, and the three refusals that can follow are then applied against that landing place in the fixed §2.7 order rather than in the order the walk discovered them. Scope goes first, and it is checked against the part the walk landed on, not the one the contractor named: on an `OIL_AND_GREASE` contract, an effective part whose `part_class` is anything other than `LUBRICATION` is outside the covered scope and the order is refused with `ERR_SCOPE_EXCLUDES_REPAIR`. Next, if the part the walk lands on is `DISCONTINUED` the order is refused with `ERR_PART_DISCONTINUED`. Last, if the walk would take more than **three** hops the order is refused with `ERR_SUPERSESSION_CHAIN_TOO_DEEP` — so a chain that is both too deep and out of scope reports the scope failure. A `FULL_MAINTENANCE` contract may order any class.

With the effective part fixed, the six pricing and promising steps run in this order and no other. Step one computes `subtotal_cents` as the effective part's `unit_price_cents` multiplied by `quantity`. Step two selects a freight tier **keyed on that subtotal alone** — never on the eventual total — where a subtotal below 100,000 cents is the small tier, a subtotal from 100,000 cents up to but not including 500,000 cents is the medium tier, and a subtotal of 500,000 cents or more is the large tier. Step three computes `expedite_surcharge_cents`: when `expedite` is true it is the subtotal multiplied by the tier's rate — 20 percent in the small tier, 15 percent in the medium tier, 10 percent in the large tier — rounded half up to a whole cent; when `expedite` is false it is 0. Step four computes `total_cost_cents` as `subtotal_cents` plus `expedite_surcharge_cents`. Step five computes `lead_time_days_applied`: take the effective part's `lead_time_days`, subtract **seven days** if `expedite` is true, add **ten days for every hop** recorded in `substitution_hops`, and then take whichever is larger — that figure or a floor of **three days**. Step six computes `promised_date` as the calendar date of the current logical clock advanced by `lead_time_days_applied` whole days.

Because the tier is keyed on the subtotal, an order whose subtotal sits just under 500,000 cents draws the medium tier's 15 percent surcharge even though the surcharge pushes its `total_cost_cents` well past 500,000; the large tier's 10 percent rate does not apply retroactively.

Whether the order is created in `ORDERED` or in `SUBSTITUTED` depends on the same walk. An order whose requested part was `ACTIVE` has `substitution_hops` 0, an `effective_part_id` equal to its `part_id`, no substitution penalty in its lead time, is created in `ORDERED`, and fires no window cascade at all. An order whose requested part was `OBSOLETE` with a successor has an `effective_part_id` different from its `part_id`, takes the successor's price and lead time in place of the requested part's, carries a `substitution_hops` of at least 1 with ten days of penalty per hop, is created in `SUBSTITUTED`, and does fire the window cascade. Either way, creating the order increments the car's `open_part_order_count` by 1 and increments the car's `version` by 1.

When the order carries a `window_id` **and** its `substitution_hops` is at least 1, the lead-time cascade runs against that one window; an order that resolved to zero hops never touches a window however distant its promise. If the order's `promised_date` is on or before the calendar date of the window's `window_start`, the part arrives in time and nothing moves — `reschedule_count` is left alone. Otherwise the shortfall is the whole number of days from the window's start date forward to `promised_date`, and the candidate placement is the window's `window_start` and `window_end` each advanced by that identical whole-day offset, which preserves `duration_minutes` exactly. That candidate is then tested against the same per-car interval rule of §3.5, consulting the car's other windows in `SCHEDULED` or `COMPLETED`. If the shifted placement would collide, the shift is refused rather than forced: `window_start` and `window_end` stay exactly where they were, the window's `status` becomes `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED`, its `reschedule_count` is incremented by 1, its `duration_minutes` drops out of the car's `scheduled_downtime_minutes` because a window awaiting rescheduling no longer occupies the car, and the car's `version` is incremented by 1. If the shifted placement is clear, `window_start` and `window_end` take the new values, the window stays `SCHEDULED`, and `reschedule_count` is still incremented by 1. Note that a successful shift leaves `scheduled_downtime_minutes` untouched, since the duration is unchanged.

The cascade then propagates upward, but only when the shifted window carries a `project_id`. That project's `target_completion_date` is recomputed as the latest `window_end` date across its windows in `SCHEDULED`, `COMPLETED` or `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED`, and if the recomputed date is later than the governing contract's `term_end_date` while the project is in `APPROVED` or `IN_PROGRESS`, the project is flagged `AT_RISK`. That is the full three-hop chain: an order against an obsolete part lengthens the promise, the lengthened promise moves or freezes a window, and the moved window pushes a project past the end of the contract that pays for it.

Receipt closes the order out. A `ServiceContractor` records it against an order in `ORDERED` or in `SUBSTITUTED`, driving it to `RECEIVED`, decrementing the car's `open_part_order_count` by 1 and incrementing the car's `version` by 1. `RECEIVED` and `CANCELLED` are terminal; cancellation is never requested directly and arrives only through the car-decommissioning cascade of §3.1, which sweeps every `ORDERED` or `SUBSTITUTED` order on that car to `CANCELLED` and decrements `open_part_order_count` accordingly.

### 3.7. Measuring Uptime, Car by Car

Uptime is the number the whole program is judged by, so its definition is spelled out here in full and every other calculation in this document refers back to it. Uptime is always computed for one car and one `period_month`. The period runs from the first instant of that month at 00:00:00.000 UTC, inclusive, up to the first instant of the following month at 00:00:00.000 UTC, exclusive; `period_minutes` is the count of whole minutes in that half-open span — 1,440 minutes for each whole day the month contains.

Against that period the server assembles two families of occupation intervals, every one of them first clipped to the period so that nothing outside the month can contribute.

The **unplanned** family has three sources. Each cleared callback on the car that was reported with `out_of_service` true contributes the span from its `reported_at` up to but not including its `cleared_at`. Each callback on the car that was reported with `out_of_service` true and is *still* open — that is, in `REPORTED`, `ESCALATED` or `ON_SITE` — contributes the span from its `reported_at` running to the end of the period, on the reasoning that a car nobody has cleared is still down. Each completed inspection on the car whose `result` was `FAIL` contributes the span from its `result_recorded_at` up to the car's `red_tag_lifted_at`, or to the end of the period when no red tag has yet been lifted. Callbacks in `CANCELLED` contribute nothing at all. Those intervals are then **union-merged** — sorted by interval start ascending and, for equal starts, by interval end ascending, then coalesced so that a minute covered by two of them is counted exactly once — and the total merged length in whole minutes is `unplanned_outage_minutes`.

The **planned** family has one source: every downtime window on the car whose `status` is `SCHEDULED` or `COMPLETED` contributes the span from its `window_start` up to but not including its `window_end`. Windows in `CANCELLED` or `RESCHEDULE_REQUIRED` contribute nothing. These are union-merged the same way, and then — this is the step that keeps the arithmetic honest — the portion of the merged planned total that intersects the merged unplanned total is subtracted out. A minute is never counted as both planned and unplanned; when the two families overlap, the minute belongs to the unplanned side. What remains is `planned_downtime_minutes`.

`available_minutes` is then `period_minutes` less `planned_downtime_minutes` less `unplanned_outage_minutes`, and from it the two uptime variants are derived. The **exclusive** variant, reported as `uptime_pct_exclusive`, divides `available_minutes` by `period_minutes` reduced by `planned_downtime_minutes` and multiplies by 100; because scheduled downtime is removed from the denominator, a planned modernization outage never depresses this number. The **inclusive** variant, reported as `uptime_pct_inclusive`, divides `available_minutes` by the full `period_minutes` and multiplies by 100; here planned outages depress uptime exactly as unplanned ones do. Either variant reports exactly 100.00 without dividing whenever its own denominator comes out at zero or less. Both are rounded half up to 2 decimal places. Which variant carries commercial weight is decided by contract scope: a `FULL_MAINTENANCE` contract is measured on the exclusive variant, an `OIL_AND_GREASE` contract on the inclusive one.

Any of the five roles may pull the report for one car by supplying the `period_month` it wants as a query parameter; that parameter is mandatory, and an absent or malformed one is refused with `ERR_VALIDATION`. The report always returns **both** variants side by side regardless of scope, together with `car_id`, `period_month`, `period_minutes`, `unplanned_outage_minutes`, `planned_downtime_minutes`, `available_minutes`, and `outage_interval_count` — the number of merged unplanned intervals that produced `unplanned_outage_minutes`. A car that has been `DECOMMISSIONED` still answers this per-car report, but it is excluded from every contract-level roll-up built on top of it.

### 3.8. Assessing the Month: Penalties, Credits and Disputes

Once a billing month is behind the program, a `ProgramAdministrator` closes the books on it by assessing that month. The assessment names the `contract_id`, the `period_month` being assessed, and the `expected_contract_version` that §2.8 requires. It can only be run on a **closed** month — the logical clock must already have reached or passed the first instant of the month following `period_month`, or the request is refused with `ERR_PENALTY_PERIOD_NOT_CLOSED` — and only on a contract that still has at least one car whose `availability_state` is not `DECOMMISSIONED`, or the request is refused with `ERR_NO_CARS_IN_SCOPE`.

The assessment first fixes the ground it will stand on. `schedule_applied` is a copy of the contract's `penalty_schedule` taken at this moment, so that a later renegotiation cannot rewrite history. `uptime_variant_applied` is `EXCLUSIVE` when the contract's `scope_type` is `FULL_MAINTENANCE` and `INCLUSIVE` when it is `OIL_AND_GREASE`. The car set is every car on the contract whose `availability_state` is not `DECOMMISSIONED`, walked in `car_code` ascending order. `uptime_pct_applied` is the arithmetic mean of the per-car uptime figures for the period under the applied variant — each of those per-car figures already rounded to 2 decimal places by §3.7 — with the mean itself then rounded half up to 2 decimal places. The shortfall in percentage points is the contracted uptime target of **98.50 percent** less `uptime_pct_applied`, floored at zero: a contract that beat its target has a shortfall of exactly 0.00, never a negative number.

Three breach counters are then tallied over the period, each with an explicit status filter. `late_callback_count` counts callbacks on the car set whose `status` is `CLEARED`, whose `callback_type` is *not* `ENTRAPMENT`, whose `sla_breached` is true, and whose `reported_at` falls inside the half-open period window. `entrapment_breach_count` applies the identical filter except that `callback_type` must be `ENTRAPMENT`. `missed_visit_count` counts maintenance visits on the car set whose `period_month` string equals the assessed period exactly and whose `status` is either `MISSED` or `REJECTED`. Callbacks still open, callbacks cancelled, and visits in any other status are all outside the count.

From here the two schedules diverge completely, and for identical incident data they produce materially different numbers.

Under **`SCHEDULE_A`** the penalty is a sum of flat per-incident fees plus a linear charge for the uptime shortfall. `raw_amount_cents` is 25,000 cents for each late callback, plus 100,000 cents for each entrapment breach, plus 75,000 cents for each missed or rejected visit, plus the shortfall in percentage points multiplied by 40,000 cents per percentage point and rounded half up to a whole cent. `cap_amount_cents` is 25 percent of the contract's `monthly_base_fee_cents`, rounded half up to a whole cent. This schedule is insensitive to how bad any single breach was and sensitive only to how many there were.

Under **`SCHEDULE_B`** the penalty is a percentage of the monthly fee, built from three factors. The tiered uptime rate is 0 when `uptime_pct_applied` is 98.50 or above, 5 percent when it is 97.00 or above but below 98.50, 10 percent when it is 95.00 or above but below 97.00, and 20 percent when it is below 95.00. The breach rate is 2 percent multiplied by a weighted incident count in which each late callback counts once, each entrapment breach counts **twice**, and each missed or rejected visit counts once. The severity weight is 3 whenever `entrapment_breach_count` is greater than zero; otherwise it is 2 whenever the shortfall in percentage points is at least 2.00; otherwise it is 1. `raw_amount_cents` is then the contract's `monthly_base_fee_cents` multiplied by the sum of the tiered uptime rate and the breach rate, multiplied by the severity weight, with the whole product rounded half up to a whole cent exactly once. `cap_amount_cents` is 30 percent of `monthly_base_fee_cents`, rounded half up to a whole cent.

Whichever schedule ran, `amount_cents` is the smaller of `raw_amount_cents` and `cap_amount_cents`.

Alongside the penalty the same operation computes the month's service credit, and its rounding order is pinned because the alternative order gives a different total. Let the car count be the number of cars in the same non-decommissioned car set. For each car in `car_code` ascending order, that car's raw credit is the contract's `monthly_base_fee_cents`, multiplied by one divided by the car count — kept as an exact fraction and never pre-rounded — multiplied by 100 less that car's own uptime percentage under the applied variant, divided by 100. That raw figure is rounded half up to a whole cent **per car**, and only then are the per-car figures summed into `service_credit_cents`. Rounding once per car before summation, rather than summing exact fractions and rounding once at the end, is the required order; the two orders differ, and for three cars on a 500,000-cent fee each running 99.14 percent uptime the pinned order yields 1,433 plus 1,433 plus 1,433, or 4,299 cents, where the alternative would yield 4,300.

The created assessment lands in `status` `ISSUED`, recording `assessed_by_user_id` from the acting `X-User-ID`. Re-assessing a period is explicitly supported: when an assessment for the same `contract_id` and `period_month` already exists in `ISSUED`, that prior assessment is driven to `SUPERSEDED` and its `amount_cents` is subtracted back out of the contract's `penalty_lifetime_assessed_cents`, so assessments for one period supersede rather than accumulate. The new `amount_cents` is then added to `penalty_lifetime_assessed_cents`, `penalty_balance_cents` is recomputed, and the contract's `version` is incremented by 1.

The contract's ledger is a conserved quantity with a single defining relation: `penalty_balance_cents` always equals `penalty_lifetime_assessed_cents` less `penalty_lifetime_waived_cents` less `penalty_lifetime_credited_cents`. The lifetime assessed figure is the running total of `amount_cents` across the contract's assessments that are not `SUPERSEDED`.

A `BuildingManager` who disagrees disputes the assessment, driving `ISSUED → DISPUTED` and recording a `dispute_reason`. Disputing changes no money: `penalty_balance_cents` is left exactly as it was while the argument is had. Resolution is then a `ProgramAdministrator`'s decision, and it must be a *different* administrator than the one recorded in `assessed_by_user_id`. The resolver supplies a `resolution` of `UPHELD`, `WAIVED` or `CREDITED` together with an optional `resolution_note`, and `resolved_by_user_id` is stamped from the acting `X-User-ID`. All three branches move the assessment to a terminal state and increment the contract's `version` by 1, and all three record the note; what differs is the ledger. `DISPUTED → UPHELD` leaves `penalty_balance_cents` unchanged — the penalty stands. `DISPUTED → WAIVED` adds `amount_cents` to `penalty_lifetime_waived_cents` and recomputes `penalty_balance_cents` from the defining relation, restoring the pre-assessment balance exactly. `DISPUTED → CREDITED` adds `amount_cents` to `penalty_lifetime_credited_cents` and recomputes `penalty_balance_cents` the same way; the money lands in a different bucket, but the balance effect is identical. `UPHELD`, `WAIVED`, `CREDITED` and `SUPERSEDED` are all terminal.

### 3.9. Performance Scoring and the Renegotiation Cycle

A renegotiation is not something either party may demand at will. It is available only when the contract's measured performance has fallen below a stated threshold, and that measurement is made by the server at the moment the renegotiation is opened.

The score looks at exactly one month of history: the most recent calendar month that lies entirely in the past relative to the logical clock — that is, the month before the clock's current month. Over that month, and restricted to the contract's cars whose `availability_state` is not `DECOMMISSIONED` in `car_code` ascending order, three component percentages are computed. The **uptime component** is the same contract-level mean uptime described in §3.8 under the same variant selected by `scope_type`, except that a contract with no non-decommissioned cars scores 100.00 rather than failing. The **response component** is 100.00 when no callback on those cars reached `CLEARED` with a `reported_at` inside the scoring month; otherwise it is 100 multiplied by the number of those cleared callbacks whose `sla_breached` is false, divided by the total number of them, rounded half up to 2 decimal places. The **visit component** is 100.00 when no maintenance visit on those cars carries that scoring month as its `period_month` in one of the statuses `VERIFIED`, `DISPUTED`, `REJECTED`, `MISSED` or `PERFORMED`; otherwise it is 100 multiplied by the number of those visits whose `status` is `VERIFIED`, divided by the total number of them, rounded half up to 2 decimal places. Visits still `SCHEDULED` and callbacks that were `CANCELLED` are excluded from both the numerators and the denominators.

A fourth term drags the score down in proportion to money owed. The penalty drag in points is the contract's `penalty_balance_cents` divided by 100,000 — one point of drag per 1,000 dollars of outstanding balance — rounded half up to 2 decimal places, and then capped at **15.00 points** however large the balance grows.

The raw score is then the uptime component weighted at 0.50, plus the response component weighted at 0.25, plus the visit component weighted at 0.25, less the penalty drag. That raw figure is rounded half up to 2 decimal places exactly once, and the result is then clamped into the range from 0.00 to 100.00 inclusive. The clamped value is stored on the contract as `performance_score`, which stays null until the first evaluation ever runs.

Opening a renegotiation is the `BuildingManager`'s prerogative. The proposal names the `contract_id` and carries the three terms up for negotiation: `proposed_monthly_fee_cents` of at least 1 cent, `proposed_callback_response_minutes` of at least 1 minute, and a `proposed_scope_type` of either `FULL_MAINTENANCE` or `OIL_AND_GREASE`. The contract must be exactly `ACTIVE`, and its `renegotiation_round_count` must still be below **four**, or the request is refused with `ERR_RENEGOTIATION_ROUND_LIMIT` — a check that runs before the score is evaluated at all. The score is then evaluated, and the request proceeds only if it is **strictly below 75.00**; a score of exactly 75.00 is not low enough and the request is refused with `ERR_RENEGOTIATION_NOT_WARRANTED`, and on that refusal nothing is written, so the contract's stored `performance_score` is left exactly as it was. Only a request that clears that gate stores the freshly evaluated score on the contract. When it succeeds, a round is created in `status` `OPEN` with `opened_by_user_id` stamped from the acting `X-User-ID` and `trigger_performance_score` set to the score just evaluated; the contract's `renegotiation_round_count` is incremented by 1 and the round's `round_number` is that resulting count, so the first round of a contract's life is round 1; the contract moves `ACTIVE → UNDER_RENEGOTIATION`; and the contract's `version` is incremented by 1.

Countering is what makes this a cycle. Either a `ServiceContractor` or a `BuildingManager` may counter an `OPEN` round, provided the contract is `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION`, the contract's `renegotiation_round_count` is still below four, and the acting `X-User-ID` differs from that round's `opened_by_user_id`. The countered round moves `OPEN → COUNTERED` with `countered_by_user_id` stamped, and in the same operation a **successor round is created** in `OPEN`, carrying the counterparty's own `proposed_monthly_fee_cents`, `proposed_callback_response_minutes` and `proposed_scope_type`, with `opened_by_user_id` set to the countering user, with `trigger_performance_score` copied unchanged from the round being countered rather than re-evaluated, and with `round_number` equal to the contract's `renegotiation_round_count` after that count is incremented by 1. The response body is that newly created successor round rather than the round just countered. The contract's `version` is incremented by 1. Because each counter both closes one round and opens another, the negotiation loops — open, countered, open again — until somebody accepts, somebody closes it out, or the fourth round is reached and no further round may be appended.

Acceptance ends the negotiation on terms. Either party may accept an `OPEN` round while the contract is `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION`, provided the acting `X-User-ID` differs from that round's `opened_by_user_id` — a party cannot accept its own proposal. The round moves `OPEN → ACCEPTED` with `accepted_by_user_id` stamped, and the contract is rewritten from the proposal: `monthly_base_fee_cents` takes `proposed_monthly_fee_cents`, `callback_response_minutes` takes `proposed_callback_response_minutes`, and `scope_type` takes `proposed_scope_type`. Every *other* round on the contract still sitting in `OPEN` is driven to `WITHDRAWN`, the contract returns `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION → ACTIVE`, and the contract's `version` is incremented by 1. Note what the rewrite does *not* touch: `entrapment_response_minutes` and `penalty_schedule` are not negotiable terms in this system, and assessments already issued keep the `schedule_applied` they recorded.

Closing ends the negotiation without terms. The closing request carries an `outcome` of either `REJECTED` or `WITHDRAWN`, and which one is appropriate depends on who is acting: `REJECTED` is the counterparty turning the proposal down, and is used when the acting `X-User-ID` differs from the round's `opened_by_user_id`, while `WITHDRAWN` is the opener taking its own proposal off the table, and is used when the acting `X-User-ID` is that same `opened_by_user_id`. That correspondence is a convention the caller keeps, not a rule the server checks: closing carries no segregation-of-duties test, and either party may close an `OPEN` round with either `outcome`. Either way the round moves from `OPEN` to the corresponding terminal state, and the contract returns `UNDER_RENEGOTIATION → ACTIVE` with its `version` incremented by 1 once no round on the contract remains in `OPEN`. `ACCEPTED`, `REJECTED` and `WITHDRAWN` are the three terminal round states.
