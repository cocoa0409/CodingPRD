# Wholesale Auction Arbitration API — Product Requirements Document

## 1. Introduction

A wholesale vehicle auction lives or dies on the credibility of what is said at the block. When a unit rolls across the ramp under a green, yellow or red light, the announcements the block clerk reads — frame damage, a branded or missing title, an odometer that does not agree with the paperwork, a knock in the driveline — are the contract between two dealers who will never meet. The arbitration department exists for the cases where that contract fails: the buying dealer discovers a defect that was never announced, and someone has to decide, quickly and defensibly, whether the sale stands, whether the seller writes a repair allowance, or whether the whole transaction is unwound and the unit goes home.

Today that adjudication is carried out on spreadsheets and email threads, and it does not survive scrutiny. Arbitration windows differ by defect family — a mechanical complaint expires in hours while a title problem never expires at all — and staff cannot reliably say whether a claim arrived in time, whether the buyer drove too far to still have rights, or whether a post-sale inspection extended the window or quietly waived it. Repair-allowance haggling has no round limit and no cool-off discipline. Transportation invoices are absorbed by whoever shouts loudest. Sellers who lose arbitration after arbitration face no consequence until a regional manager notices, and buyers who file baseless claims face none at all.

This document specifies the service that replaces those spreadsheets. It is mounted under `/arbitration` and it owns the full dispute lifecycle: the registry of consignors, buying dealers and published arbitration policy; the sale record and its block announcements; the paid post-sale inspection programme; claim intake with its window, mileage, announcement and inspection gauntlet; evidence filing and arbitrator intake review; seller response and the repair-allowance negotiation cycle; transportation cost allocation; the four terminal arbitration outcomes; and the consequence machinery that tracks seller arbitration rates, moves sellers between policy tiers, holds their settlement money, and assesses escalating fees against buyers who abuse the process. Every temporal rule in the service reads a server-controlled logical clock rather than wall time, so that a full dispute spanning weeks can be driven deterministically in a single test session.

## 2. Global Specifications & System-Wide Principles

Everything in this section applies to every route in the service unless a later section names an explicit exception. Where §2 and §3 discuss the same rule, they are reinforcing each other, not competing: §2 fixes the mechanical contract, §3 supplies the business reason.

### 2.1. User and Role-Based Access Control

Authentication is header-based. Every request carries two headers: `X-User-Role`, naming the caller's role, and `X-User-ID`, naming the individual actor. Both are mandatory on every route including reads. If `X-User-Role` is absent or blank, the request fails with `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED` (HTTP 401); the same code and status apply when `X-User-ID` is absent or blank. A header whose value consists only of whitespace counts as blank. The role header is inspected before the identity header, so a request missing both is reported against the role header. Beyond that presence test neither header value is ever trimmed or case-folded: the role must match one of the six spellings below exactly, so a value of `AuctionAdmin ` with a trailing space is present and non-blank and therefore reaches the authorisation check, which rejects it with `ERR_UNAUTHORIZED`; and `X-User-ID` is likewise compared verbatim, whitespace included, in every ownership check. Once both headers are present, the service checks the supplied role against the roles authorised for the requested operation in §4; a role that is not listed there is rejected with `ERR_UNAUTHORIZED` (HTTP 403). Both of these checks precede request-body parsing, so a malformed payload sent by an unauthenticated or unauthorised caller never produces a schema error.

Six roles exist, spelled exactly as follows.

- **`AuctionAdmin`** — auction house operations. Registers sellers and buyers, publishes arbitration policy, releases sale proceeds, records transportation legs, reinstates blocked sellers, and drives the logical clock and the deadline sweep.
- **`BlockClerk`** — sale-block staff. Records units as they cross the block and the condition announcements read on them.
- **`Arbitrator`** — arbitration department officer. Opens intake review, escalates exhausted negotiations, and issues decisions. A segregation-of-duties rule bars the officer who opened a review from deciding the same case.
- **`Inspector`** — third-party post-sale inspection technician. Files inspection findings and may attach evidence to a case.
- **`BuyerRep`** — the buying dealer's representative. Purchases post-sale inspections, files and evidences claims, counters and accepts allowance offers, withdraws claims, and pays down frivolous-claim fees.
- **`SellerRep`** — the consigning seller's representative. Answers a claim with an allowance offer, a dispute, or a conceded unwind.

Identity semantics differ by role, and they matter because ownership checks compare `X-User-ID` against a stored foreign key rather than against a session.

When `X-User-Role` is `BuyerRep`, `X-User-ID` is the `id` of the acting `Buyer`; ownership checks on any resource that references a `Buyer` compare that resource's buyer foreign key to `X-User-ID`. Concretely, filing a claim or purchasing an inspection on a sale requires `X-User-ID` to equal that sale's `buyer_id`, failing with `ERR_NOT_SALE_BUYER` (HTTP 403); acting on an existing case requires `X-User-ID` to equal the case's `claimant_buyer_id`, failing with `ERR_NOT_CASE_OWNER` (HTTP 403).

When `X-User-Role` is `SellerRep`, `X-User-ID` is the `id` of the acting `Seller`; ownership checks on any resource that references a `Seller` compare that resource's seller foreign key to `X-User-ID`. Answering a claim therefore requires `X-User-ID` to equal the `seller_id` of the disputed sale, failing with `ERR_NOT_SALE_SELLER` (HTTP 403).

When `X-User-Role` is `AuctionAdmin`, `BlockClerk`, `Arbitrator` or `Inspector`, `X-User-ID` is an opaque operator identifier not tied to any persisted entity. The server may record it — an `Arbitrator` identifier is stored as `intake_arbitrator_user_id` when a review is opened and as `decided_by_arbitrator_user_id` when a decision is rendered, and an `Inspector` or `BuyerRep` identifier is stored as `submitted_by_user_id` alongside `submitted_by_role` on each evidence row — but it is never compared against any entity's `id`. The one comparison the service does make between two opaque identifiers is the segregation-of-duties test: the `Arbitrator` rendering a decision must present an `X-User-ID` different from the case's stored `intake_arbitrator_user_id`, otherwise the decision is refused with `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES` (HTTP 403).

Read access is uniformly broad: every retrieval route in the service admits all six roles. Write access is narrow and is enumerated per operation in §4.

### 2.2. API Response Structure

#### Standard Success Response Format

All successful API responses (those with 2xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format in their JSON body:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z",
    "limit": 10,
    "offset": 0,
    "total_count": 100,
    "has_more": true
  },
  "response_type": "object" | "array",
  "data": { }
}
```

- The `api_request_id` is a server-generated unique identifier for the request that starts with `req_`.
- The `api_request_timestamp` is a server-generated ISO 8601 UTC timestamp with three fractional digits. It is read from the host's real wall clock rather than from the logical clock of §2.4, so it advances between two requests served at the same logical instant and bears no relation to the logical-clock stamps carried on the resources in `data`.
- The `response_type` is either "object" for a single resource or "array" for a list of resources.
- The `data` field contains the resource object or an array of resource objects. For 2xx responses with no data, it contains an empty object. Unless otherwise noted, operations that create or modify a resource will return the created or updated resource object in the data field.
- Pagination-related fields in `meta` (`limit`, `offset`, `total_count`, `has_more`) are included for list endpoints.

#### Standard Error Response Format

All error API responses (4xx status codes) follow this standard envelope format:

```json
{
  "meta": {
    "api_request_id": "req_RANDOMALPHANUM123456",
    "api_request_timestamp": "2025-05-24T10:30:00.123Z"
  },
  "response_type": "error",
  "data": {
    "error_id": "UNIQUE_ERROR_IDENTIFIER_STRING",
    "message": "A human-readable explanation of the error."
  }
}
```

- The `meta` object for error responses contains only `api_request_id` and `api_request_timestamp`.
- The `error_id` is a unique, machine-readable string that identifies the specific error.
- The `message` provides a human-readable explanation of what went wrong.

### 2.3. Pagination

Two retrieval routes are paginated: the sale list and the arbitration-case list. Both accept a `limit` and an `offset` query parameter. `limit` defaults to 10 when omitted, may not be smaller than 1, and may not be larger than 100. `offset` defaults to 0 and may not be negative. A value outside those bounds is a schema failure and is rejected with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST` (HTTP 400), and so is a value that is not a base-10 integer at all — `limit=abc` and `limit=2.5` are both rejected the same way, while surrounding whitespace is tolerated.

Ordering is fixed and is not client-controllable: every list route in the service returns rows in ascending `created_at` order. Paginated responses carry `response_type` of `"array"`, the rows themselves in `data`, and four pagination fields inside `meta` alongside `api_request_id` and `api_request_timestamp`: `limit`, `offset`, `total_count` and `has_more`. `total_count` counts the rows that match the supplied filters *before* `limit` and `offset` are applied, so a caller can tell how deep the result set runs. `has_more` is true when the sum of `offset` and the number of rows actually returned in `data` is strictly less than `total_count`, and false otherwise.

Every other list route in the service — announcements on a sale, inspections on a sale, ledger entries on a sale or a case, evidence on a case, negotiation rounds on a case, the decision on a case, frivolous fees on a case or a buyer, and policy actions on a case or a seller — accepts no `limit` or `offset` and is unpaginated. Those routes return every matching row in one response and still populate `meta`: `total_count` equals the number of rows returned, `has_more` is false, `limit` is null and `offset` is 0.

### 2.4. The Logical Clock

Every deadline, window, cooldown and rolling-window computation in this service reads a single server-owned **logical clock** rather than the host's wall-clock time. Before any caller sets it, the logical clock reads exactly its startup default of `2026-08-01T00:00:00Z`, and it never falls back to real time under any circumstance. An `AuctionAdmin` moves it with a clock-setting capability that takes a single `to_iso8601` instant and answers with the object `{ current_utc }`. The clock is monotonic: the requested instant must be at or after the clock's current value, and an earlier instant is refused with `ERR_CLOCK_NOT_MONOTONIC` (HTTP 409). Setting the clock to exactly its current value is permitted and is a no-op.

Whenever this document says "now", "the current logical clock", or describes a timestamp as being recorded at the moment of an action, it means the logical clock's value at the instant the request is served. All datetimes crossing the API — those supplied by clients and those minted by the server — are ISO 8601 UTC strings with exactly three fractional-second digits. On the way in a client instant is accepted in any ISO 8601 form the parser recognises: a trailing `Z`, an explicit numeric UTC offset such as `+02:00`, or no zone marker at all, which is read as UTC. Whatever arrives is converted to UTC and re-serialised into exactly that three-digit form before it is stored or echoed, so the value a client reads back is often not byte-identical to the one it sent — the clock's own startup default, for instance, reads as `2026-08-01T00:00:00.000Z`.

### 2.5. Record Identity, Timestamps and Versioning

Every resource carries an `id` that is a server-minted UUID. Identifiers are bare UUIDs with no type prefix, and clients never supply them. Every resource also carries a `created_at`, an ISO 8601 UTC instant with three fractional digits recording when the row was inserted; `created_at` is the sort key for every list route and the tie-breaker for several "most recent row" selectors described in §3. `created_at`, and equally the `updated_at` of the mutable types below, is the single deliberate exception to §2.4: both are taken from the host's real wall clock at the instant of the write rather than from the logical clock, so they always run forward across a session however the logical clock has been driven, and they never coincide with the logical-clock stamps (`announced_at_utc`, `claimed_at_utc`, `posted_at_utc`, `settled_at_utc` and their kin) written by the very same request. Because wall-clock millisecond resolution admits ties, two rows sharing a `created_at` value are ordered by their insertion sequence — in list routes and equally in every selector that ranks on `created_at`, so the later-inserted row is the more recent one.

Seven of the fourteen resource types are mutable and carry two further common properties. `Seller`, `Buyer`, `ArbitrationPolicy`, `AuctionSale`, `PostSaleInspection`, `ArbitrationCase` and `NegotiationRound` each expose an `updated_at` (same format as `created_at`, refreshed on every mutation) and a `version`, an integer that starts at 1 when the row is created and increases by exactly 1 on every mutation of that row — including mutations caused indirectly by a cascade from some other resource, not merely those requested directly against it.

The remaining seven resource types are append-only records of something that already happened, and they carry only `id` and `created_at`. `SaleAnnouncement`, `ClaimEvidence`, `TransportLeg`, `ArbitrationDecision`, `SettlementLedgerEntry`, `FrivolousFeeAssessment` and `SellerPolicyAction` have **no** `updated_at` and **no** `version` field: those two properties are absent from their payloads entirely. Nothing in the service ever edits or deletes one of these rows once written.

Two of the append-only types do, however, record the version of the parent they were written against. A `ClaimEvidence` row and a `TransportLeg` row each store `case_version_after`, the `ArbitrationCase.version` value that resulted from appending that row. Because appending either row bumps the case by one, `case_version_after` is never lower than 2, and on a `TransportLeg` it is precisely the value a subsequent leg's stale-version check must carry.

### 2.6. Stale-Version Guards on Case and Buyer Writes

Two write operations are guarded by optimistic concurrency control, because both are legitimately repeatable and therefore cannot be screened out by a state precondition.

Recording a transportation leg against a case requires a non-optional `expected_case_version` field in the request payload, carrying the `version` of the target `ArbitrationCase` as the client last observed it. If that value does not equal the case's current `version`, the request is rejected with `ERR_STALE_CASE_VERSION` (HTTP 412) and no leg is written. A case admits any number of legs, so a second leg is perfectly valid on its own terms; the stale-version check is what stops a client from computing an allocation basis against a case whose negotiation state or leg set has moved on since it read it.

Paying down a buyer's frivolous-fee balance requires a non-optional `expected_buyer_version` field carrying the `version` of the target `Buyer` as the client last observed it. A mismatch with the buyer's current `version` is rejected with `ERR_STALE_BUYER_VERSION` (HTTP 412) and the balance is untouched. Partial payments are legitimate, so a second payment observing the same balance would otherwise succeed; the version check is what makes the outcome deterministic once an unwind fee offset or a fresh assessment has already moved the balance.

No other operation in the service accepts a version field. Requests to any endpoint outside these two must not be required to carry one, and supplying one where it is not defined is simply an unknown key.

### 2.7. Money, Basis Points and Rounding

All monetary amounts are integers denominated in cents, and every field carrying money is named with a `_cents` suffix. No monetary value is ever expressed as a decimal number of dollars across the API.

Proportional quantities — allowance ceilings, arbitration rates, transport allocation shares — are expressed in **basis points**, where 10000 basis points is the whole. Converting a basis-point proportion into cents means multiplying the base amount by the basis-point figure, dividing by 10000, and rounding the result **half up** to a whole number of cents. Half-up rounding is the single rounding rule used everywhere in this service: a value exactly on the halfway boundary rounds away from zero to the larger magnitude.

Two rounding disciplines are worth stating up front because they are easy to get wrong. First, where a formula applies more than one proportional step, each step is rounded before the next one is applied, and any bounding comparison happens after the roundings, never before. Second, where an amount is allocated across a collection of rows, each row's share is rounded individually and the rounded shares are then summed; summing the raw amounts and rounding once at the end is a defect, and the two methods give different answers.

Exactly one quantity in the service is not an integer: `hours_elapsed_at_claim` is a decimal number of hours, rounded half up to three decimal places.

### 2.8. Path Resolution and Rejection Precedence

Path parameters are resolved strictly left to right. On a route addressing a negotiation round, the case identifier in the path is resolved first and a missing case yields `ERR_CASE_NOT_FOUND` (HTTP 404); only then is the round identifier resolved, and it must belong to that case — a round that exists but whose `case_id` points at a different case is reported as `ERR_ROUND_NOT_FOUND` (HTTP 404), exactly as if it did not exist. The same left-to-right discipline applies to sub-resource routes hanging off a sale, a seller, a buyer or an inspection.

When a single request violates more than one rule, the response is fully determined by a fixed global precedence. Checks are evaluated in the order below and the **first** failure is the one reported; nothing later can mask or override it.

1. `X-User-Role` present and non-blank — else `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED`.
2. `X-User-ID` present and non-blank — else `ERR_UNAUTHENTICATED`.
3. The acting role is authorised for the operation — else `ERR_UNAUTHORIZED`.
4. The body parses; every required input field is present and non-null; every enum-typed value is a declared member of its enum; integer fields hold integers — a JSON boolean is not an integer, and a boolean-typed field accepts nothing but `true` or `false`; cents-denominated fields are not negative; datetime fields parse as ISO 8601 instants — else `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`. The "present and non-null" rule binds only *required* fields: an optional field supplied explicitly as `null` is indistinguishable from an omitted one and simply takes its declared default, so a `seller_response_hours` of `null` stores 24 rather than null. Keys the operation does not define are ignored wherever they appear. This is also where a supplied `superseded_case_id` whose sale or defect class disagrees with the new claim is rejected, and where a sale's `hammer_time_utc` later than the current logical clock is rejected.
5. A sale identifier in the path resolves to an `AuctionSale` — else `ERR_SALE_NOT_FOUND`. This guards announcement recording, announcement listing, settlement, sale reads, the sale's inspection list and the sale's ledger-entry list.
6. A case identifier in the path resolves to an `ArbitrationCase` — else `ERR_CASE_NOT_FOUND`. This guards every read and write route addressed at a specific case.
7. A round identifier in the path resolves to a `NegotiationRound` whose `case_id` equals the path's case — else `ERR_ROUND_NOT_FOUND`. This guards countering and accepting.
8. A seller identifier in the path resolves to a `Seller` — else `ERR_SELLER_NOT_FOUND`. This guards reinstatement, seller reads, the seller's policy-action list and the seller's scorecard.
9. A buyer identifier in the path resolves to a `Buyer` — else `ERR_BUYER_NOT_FOUND`. This guards fee payment, buyer reads and the buyer's frivolous-fee list.
10. An inspection identifier in the path resolves to a `PostSaleInspection` — else `ERR_INSPECTION_NOT_FOUND`. This guards inspection completion. A policy identifier in the path that resolves to no `ArbitrationPolicy` is likewise reported as `ERR_POLICY_NOT_FOUND` (HTTP 404).
11. A `seller_id` supplied in a sale-recording body resolves to a `Seller` — else `ERR_SELLER_NOT_FOUND`.
12. A `buyer_id` supplied in a sale-recording body resolves to a `Buyer` — else `ERR_BUYER_NOT_FOUND`.
13. A `sale_id` supplied in a claim-intake or inspection-purchase body resolves to an `AuctionSale` — else `ERR_SALE_NOT_FOUND`.
14. Exactly one `ArbitrationPolicy` exists whose `defect_class` equals the claimed `defect_class` — else `ERR_POLICY_NOT_FOUND`. This guards claim intake.
15. A supplied `superseded_case_id` resolves to an `ArbitrationCase` — else `ERR_CASE_NOT_FOUND`.
16. A `BuyerRep` acting on a sale presents an `X-User-ID` equal to that sale's `buyer_id` — else `ERR_NOT_SALE_BUYER`. This guards claim intake and inspection purchase.
17. A `BuyerRep` acting on a case presents an `X-User-ID` equal to the case's `claimant_buyer_id` — else `ERR_NOT_CASE_OWNER`. This guards evidence filing, withdrawal, countering and accepting.
18. A `SellerRep` acting on a case presents an `X-User-ID` equal to the `seller_id` of the case's sale — else `ERR_NOT_SALE_SELLER`. This guards the seller response.
19. The `Arbitrator` issuing a decision presents an `X-User-ID` different from the case's `intake_arbitrator_user_id` — else `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES`.
20. `expected_case_version` equals the case's current `version` — else `ERR_STALE_CASE_VERSION`. This guards transportation-leg recording, and it is checked *before* the case's own state precondition.
21. `expected_buyer_version` equals the buyer's current `version` — else `ERR_STALE_BUYER_VERSION`. This guards fee payment, and it is checked *before* the payment-amount check.
22. The `ArbitrationCase` is in a status from which the requested transition is declared in §3 — else `ERR_INVALID_CASE_STATE` (HTTP 409). This guards review opening, seller response, escalation, decision, withdrawal, evidence filing, transportation-leg recording, countering and accepting. For the two append-only routes — evidence filing and transportation-leg recording — the permitted statuses are the five non-terminal ones: `SUBMITTED`, `UNDER_REVIEW`, `NEGOTIATION`, `COUNTERED` and `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`.
23. The `NegotiationRound` status is `OPEN` — else `ERR_INVALID_ROUND_STATE` (HTTP 409). This guards countering and accepting.
24. The `PostSaleInspection` status is `REQUESTED` — else `ERR_INVALID_INSPECTION_STATE` (HTTP 409). This guards inspection completion.
25. The sale's `settlement_status` is `UNSETTLED` — else `ERR_SALE_ALREADY_SETTLED` (HTTP 409). This guards settlement and inspection purchase.
26. The seller's `policy_tier` is `BLOCKED` — else `ERR_SELLER_NOT_BLOCKED` (HTTP 409). This guards reinstatement, which only makes sense on a blocked seller.
27. The acting buyer's `claim_privilege_status` is not `SUSPENDED` — else `ERR_BUYER_PRIVILEGE_SUSPENDED` (HTTP 409). This guards claim intake.
28. The acting buyer's `claim_privilege_status` is `ACTIVE` — else `ERR_BUYER_FEE_HOLD` (HTTP 409). This guards inspection purchase, which is stricter than claim intake because an inspection is a paid privilege: a `FEE_HOLD` buyer may still file a claim but may not buy an inspection.
29. The sale's seller is not `BLOCKED` and the sale's `settlement_hold` is false — else `ERR_SELLER_BLOCKED` (HTTP 409). This guards settlement.
30. No `SaleAnnouncement` on the sale carries the claimed `defect_class` — else `ERR_DEFECT_ANNOUNCED` (HTTP 409). The sole exemption is `TITLE_MISSING`, which remains claimable even after it was announced.
31. The governing completed inspection has not waived rights for the claimed class — else `ERR_PSI_WAIVED_RIGHTS` (HTTP 409).
32. A requested inspection window does not overlap a still-outstanding inspection on the same sale — else `ERR_PSI_WINDOW_OVERLAP` (HTTP 409).
33. The claim is inside its arbitration window on the time axis — else `ERR_ARBITRATION_WINDOW_EXPIRED` (HTTP 409).
34. The claim is inside its mileage allowance — else `ERR_MILEAGE_LIMIT_EXCEEDED` (HTTP 409). This sits deliberately below the time check, so a claim that busts both the clock and the odometer reports the time failure.
35. No countered round on the case is still inside its cooldown — else `ERR_ROUND_COOLDOWN_ACTIVE` (HTTP 409). This guards the seller response.
36. The current logical clock is at or before the round's response deadline — else `ERR_ROUND_EXPIRED` (HTTP 409). This guards countering and accepting.
37. A requested logical-clock instant is at or after the current one — else `ERR_CLOCK_NOT_MONOTONIC` (HTTP 409).
38. No existing `Seller` or `Buyer` already holds the submitted `dealer_license_no` — else `ERR_DUPLICATE_DEALER_LICENSE` (HTTP 409). This guards both seller and buyer registration.
39. No existing `ArbitrationPolicy` already governs the submitted `defect_class` — else `ERR_DUPLICATE_POLICY_DEFECT_CLASS` (HTTP 409).
40. No non-terminal case exists on the same sale for the same defect class — else `ERR_DUPLICATE_OPEN_CLAIM` (HTTP 409).
41. Negotiation is exhausted or timed out — else `ERR_NEGOTIATION_NOT_EXHAUSTED` (HTTP 409). This guards escalation.
42. A non-unwind outcome is permitted — else `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY` (HTTP 409). This guards the decision and sits above both the evidence-sufficiency group and the fiscal group, because releasing a unit whose seller has already conceded an unwind, or awarding money under a policy that forbids allowances, is a compliance failure rather than an arithmetic one.
43. A non-title claim carries an inspection report — else `ERR_MISSING_INSPECTION_REPORT` (HTTP 422). This guards review opening.
44. A title claim carries a title document — else `ERR_TITLE_CLAIM_REQUIRES_TITLE_EVIDENCE` (HTTP 422). This guards review opening.
45. Verified third-party evidence is present where it is demanded — else `ERR_THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION_REQUIRED` (HTTP 422). This guards both review opening and the decision.
46. `claim_amount_cents` reaches the policy's minimum — else `ERR_CLAIM_BELOW_MINIMUM` (HTTP 422).
47. The offered or requested allowance is within the policy cap — else `ERR_ALLOWANCE_EXCEEDS_CAP` (HTTP 422). This guards the seller response and the decision.
48. A fee payment does not exceed the outstanding balance — else `ERR_PAYMENT_EXCEEDS_BALANCE` (HTTP 422).

Three precedence pairs deserve to be called out because they are the ones that surprise operators. A suspended buyer filing a time-barred claim receives `ERR_BUYER_PRIVILEGE_SUSPENDED`, not `ERR_ARBITRATION_WINDOW_EXPIRED`, and equally receives `ERR_BUYER_PRIVILEGE_SUSPENDED` rather than `ERR_CLAIM_BELOW_MINIMUM` when the claim is also under the policy minimum. A claim that is both time-barred and under the policy minimum reports `ERR_ARBITRATION_WINDOW_EXPIRED`. And a decision that both violates the mandatory-unwind rule and over-caps its allowance reports `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY`, not `ERR_ALLOWANCE_EXCEEDS_CAP`.

### 2.9. Cascade Discipline and Counter Conservation

Many operations in this service mutate more than one resource. Where they do, the secondary effects are as much part of the contract as the primary one, and they are all applied within the single request that triggered them — this service has no deferred or eventually-consistent path. Cascades are transitive: an effect can itself cause a further effect, and §3 spells out the chains hop by hop. Every resource touched anywhere in a cascade has its own `version` incremented by 1 and its `updated_at` refreshed, whether or not the client named it. That increment is once per request rather than once per touch: a row a single request reaches by two different paths — the consignor whose `open_claim_count` is released and whose scorecard is then recomputed in the same decision, say — still ends the request exactly one version above where it started.

Where a cascade touches a *set* of rows rather than one row, the rows are processed in ascending `created_at` order. That ordering is observable, because each row's version bump and each ledger posting lands in that order.

Three counters are maintained as exact aggregates rather than as free-running tallies, and they must agree with the underlying rows after **every** request that changes any case's status. A case counts as **open** exactly when its `status` is none of `RESOLVED_ALLOWANCE`, `UNWOUND`, `DENIED`, `WITHDRAWN` or `EXPIRED`. Given that definition: `Buyer.open_claim_count` equals the number of cases whose `claimant_buyer_id` is that buyer and which are open; `Seller.open_claim_count` equals the number of open cases whose sale belongs to that seller; and `AuctionSale.open_case_count` equals the number of open cases on that sale. A fourth condition ties the sale's dispute dimension to those counters: whenever a sale's `open_case_count` is 0 and no `ArbitrationDecision` exists on any of that sale's cases, the sale's `arbitration_status` is `NONE`.

The practical consequence is that every forward path and every compensating path must move these counters symmetrically. File a claim and then withdraw it, and all four conditions return to their pre-claim values — including the sale's `arbitration_status` reverting from `ACTIVE` back to `NONE`. A case expired by the deadline sweep must decrement exactly as the operator-driven withdrawal does; the two paths differ in who triggered them and in nothing else.

### 2.10. The Settlement Ledger as a Zero-Sum Record

Money movements are recorded as `SettlementLedgerEntry` rows. Each row names the sale it belongs to through `sale_id`, optionally the case that caused it through `case_id` (null for proceeds entries posted at settlement, which are not caused by any dispute), the `party` it lands on — one of `BUYER`, `SELLER` or `AUCTION` — the `entry_type` giving the economic reason, an `amount_signed_cents` where a positive figure credits the party and a negative figure debits it, and a `posted_at_utc` recording the logical-clock instant of the posting.

Six entry types exist, and every one of them is posted as a **balanced batch** whose signed amounts sum to exactly zero:

- `SALE_PROCEEDS` — the seller is credited the sale price and the buyer is debited the same amount.
- `SALE_PROCEEDS_REVERSAL` — the mirror image: the buyer is credited the sale price and the seller is debited it.
- `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` — the buyer is credited the allowance and the seller is debited it.
- `TRANSPORT_ALLOCATION` — one batch per transportation leg: the buyer is debited its leg share, the seller is debited its leg share, and the auction house is credited the sum of those two shares.
- `FRIVOLOUS_FEE_ASSESSED` — the buyer is debited the assessed fee and the auction house is credited it.
- `FRIVOLOUS_FEE_OFFSET` — the buyer is debited the offset amount and the auction house is credited it.

From this follows the ledger's governing invariant: at every observable moment, the signed amounts of all entries belonging to one sale sum to exactly zero. A second, sharper invariant governs compensation: settle a sale and then unwind it, and the per-party net across the `SALE_PROCEEDS` and `SALE_PROCEEDS_REVERSAL` entry types is exactly zero for the buyer *and* exactly zero for the seller — not merely zero when the two parties are added together. This is why an unwind of a sale that was never settled posts no reversal rows at all: there were no proceeds to reverse, and inventing a reversal pair would break the first invariant by twice the sale price.

One movement in the service is deliberately *not* ledgered: a buyer's cash payment against an outstanding frivolous-fee balance posts no `SettlementLedgerEntry` at all, because that money is settled with the auction house directly and never touches a sale's books.

## 3. Business Logic & Workflows

This section walks the dispute from the registry entries that must exist before a unit ever crosses the block, through intake, negotiation and adjudication, to the consequences that outlive the case. Rules are stated where the business narrative places them; a reader assembling one operation will find its inputs and its guards distributed across the subsections that own them.

### 3.1. The Registry: Consignors, Buying Dealers and Published Arbitration Policy

Nothing else in the service can happen until the auction house has registered the two dealers and published the policy that will govern their dispute. An `AuctionAdmin` registers a consigning seller with a legal `name` — between 1 and 120 characters, and not blank — and a state `dealer_license_no` of between 4 and 24 characters. License numbers are globally unique across the *whole* dealer population: a submitted `dealer_license_no` that is already held by any registered seller **or** by any registered buyer is refused with `ERR_DUPLICATE_DEALER_LICENSE`. That collision test is an exact, case-sensitive comparison of the submitted string against the stored ones, with no trimming, case-folding or punctuation-stripping normalisation of any kind: `AB1234` and `ab1234` are two different licence numbers and both may be registered. A freshly registered consignor begins in `policy_tier` `GOOD_STANDING`, with `blocked_at_utc` null, `reinstated_count` 0, and every counter and rate on its record at 0: `rolling_window_sales_count`, `rolling_window_upheld_count`, `lifetime_sales_count`, `lifetime_upheld_count`, `open_claim_count`, `rolling_arbitration_rate_bps` and `lifetime_arbitration_rate_bps`.

Buying dealers are registered the same way, with the same `name` and `dealer_license_no` rules and the same shared uniqueness namespace. A new buyer starts with `claim_privilege_status` `ACTIVE`, `frivolous_claim_count` 0, `frivolous_fee_balance_cents` 0, `open_claim_count` 0 and `lifetime_claim_count` 0. Those three states — `ACTIVE`, `FEE_HOLD` and `SUSPENDED` — are the buyer's licence to use the arbitration process at all, and §3.9 describes how a buyer loses it.

Arbitration policy is published one defect family at a time. The five families are `FRAME_DAMAGE`, `MECHANICAL`, `MILEAGE_DISCREPANCY`, `TITLE_BRANDED` and `TITLE_MISSING`, and exactly one policy may exist per family: a second publication for a `defect_class` that already has a policy is refused with `ERR_DUPLICATE_POLICY_DEFECT_CLASS`. Each policy is created at `version` 1 and carries nine settings.

Three of the nine are mandatory scalars. `min_claim_amount_cents` is the smallest claim figure the department will entertain under this policy and may not be negative. `max_allowance_bps` is the repair-allowance ceiling expressed as basis points of the sale price and must be between 0 and 10000 inclusive; a policy that sets it to 0 forbids repair allowances entirely, so the only way to uphold such a claim is to unwind the sale. `psi_extension_hours` is the number of hours a matching post-sale inspection finding adds to the window and may not be negative.

Two are booleans that shape evidence and rights, and both are mandatory: each must be present and must be a JSON `true` or `false`, since neither carries a default to fall back on. `requires_third_party_verification`, when true, means intake review cannot be opened on a case under this policy unless a verified third-party verification is already on file. `psi_clean_waives_rights`, when true, means a completed post-sale inspection that did *not* find this defect family strips the buyer's right to arbitrate it at all.

The remaining four are the temporal settings, and three of them are optional. `window_hours` is the length of the arbitration window in hours, measured from the moment the hammer fell, and it must be at least 1 when supplied; omitting it stores null, which means the window is **unlimited** and the elapsed-time test is skipped entirely — this is how title arbitration works, since a branded or missing title is a defect the buyer may raise whenever it surfaces. `mileage_limit` is the maximum number of miles the buyer may put on the unit after the sale and still arbitrate; it may not be negative when supplied, and omitting it stores null, meaning the mileage test is skipped entirely. `seller_response_hours` is how long the consignor has to answer once intake review is opened; it must be at least 1 and, when omitted, stores 24 hours.

### 3.2. The Block: Recording Sales, Reading Announcements and Releasing Proceeds

A `BlockClerk` records each unit as it crosses the block. The sale record carries the `vin` of the unit — exactly 17 characters, uppercase alphanumeric — the `seller_id` of the consignor who ran it, the `buyer_id` of the dealer who bought it at the hammer, a `sale_price_cents` of at least 1, the `hammer_time_utc` at which the hammer fell, the `announced_odometer_miles` read out at the block — an integer that may not be negative — the `light_channel` under which the unit sold, and the `title_status` declared. `hammer_time_utc` is the t-zero for every arbitration window in the system and may not be later than the current logical clock; a hammer time in the logical future is a malformed request answered with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`, screened alongside the field checks at the schema stage and therefore *before* the body's `seller_id` and `buyer_id` are resolved. `light_channel` is one of `GREEN`, `YELLOW` or `RED` and is more than descriptive colour: it selects the severity multiplier that discounts any repair allowance later awarded on the unit. `title_status` is one of `CLEAN`, `BRANDED_SALVAGE` or `MISSING`.

A sale carries two independent state dimensions that never gate one another. The money dimension, `settlement_status`, begins at `UNSETTLED` and admits exactly three moves: `UNSETTLED → SETTLED` when proceeds are released, `SETTLED → REVERSED` when a settled sale is unwound, and `UNSETTLED → REVERSED` when a sale is unwound before its money ever moved. The dispute dimension, `arbitration_status`, begins at `NONE` and admits `NONE → ACTIVE` when a claim is filed, `ACTIVE → NONE` when the last open claim is released without a decision, and `ACTIVE → RESOLVED` when a decision lands. Alongside them the record keeps `open_case_count` at 0, a `settlement_hold` flag at false, and `settled_at_utc` at null. Recording the sale has one consequence outside the sale row, and it is a purely derivational one: the unit immediately counts toward its consignor's arbitration scorecard, which is what makes a seller's rate meaningful rather than a function of how many claims happened to be filed. That counting is done by the scorecard computation of §3.9 reading the sales collection whenever it runs, not by writing anything onto the consignor — so recording a sale leaves the `Seller` row completely untouched: its stored `lifetime_sales_count` and `rolling_window_sales_count` are **not** advanced, its rates are not refreshed, its `updated_at` is not stamped and its `version` does not move. Those stored figures catch up only when an upheld decision recomputes them, while the scorecard read of §3.9 shows the sale at once.

Sales can be listed with three optional filters — `seller_id`, `buyer_id` and `settlement_status` — alongside the `limit` and `offset` controls of §2.3. The three combine as a conjunction. A `settlement_status` filter must name a declared member of that enum or the request is rejected with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`; the two identifier filters, by contrast, are matched verbatim against the stored foreign keys and are never resolved against the registry, so a `seller_id` or `buyer_id` naming nobody yields an empty `data` array with `total_count` 0 rather than a 404.

Condition announcements are the block's disclosure record. A `BlockClerk` attaches an announcement to a sale with a `defect_class` drawn from the same five families and the verbatim `disclosure_text` read at the block, between 1 and 500 characters and not blank. The row records its `sale_id` from the path it was posted against and an `announced_at_utc` taken from the logical clock, and it is immutable thereafter — announcements carry no `updated_at` and no `version`. Nothing constrains how many announcements a sale may carry or when they may be recorded: the same `defect_class` may be announced repeatedly on one sale, and an announcement may be attached to a sale that has already been settled or already carries open cases. Recording one never touches the sale row itself, so the sale's `version` does not move. Their business effect is subtractive: once a defect family has been announced on a sale, a buyer may no longer arbitrate that family, and an attempt to do so is refused with `ERR_DEFECT_ANNOUNCED`. There is exactly one exemption, and it exists because a missing title is a paperwork failure the auction house cannot cure by talking about it: `TITLE_MISSING` remains claimable even when it was announced at the block.

Releasing proceeds is an `AuctionAdmin` action that advances `settlement_status` `UNSETTLED → SETTLED` and stamps `settled_at_utc` with the current logical clock. It posts a balanced `SALE_PROCEEDS` pair — the seller credited the full `sale_price_cents`, the buyer debited the same figure, both rows with a null `case_id` since no dispute caused them — and increments the sale's `version` by 1. The request carries no payload at all and any body sent with it is ignored, and the two ledger rows are written in the order just stated, so the seller's credit precedes the buyer's debit when the sale's ledger is listed. Two guards apply. A sale whose `settlement_status` is not `UNSETTLED` is refused with `ERR_SALE_ALREADY_SETTLED`, so proceeds cannot be released twice and a reversed sale cannot be re-released. And a sale is refused with `ERR_SELLER_BLOCKED` when its consignor's `policy_tier` is `BLOCKED` or when the sale itself carries `settlement_hold` true — the auction house's lever against a seller whose arbitration record has collapsed. Crucially, settlement is *not* blocked by an open dispute: a sale whose `arbitration_status` is `ACTIVE` may still be settled, because the two dimensions are orthogonal and the department would otherwise be able to hold a seller's money hostage merely by receiving a claim.

Once a sale is settled, `settled_at_utc` is never rewritten. A later unwind moves `settlement_status` `SETTLED → REVERSED` but leaves the original settlement instant in place as a historical fact.

### 3.3. The Post-Sale Inspection Programme and Its Effect on Arbitration Rights

The post-sale inspection is a paid product the buying dealer may purchase on a unit it has just bought, and it is the single most consequential optional step in the whole process, because its findings can either buy the buyer extra time to arbitrate or destroy the right to arbitrate altogether.

A `BuyerRep` purchases one by naming the `sale_id` and a `program_tier` of either `STANDARD` or `PREMIUM`. The tier selects the fee: `fee_cents` is 12500 for a `STANDARD` programme and 27500 for a `PREMIUM` one. That figure is a recorded price and nothing more: buying an inspection posts no `SettlementLedgerEntry`, moves no counter and bumps no `version` on the buyer, the sale or the consignor, and no operation anywhere in the service ever collects, offsets or reverses it — the inspection row is the only trace the purchase leaves. The new row records its `sale_id`, `requested_at_utc` from the logical clock, computes `window_end_utc` as 72 hours (three days) after `requested_at_utc`, sets `status` to `REQUESTED`, leaves `completed_at_utc` null and `findings` as the empty array; it stores no purchaser identity of its own, so the buying dealer is reachable only through the sale's `buyer_id`. Four guards run before it is written, and they fire in this order: the named sale must exist; the caller's `X-User-ID` must be that sale's `buyer_id`, or the purchase is refused with `ERR_NOT_SALE_BUYER`; the sale's `settlement_status` must still be `UNSETTLED`, since inspecting a unit whose money has already changed hands serves no purpose — otherwise `ERR_SALE_ALREADY_SETTLED`; and only then is the buyer read, whose `claim_privilege_status` must be exactly `ACTIVE`, so a buyer in `FEE_HOLD` or `SUSPENDED` is refused with `ERR_BUYER_FEE_HOLD`. A settled sale bought by a fee-held buyer therefore reports `ERR_SALE_ALREADY_SETTLED`, matching the ladder of §2.8.

The fifth guard is the interesting one. Each inspection occupies a **half-open** interval on its sale that runs from `requested_at_utc` up to but not including `window_end_utc`. A new purchase's interval runs from the current logical clock up to but not including 72 hours later. If that interval overlaps the interval of any inspection on the same sale whose `status` is still `REQUESTED`, the purchase is refused with `ERR_PSI_WINDOW_OVERLAP`. Because both intervals exclude their right endpoint, a request arriving at exactly the incumbent inspection's `window_end_utc` touches but does not overlap, and is accepted. Completed and expired inspections do not reserve anything, so a sale can carry a long history of inspections as long as no two outstanding ones collide.

An `Inspector` closes the loop by filing `findings`: an array of defect families drawn from the same five values, with no duplicates, and legitimately empty. `findings` is a required field — omitting it, or sending it as `null` or as anything other than a JSON array, is a malformed request answered with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`, and a repeated family inside the array is rejected the same way rather than silently de-duplicated. The array is stored in exactly the order it was submitted; no other field is accepted from the client. Any `Inspector` may complete any inspection: completion carries no ownership test of any kind, the acting `X-User-ID` is not compared against anything and is not recorded on the row. Filing moves `status` `REQUESTED → COMPLETED`, stamps `completed_at_utc` with the logical clock, stores the array, and increments the inspection's `version` by 1 — and touches nothing else: the sale, its buyer and its consignor are all left exactly as they were. An inspection whose `status` is not `REQUESTED` cannot be completed and is refused with `ERR_INVALID_INSPECTION_STATE`, which is how a second completion of the same inspection and any attempt on an expired one are both turned away.

From the moment an inspection is completed, it may govern every later claim on that sale. When more than one completed inspection exists on a sale, the **governing** one is chosen deterministically: take all inspections on the sale whose `status` is `COMPLETED`, rank them by `completed_at_utc` descending and break ties by `created_at` descending, and take the first. Inspections still in `REQUESTED` and inspections that reached `EXPIRED` are never governing, and when the selection comes up empty the sale simply has no inspection effect.

The governing inspection acts in one of two opposite directions, decided by the claimed family and the policy's `psi_clean_waives_rights` flag. If the governing inspection's `findings` **contain** the claimed defect family, the inspector has corroborated the buyer, and the policy's `psi_extension_hours` is added to the arbitration window for that claim. If the `findings` **omit** the claimed family — including the case of a completely clean inspection with an empty array — and the governing policy sets `psi_clean_waives_rights` to true, then the buyer paid a professional to look for this defect, the professional did not find it, and the right to arbitrate that family on this sale is gone: the claim is refused with `ERR_PSI_WAIVED_RIGHTS`. If `psi_clean_waives_rights` is false, a clean inspection is simply neutral and adds no extension.

Inspections do not live forever. An outstanding inspection whose `window_end_utc` has been reached is expired by the deadline sweep described in §3.9, and an outstanding inspection also expires as a consequence of its buyer being suspended for frivolous claims. Every inspection requested on a sale can be listed, in ascending `created_at` order.

### 3.4. Filing a Claim and the Arbitration Window Gauntlet

A `BuyerRep` files a claim by naming the `sale_id`, the `defect_class` complained of, a `claim_amount_cents` of at least 0 asserting the repair or diminution figure, the `odometer_at_claim` reading at the moment of filing (also at least 0), a `complaint_text` narrative between 1 and 1000 characters and not blank, and optionally a `superseded_case_id`, which stores null when it is omitted. Nothing else is accepted from the client: the claimant is not supplied but derived, and every derived quantity on the new case is computed by the server before the row exists. Both `claim_amount_cents` and `complaint_text` are stored verbatim and are pure record: apart from the `min_claim_amount_cents` floor tested below, the claimed figure never bounds an offer, a counter, an award or a cap anywhere later in the dispute, so a buyer who claims 900000 cents and a buyer who claims the policy minimum are treated identically by every subsequent computation.

Intake runs a gauntlet, and the order in which its checks fire is part of the contract (see the global precedence in §2.8). The sale must exist, or `ERR_SALE_NOT_FOUND`. Exactly one policy must govern the claimed family, or `ERR_POLICY_NOT_FOUND`. A supplied `superseded_case_id` must resolve to a real case, or `ERR_CASE_NOT_FOUND`; and that prior case must be on the *same* sale, complain of the *same* defect family, and sit in `WITHDRAWN` or `EXPIRED` status — a refile is only ever a replacement for a claim the buyer abandoned or let lapse, never for one that was decided, and a `superseded_case_id` failing any of those three conditions is a malformed request answered with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`. Although that identifier is the schema-stage one, this particular test is necessarily evaluated *here* — after the sale, the policy and the prior case have all been resolved — so a filing that names both an unknown `sale_id` and a mismatched `superseded_case_id` reports `ERR_SALE_NOT_FOUND`, and one whose defect family has no published policy reports `ERR_POLICY_NOT_FOUND`, in each case never reaching the mismatch. The caller's `X-User-ID` must equal the sale's `buyer_id`, or `ERR_NOT_SALE_BUYER`. The buyer's `claim_privilege_status` must not be `SUSPENDED`, or `ERR_BUYER_PRIVILEGE_SUSPENDED` — note that a `FEE_HOLD` buyer is still permitted to file, unlike the inspection purchase of §3.3. No announcement of the same family may exist on the sale unless the family is `TITLE_MISSING`, or `ERR_DEFECT_ANNOUNCED`. The governing inspection must not have waived rights, or `ERR_PSI_WAIVED_RIGHTS`. The window and mileage tests below must pass. No non-terminal case for the same sale and the same family may already exist, or `ERR_DUPLICATE_OPEN_CLAIM` — a sale may legitimately carry several concurrent cases, just not two for one defect family. And `claim_amount_cents` must reach the policy's `min_claim_amount_cents`, or `ERR_CLAIM_BELOW_MINIMUM`.

**Window evaluation.** Before the case row is persisted, the server evaluates eligibility on two independently nullable axes and stores the whole working on the case. First, `hours_elapsed_at_claim` is the difference between `claimed_at_utc` — the logical-clock instant the claim is filed — and the sale's `hammer_time_utc`, taken in seconds, divided by 3600, and rounded half up to three decimal places; it crosses the API as a JSON number rather than as a quoted string, so a claim filed exactly one and a half hours after the hammer reads `1.5`. Second, `miles_driven_at_claim` is the `odometer_at_claim` reading minus the sale's `announced_odometer_miles`, a **signed** subtraction that is never clamped: a filing whose odometer sits below the announced reading stores a negative `miles_driven_at_claim`, and a negative figure trivially satisfies any mileage limit. Third, `psi_extension_applied_hours` is the policy's `psi_extension_hours` when a governing inspection exists and its `findings` contain the claimed family, and 0 in every other situation — no governing inspection at all, or a governing inspection whose findings omit the family. Fourth, `effective_window_hours` is null when the policy's `window_hours` is null, and otherwise the policy's `window_hours` plus `psi_extension_applied_hours`. Those last two are computed independently of one another, so a matching inspection under an unlimited-window policy stores its non-zero `psi_extension_applied_hours` alongside a null `effective_window_hours` — the extension is recorded even where there is no finite window for it to extend.

The two axes are then tested. The time axis passes when the policy's `window_hours` is null, and otherwise when `hours_elapsed_at_claim` is less than or equal to `effective_window_hours`; a claim whose elapsed hours land exactly on `effective_window_hours` is still in time. The mileage axis passes when the policy's `mileage_limit` is null, and otherwise when `miles_driven_at_claim` is less than or equal to `mileage_limit`; a claim exactly on the limit is still eligible. `window_eligible` is stored as true only when both axes pass — and since either axis failing rejects the filing outright, every case that actually reaches the store carries `window_eligible` true; the field records the working rather than gating anything a reader must test. When the time axis fails, intake is refused with `ERR_ARBITRATION_WINDOW_EXPIRED`. When the time axis passes but the mileage axis fails, intake is refused with `ERR_MILEAGE_LIMIT_EXCEEDED`. When both fail, the time error is the one reported — the clock outranks the odometer.

All eight combinations of (unlimited window or not, unlimited mileage or not, inspection extension applied or not) are reachable and each must behave as written. A title claim under an unlimited-window, unlimited-mileage policy always passes both axes regardless of how long ago the hammer fell or how far the unit was driven.

A successful filing writes the case at `status` `SUBMITTED` with `claimed_at_utc` from the logical clock, and derives three references from the sale and the policy rather than from the request: `claimant_buyer_id` is copied from the sale's `buyer_id`, `policy_id` is the resolved policy, and `policy_version_at_intake` pins that policy's `version` so that a later edit to policy cannot retroactively move this case's window or cap. The negotiation and adjudication fields start neutral: `current_round_index` 0, `seller_conceded` false, `seller_response_timed_out` false, `fault_party` `NONE`, `review_opened_at_utc` null, `intake_arbitrator_user_id` null, `latest_verified_estimate_cents` 0 and `verified_true_mileage` null.

Filing also moves three other records in the same request. The sale's `arbitration_status` moves `NONE → ACTIVE` — but only when it genuinely stands at `NONE`: a sale that already carries an open case simply stays `ACTIVE`, and a sale whose earlier case has already been decided stays `RESOLVED` rather than being dragged back to `ACTIVE`, so a fresh claim on a different defect family of a resolved sale raises the counters without disturbing the dispute dimension at all. Whichever of those three it was, the sale's `open_case_count` rises by 1 and its `version` rises by 1. The buyer's `open_claim_count` and `lifetime_claim_count` each rise by 1 and its `version` rises by 1. The consignor's `open_claim_count` rises by 1 and its `version` rises by 1. This is the forward half of the counter conservation described in §2.9; §3.9 covers the compensating halves.

Cases can be listed with five optional filters — `sale_id`, `claimant_buyer_id`, `policy_id`, `superseded_case_id` and `status` — alongside `limit` and `offset`. The five combine as a conjunction. A `status` filter must name a declared member of the ten-value case-status enum or the request is rejected with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`; the four identifier filters, by contrast, are matched verbatim against the stored values and are never resolved against anything, so a `sale_id`, `claimant_buyer_id`, `policy_id` or `superseded_case_id` naming nothing yields an empty `data` array with `total_count` 0 rather than a 404. Filtering by `superseded_case_id` is how an operator walks a refile chain forward from the abandoned claim to its replacement; cases that supersede nothing carry a null there and are matched by no value of that filter.

### 3.5. Evidence Files and Opening Intake Review

A claim with nothing behind it is a phone call, not an arbitration. Both a `BuyerRep` — acting on its own case only, or `ERR_NOT_CASE_OWNER` — and an `Inspector`, who may support any case, attach evidence to a case while that case is in any of the five non-terminal statuses `SUBMITTED`, `UNDER_REVIEW`, `NEGOTIATION`, `COUNTERED` or `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`; an attempt against a case that has already reached a terminal status is refused with `ERR_INVALID_CASE_STATE`.

Each evidence row names its `evidence_type` — one of `INSPECTION_REPORT`, `DIAGNOSTIC_SCAN`, `THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION`, `PHOTO_SET` or `TITLE_DOCUMENT` — the `provider_name` of the shop, scan-tool vendor or verifier that produced the artefact (1 to 120 characters, not blank), and a `document_reference` locator for the artefact itself (also 1 to 120 characters, not blank). Two optional figures may travel with it: `repair_estimate_cents`, the repair cost the artefact substantiates, and `odometer_reading`, the true mileage the artefact substantiates. Either may be omitted, in which case it stores null; neither may be negative. The row also records its `case_id` from the path it was filed against, a `recorded_at_utc` taken from the logical clock, and the submitter's identity as `submitted_by_role` — either `BuyerRep` or `Inspector` — and `submitted_by_user_id`, both taken from the request headers rather than the body.

The `verified` flag is where implementations most often go wrong, so it is worth stating flatly: `verified` is a pure function of `evidence_type` and of nothing else. It is true precisely for `INSPECTION_REPORT` and `THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION`, and false for `DIAGNOSTIC_SCAN`, `PHOTO_SET` and `TITLE_DOCUMENT`. The submitting role has no influence on it — an `Inspector`-filed photo set is unverified and a `BuyerRep`-filed inspection report is verified — and any value a client supplies for it is ignored.

Filing evidence increments the parent case's `version` by 1, and the resulting value is written onto the evidence row as `case_version_after`. Because the append itself is the mutation, that value is never below 2. Evidence rows are immutable and carry no `updated_at` and no `version` of their own; the whole file can be listed in ascending `created_at` order.

Two derived readings on the case are maintained from the evidence file, and both look at the *latest* qualifying artefact rather than the best or the largest. `latest_verified_estimate_cents` is found by taking the case's evidence rows whose `verified` is true and whose `repair_estimate_cents` is not null, ranking them by `recorded_at_utc` descending with ties broken by `created_at` descending, and reading the first row's `repair_estimate_cents`; when no row qualifies it is 0. `verified_true_mileage` is found by taking the case's evidence rows whose `evidence_type` is `THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION`, whose `verified` is true and whose `odometer_reading` is not null, ranking them the same way, and reading the first row's `odometer_reading`; when no row qualifies it stays null. A later unverified artefact therefore never displaces an earlier verified one, and a diagnostic scan carrying a large repair figure moves neither reading. Both readings are recomputed from the whole file on every append, inside the very same single `version` bump the append itself causes — refreshing them is never a second mutation of the case — and once a reading has been established a later append can only move it to another qualifying row's figure, never back to 0 or to null.

Opening intake review is an `Arbitrator` action and the case's first state transition: `SUBMITTED → UNDER_REVIEW`. The request carries no payload at all — no note, no reason, nothing — and any body sent with it is ignored rather than rejected. A case in any other status is refused with `ERR_INVALID_CASE_STATE`, and that status test is evaluated *before* any evidence is examined, so a case already in `UNDER_REVIEW` with no inspection report on file reports `ERR_INVALID_CASE_STATE` and not `ERR_MISSING_INSPECTION_REPORT`. Three evidence-sufficiency gates then apply, in this order.

For every family other than the two title families — that is, for `FRAME_DAMAGE`, `MECHANICAL` and `MILEAGE_DISCREPANCY` — at least one evidence row on the case must carry `evidence_type` `INSPECTION_REPORT`, or review opening is refused with `ERR_MISSING_INSPECTION_REPORT`. For `TITLE_BRANDED` and `TITLE_MISSING` claims that first gate does not apply and a different one does: at least one evidence row must carry `evidence_type` `TITLE_DOCUMENT`, or the request is refused with `ERR_TITLE_CLAIM_REQUIRES_TITLE_EVIDENCE`. A title claim therefore needs no inspection report, and a mechanical claim needs no title document; the two gates are mutually exclusive and neither substitutes for the other. Finally, and independently of the family, when the governing policy sets `requires_third_party_verification` to true, at least one evidence row must have `evidence_type` `THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION` **and** `verified` true, or the request is refused with `ERR_THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION_REQUIRED`.

A successful opening stamps `review_opened_at_utc` with the current logical clock — the moment from which the consignor's `seller_response_hours` starts running — records the acting officer's `X-User-ID` as `intake_arbitrator_user_id`, and increments the case's `version` by 1. Recording that identifier has a permanent consequence: from this point the same officer is barred from rendering the case's decision, and an attempt to do so is refused with `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES`. The department's separation between the officer who intakes and the officer who adjudicates is enforced by the data, not by procedure.

### 3.6. Seller Response and the Repair-Allowance Negotiation Cycle

Once review is open, the consignor answers. A `SellerRep` whose `X-User-ID` matches the `seller_id` of the case's sale — otherwise `ERR_NOT_SALE_SELLER` — submits a `response_type` and a `response_note` of 1 to 500 characters that is not blank, optionally with `seller_offer_cents`. The case must be in `UNDER_REVIEW` or `COUNTERED`, or the answer is refused with `ERR_INVALID_CASE_STATE`. Three answers are open to the consignor: `OFFER_REPAIR_ALLOWANCE`, `DISPUTE` and `ACCEPT_UNWIND`. All three are legal from *either* of those two statuses — the consignor is not locked into re-offering once a buyer has countered, and may break off the haggling with a dispute or a concession from `COUNTERED` just as readily as from `UNDER_REVIEW`. A fourth value, `TIMEOUT`, exists on the record but is reserved for the deadline sweep and is never a legal client answer; a request supplying it is refused with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`. `seller_offer_cents` belongs to the allowance answer alone, but it is still schema-checked whenever it is supplied: sent alongside a `DISPUTE` or an `ACCEPT_UNWIND` it must be a non-negative integer or the request is refused with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`, and if it survives that check it is then discarded, since those two answers always record 0. Whichever answer is given, the response is `201 Created` carrying the newly minted `NegotiationRound` in `data` — never the updated case.

Every answer mints a `NegotiationRound` row. The row carries its `case_id` from the path, a `round_index` set to the case's `current_round_index` plus 1 at creation time, the `response_type` and `response_note` as submitted, an `opened_at_utc` from the logical clock, and — as with every case-scoped write — the case's `current_round_index` advances and the case's `version` rises by 1. The remaining fields are minted uniformly whatever the answer was, which is worth stating because two of them look like they should belong to the haggling alone: **every** round, including the `CLOSED` ones a dispute or a concession produces and the `CLOSED` one the sweep mints on a timeout, carries a `response_deadline_utc` 48 hours after its own `opened_at_utc`, and every round starts with `buyer_demand_cents`, `counter_note`, `countered_at_utc` and `cooldown_until_utc` all null. On a `CLOSED` round that deadline is simply inert: a closed round can be neither countered nor accepted, and the sweep's lapsed-offer phase looks only at rounds still in `OPEN`. And `round_index` has no ceiling — the three-round threshold of §3.8 unlocks escalation without ever blocking a further answer, so a fourth and fifth round are lawful continuations of the cycle rather than errors.

An **allowance offer** is the only answer that opens haggling. `seller_offer_cents` must be supplied and strictly greater than 0, or the request is a malformed one answered with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST`. The offer must also respect the policy ceiling: the cap is the sale's `sale_price_cents` multiplied by the policy's `max_allowance_bps`, divided by 10000 and rounded half up to whole cents, and an offer above that cap is refused with `ERR_ALLOWANCE_EXCEEDS_CAP` while an offer landing exactly on it is accepted. Under a policy whose `max_allowance_bps` is 0 that cap is 0, so every legal offer — which must exceed 0 — necessarily exceeds it, and the consignor's only real answers are a dispute or a conceded unwind. The refusal such an offer meets is `ERR_ALLOWANCE_EXCEEDS_CAP`: `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY` guards the decision of §3.8 and is never raised by a seller response, not even on a case whose `seller_conceded` is already true. A cooldown guard applies too, described below. When all three pass, the round is written with `status` `OPEN`, a `response_deadline_utc` of 48 hours (two days) after `opened_at_utc`, `buyer_demand_cents` and `counter_note` and `countered_at_utc` and `cooldown_until_utc` all null, and the case advances `UNDER_REVIEW → NEGOTIATION` or, when the consignor is re-offering after a counter, `COUNTERED → NEGOTIATION`. Any round on the case still sitting in `COUNTERED` is marked `SUPERSEDED` as the new round opens, processed in ascending `created_at` order.

A **dispute** ends the haggling before it starts. The round is written with `response_type` `DISPUTE`, `seller_offer_cents` 0 and `status` `CLOSED` — the status that marks a round which never entered haggling — and the case jumps straight `UNDER_REVIEW → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`, or `COUNTERED → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW` when the consignor breaks off after a buyer's counter instead of re-offering. On that second path the round the buyer countered is **left** in `COUNTERED`: only a fresh allowance offer supersedes a countered round, so a case can perfectly well sit in `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW` still carrying one.

A **conceded unwind** is written the same way: `response_type` `ACCEPT_UNWIND`, `seller_offer_cents` 0, `status` `CLOSED`, and the case moves `UNDER_REVIEW → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW` — or `COUNTERED → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`, again leaving any countered round exactly where it stands. It carries one extra and irreversible effect: the case's `seller_conceded` flag is set true, which forces the eventual outcome to be an unwind. Once the consignor has conceded, any attempt to award a repair allowance or deny the claim is refused with `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY`.

The buyer's move against an open offer is to **counter** it, supplying a `buyer_demand_cents` figure — a required non-negative integer, where 0 is a legal demand and is stored as 0 rather than treated as an omission — and a `counter_note`, likewise required and non-blank but, unlike the consignor's 500-character `response_note`, subject to no upper length bound at all. Neither figure is compared against the standing offer or against the policy cap: a counter may demand more than the cap permits, because a demand is a position rather than an award. Countering requires the case to be in `NEGOTIATION`, the caller to be the case's claimant, the addressed round to belong to the addressed case, the round's `status` to be `OPEN` — otherwise `ERR_INVALID_ROUND_STATE` — and the current logical clock to be at or before the round's `response_deadline_utc`; a counter arriving exactly on the deadline is still in time, and one arriving strictly after it is refused with `ERR_ROUND_EXPIRED`. A successful counter moves the round `OPEN → COUNTERED`, stamps `countered_at_utc` with the logical clock, computes `cooldown_until_utc` as 2 hours after `countered_at_utc`, stores `buyer_demand_cents` and `counter_note`, and increments the round's `version` by 1. In the same request the case moves `NEGOTIATION → COUNTERED` and its `version` rises by 1. The response is `200 OK` carrying the updated round in `data`, not the case.

`cooldown_until_utc` is the trailing cool-off buffer that stops a consignor from bombarding a buyer with re-offers minutes apart, and it is an **inclusive** end: while any round on the case is in `COUNTERED` with a `cooldown_until_utc` at or after the current logical clock, a further seller response is refused with `ERR_ROUND_COOLDOWN_ACTIVE`. A re-offer arriving at exactly `cooldown_until_utc` is therefore rejected; only strictly after it does the consignor get to speak again. Despite its purpose the guard is not specific to re-offers: a `DISPUTE` or an `ACCEPT_UNWIND` arriving inside a live cooldown is refused with the same `ERR_ROUND_COOLDOWN_ACTIVE`, so a consignor who wants out of the haggling must still wait the buffer out. It is also evaluated before the allowance ceiling, so an over-cap re-offer inside a live cooldown reports `ERR_ROUND_COOLDOWN_ACTIVE` rather than `ERR_ALLOWANCE_EXCEEDS_CAP`. Only a round still sitting in `COUNTERED` can hold a cooldown open: once the next offer has superseded it, its `cooldown_until_utc` stops gating anything, though the stamp is left on the row as a historical fact. That re-offer then closes the cycle `NEGOTIATION → COUNTERED → NEGOTIATION`, and a case may loop through it repeatedly, each pass adding a round and moving the round it answers `COUNTERED → SUPERSEDED`.

The buyer's other move is to **accept** the open offer, and it resolves the case outright. Acceptance requires the same five conditions as a counter — case in `NEGOTIATION`, caller is the claimant, round belongs to the case, round `status` is `OPEN`, and the clock is at or before `response_deadline_utc` — and it takes no payload at all, any body sent with it being ignored rather than rejected. Because the request brings the case's `ArbitrationDecision` into existence, it answers `201 Created` with that newly created decision row in `data`, not with the accepted round. What follows is a five-hop transitive cascade in which each hop is caused by the hop before it rather than by the request:

- **Hop 1.** The round moves `OPEN → ACCEPTED` and the case moves `NEGOTIATION → RESOLVED_ALLOWANCE`. Because the buyer accepted what the consignor put on the table, the case's `fault_party` is set to `SELLER`.
- **Hop 2.** Because the case has reached an upheld terminal status, an `ArbitrationDecision` row is created for it: `outcome` `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE`, `allowance_variant` `NEGOTIATED_ACCEPT`, and `allowance_cents` equal to the accepted round's `seller_offer_cents`. The arbitrator-award figure is evaluated anyway and stored as `computed_award_cents`, purely so the two variants can be compared afterwards — it does **not** bound what the buyer just accepted, because a consignor is free to offer more than the department's formula would have awarded, having already been held to the policy cap at offer time. Because no officer ruled this decision, its `decided_by_arbitrator_user_id` is **null** and its `requested_allowance_cents` is null as well — nothing was requested — while its `fault_party` is the `SELLER` of hop 1, its `decided_at_utc` is the logical-clock instant of the acceptance, and its `rationale` is a server-generated string reading exactly `buyer accepted the seller's repair allowance offer`, since the accepting buyer supplies no payload from which a rationale could come. The four fields that belong to the other outcomes — `sale_price_refund_cents`, `fee_offset_applied_cents`, `frivolous_fee_cents` and `total_buyer_payout_cents` — are each 0 on this row, and the sale's `settlement_status` is left completely untouched: a negotiated allowance never reverses proceeds. Transport allocation runs over the case's transportation legs with the `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` profile, and the ledger receives a `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` pair crediting the buyer `allowance_cents` and debiting the seller the same, plus one balanced `TRANSPORT_ALLOCATION` batch per leg.
- **Hop 3.** Because an upheld decision now exists on one of the consignor's sales, that consignor's `lifetime_upheld_count` rises by 1 and its arbitration scorecard is recomputed, refreshing both `rolling_arbitration_rate_bps` and `lifetime_arbitration_rate_bps`.
- **Hop 4.** Because the recomputed rolling rate may have crossed a tier threshold, the consignor's `policy_tier` advances and a `SellerPolicyAction` row is appended recording the move.
- **Hop 5.** Because the tier may have become `BLOCKED`, every sale of that consignor whose `settlement_status` is `UNSETTLED` and whose `arbitration_status` is `NONE` has `settlement_hold` set true, processed in ascending `created_at` order, each such sale's `version` rising by 1.

Alongside the cascade the usual counters move: the sale's `arbitration_status` goes `ACTIVE → RESOLVED` and its `open_case_count` falls by 1, the buyer's `open_claim_count` falls by 1, the consignor's `open_claim_count` falls by 1, and the case's `version` rises by 1. The negotiation rounds of a case can be listed in ascending `created_at` order at any point.

### 3.7. Transportation Records and Cost Allocation

Disputes move metal. A unit that is unwound goes back to the consignor; a unit inspected off-site is hauled in and hauled out again; a re-delivery after a repair allowance is another truck. Those movements are billed, and someone has to bear them.

An `AuctionAdmin` records each movement against the case as a transportation leg while the case is in any of the five non-terminal statuses. The leg names its `leg_type` — `INBOUND_TO_AUCTION`, `RETURN_TO_SELLER` or `TO_BUYER_REDELIVERY` — the billed `miles`, which may not be negative, and the `carrier_name` that ran it, between 1 and 120 characters and not blank. The payload also carries the mandatory `expected_case_version` described in §2.6, and that stale-version check is evaluated before the case's own status is examined. A written leg records its `case_id` from the path, a `recorded_at_utc` from the logical clock, its computed `cost_cents`, and the `case_version_after` value produced by its own append; it is immutable and carries no `updated_at` and no `version`. The legs of a case can be listed in ascending `created_at` order, which is exactly the order allocation walks them in.

**Per-leg cost.** A leg's `cost_cents` is a dispatch fee plus a distance charge computed over **marginal bands**, not as an average rate. The dispatch fee is 8500 cents on every leg regardless of distance. The distance charge is then 185 cents per mile for the leg's first 100 miles, plus 155 cents per mile for the next 400 miles — that is, for the portion of the distance above 100 miles and up to 500 miles — plus 130 cents per mile for every mile beyond 500. A leg of 100 miles or less pays only the first band; a leg of 600 miles pays the full first band, the full second band, and 100 miles of the third.

**Allocation profile.** When a case is resolved, the total transportation bill is split between the buyer, the consignor and the auction house according to a profile chosen by the pair (decision outcome, `fault_party`). The shares are expressed in basis points of each leg's cost.

- For an `UNWIND`, fault genuinely decides: with `fault_party` `SELLER` the consignor bears 10000 basis points and the buyer nothing; with `fault_party` `BUYER` the buyer bears 10000 basis points and the consignor nothing; with `fault_party` `NONE` the two split it, 5000 basis points each. In all three the auction house is left with nothing.
- For a `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` the profile ignores fault entirely: whatever the `fault_party`, the buyer bears 2000 basis points and the consignor 7000, which leaves 1000 basis points — one tenth of every leg — with the auction house as the price of brokering a settlement.
- For a `CLAIM_DENIED` and equally for a `FRIVOLOUS_DENIED`, the buyer bears the whole bill: 10000 basis points to the buyer, nothing to the consignor, nothing to the auction house, again regardless of `fault_party`.

**Rounding order is normative.** Allocation walks the case's legs in ascending `created_at` order. For each leg it computes that leg's cost, then the buyer's share as the leg cost multiplied by the buyer's basis points, divided by 10000 and rounded half up, and the consignor's share the same way with the consignor's basis points. The auction house's share of that leg is whatever is left: the leg cost minus the buyer's rounded share minus the consignor's rounded share — a figure that **may be negative** when both parties' shares happen to round up. The four totals stored on the decision are then sums across legs: `transport_total_cents` is the sum of the leg costs, `transport_buyer_share_cents` the sum of the per-leg buyer shares, `transport_seller_share_cents` the sum of the per-leg consignor shares, and `transport_auction_share_cents` the sum of the per-leg residuals. Rounding each leg and then summing is the specified behaviour; summing the raw amounts and rounding once at the end produces different figures and is a defect. A case with no legs at all yields 0 for all four totals, and allocation never fails.

Each leg also produces one balanced `TRANSPORT_ALLOCATION` ledger batch, posted in the same ascending `created_at` order: the buyer is debited that leg's buyer share, the consignor is debited that leg's consignor share, and the auction house is credited the sum of those two debits. That batch is always exactly three rows, written in the fixed order buyer, then consignor, then auction house, and a share that came out 0 still gets its own row rather than being omitted — a `CLAIM_DENIED` leg therefore posts a 0-cent consignor row, so a case always contributes exactly three `TRANSPORT_ALLOCATION` entries per leg. There is a deliberate asymmetry here worth understanding, because both numbers are reported and they are different by construction. The ledger records only transfers **between** parties, so the auction house's ledger credits total the buyer's share plus the consignor's share. `transport_auction_share_cents`, by contrast, is the residual the auction house **absorbs** and settles with the carrier outside the sale ledger. The `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` profile, which parks a tenth of every leg with the house, is where that residual is most visible.

### 3.8. Escalation and the Four Arbitration Outcomes

Not every dispute settles itself. When haggling has run its course, an `Arbitrator` escalates the case with an `escalation_note` — required, at least 1 character and not blank, and subject to no upper length bound — moving it `NEGOTIATION → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW` or `COUNTERED → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`. The note is validated and then dropped: no field on the case, on any round or on any other row records it, so escalation leaves the note itself nowhere on the record. Escalation is gated on exhaustion: the case's `current_round_index` must have reached 3 rounds or more, **or** its `seller_response_timed_out` flag must be true. A case that has neither exhausted the round limit nor been abandoned by its consignor is refused with `ERR_NEGOTIATION_NOT_EXHAUSTED` — the department will not take over a negotiation the parties are still conducting. Escalating marks every round on the case still in `OPEN` or `COUNTERED` as `SUPERSEDED` — `OPEN → SUPERSEDED` or `COUNTERED → SUPERSEDED` — processed in ascending `created_at` order, and increments the case's `version` by 1.

A case can also arrive in `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW` without an escalation, by three other routes already described or described in §3.9: the consignor disputed the claim outright, the consignor conceded an unwind, or the deadline sweep timed the consignor out.

**The decision.** An `Arbitrator` other than the one who opened intake review — otherwise `ERR_SEGREGATION_OF_DUTIES` — renders the decision on a case in `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`, supplying the `outcome`, the `fault_party` held responsible, a written `rationale` between 1 and 1000 characters that is not blank, and, only when the outcome is a repair allowance, a `requested_allowance_cents` figure. `requested_allowance_cents` is required for that one outcome and is null for the other three; supplied alongside one of the other three it is still schema-checked as a non-negative integer — a negative figure, or one that is not an integer at all, is refused with `ERR_INVALID_REQUEST` — and only then discarded, the decision storing null regardless. The supplied `fault_party` — `SELLER`, `BUYER` or `NONE` — overwrites whatever `fault_party` the case had accumulated from a timeout or a lapsed round. The resulting `ArbitrationDecision` records its `case_id`, the acting officer as `decided_by_arbitrator_user_id`, and a `decided_at_utc` from the logical clock, which is also the instant the seller scorecard windows key off. Every decision row carries all four of the buyer-position figures — `sale_price_refund_cents`, `fee_offset_applied_cents`, `frivolous_fee_cents` and `total_buyer_payout_cents` — and each of them starts at 0 with only the one branch that owns it filling it in, so an arbitrated repair allowance and both denials all report `total_buyer_payout_cents` 0 even where the buyer is credited an allowance, and only an `UNWIND` ever reports a non-zero refund, offset or payout. A decision row is immutable, carries no `updated_at` and no `version`, and a case is decided at most once, so the decision read on a case returns an array of either zero or one row.

**The award formula.** Two of the four outcomes involve money computed from the case rather than negotiated, and both use one shared basis. A severity multiplier is read from the sale's `light_channel`: 10000 basis points for `GREEN`, 8500 for `YELLOW` and 6000 for `RED` — a red-light unit sold as-is earns the buyer only three fifths of what the same defect would earn under a green light. The base amount depends on the defect family. For a `MILEAGE_DISCREPANCY` claim the base is the true mileage overage priced per mile: take the case's `verified_true_mileage`, subtract the sale's `announced_odometer_miles`, treat any negative difference as 0, and multiply the result by 45 cents per mile. When no qualifying third-party verification exists on the case, the true mileage used in that subtraction is the case's own `odometer_at_claim`. For every other family the base is simply the case's `latest_verified_estimate_cents`. The policy cap is the sale's `sale_price_cents` multiplied by `max_allowance_bps`, divided by 10000, rounded half up. `computed_award_cents` is then the smaller of two figures: the base multiplied by the severity multiplier, divided by 10000 and rounded half up; and the cap. Note that both quantities are rounded before the smaller is chosen — the comparison happens after the roundings, never before.

That single formula feeds two variants, recorded on the decision as `allowance_variant`. Under `NEGOTIATED_ACCEPT`, reached when a buyer accepts a standing offer as described in §3.6, `allowance_cents` is the accepted round's `seller_offer_cents` and `computed_award_cents` is stored alongside it for comparison without constraining it. Under `ARBITRATOR_AWARD`, reached when an officer rules a repair allowance, `allowance_cents` is the smaller of the `requested_allowance_cents` figure and `computed_award_cents` — the officer can never award more than the formula supports and never more than was asked for. For the two non-allowance outcomes `allowance_variant` is `NONE` and both `allowance_cents` and `computed_award_cents` are 0: the award formula is not evaluated at all on a denial, so the decision reports a computed award of 0 even where the case file would have supported a substantial one.

**Outcome `UNWIND`.** The sale is undone. The decision is written with `allowance_variant` `NONE`, `allowance_cents` 0 and `frivolous_fee_cents` 0. Transport allocation runs with the unwind profile keyed on the supplied `fault_party`, posting its per-leg batches in ascending leg `created_at` order. The sale's `settlement_status` becomes `REVERSED` no matter what it was before — `SETTLED → REVERSED` or `UNSETTLED → REVERSED` — but the ledger consequence is conditional: a `SALE_PROCEEDS_REVERSAL` pair crediting the buyer `sale_price_cents` and debiting the consignor the same is posted **only** when the sale's `settlement_status` was `SETTLED`; when the sale was still `UNSETTLED` no reversal rows are posted at all, because no proceeds were ever released and inventing a pair would break the ledger's zero-sum invariant. The case reaches `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW → UNWOUND`.

The buyer's cash position on an unwind is computed in a **pinned order** that only looks circular. Step one: `sale_price_refund_cents` is copied from the sale's `sale_price_cents`. Step two: transport allocation runs and yields `transport_buyer_share_cents`. Step three: `fee_offset_applied_cents` is the smaller of the buyer's outstanding `frivolous_fee_balance_cents` and the **gross** refund from step one — deliberately measured against the gross figure, not against what is left after the buyer's transport share. Step four: `total_buyer_payout_cents` is the gross refund minus `transport_buyer_share_cents` minus `fee_offset_applied_cents`; because step three ignored the transport deduction, a large fee balance can drive this figure negative, and that is the specified result. Step five: the buyer's `frivolous_fee_balance_cents` is reduced by `fee_offset_applied_cents`, and when that offset is greater than 0 a `FRIVOLOUS_FEE_OFFSET` pair is posted debiting the buyer the offset and crediting the auction house the same. Step six: because the balance may now have reached 0, a buyer sitting in `FEE_HOLD` returns to `ACTIVE` — but only when its `frivolous_claim_count` is still below 3.

**Outcome `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE`.** The consignor pays for the defect and keeps the sale. Three additional gates apply, on top of the case status and segregation checks. The case's `seller_conceded` must be false and the policy's `max_allowance_bps` must be greater than 0, or the decision is refused with `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY` — a consignor who has already conceded cannot be handed a cheaper outcome, and a policy that forbids allowances cannot be worked around. For every family other than `MILEAGE_DISCREPANCY`, the case's `latest_verified_estimate_cents` must be greater than 0, or the request is refused with `ERR_THIRD_PARTY_VERIFICATION_REQUIRED`: the department will not invent a repair figure from an unverified file. And `requested_allowance_cents` must not exceed the policy cap, or `ERR_ALLOWANCE_EXCEEDS_CAP`. The decision is then written with `allowance_variant` `ARBITRATOR_AWARD`, its `computed_award_cents` and its `allowance_cents` as described above, and the ledger receives a `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` pair crediting the buyer `allowance_cents` and debiting the consignor the same, plus the `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` transport batches. The sale's `settlement_status` is left completely untouched — the allowance branch never reverses proceeds. The case reaches `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW → RESOLVED_ALLOWANCE`, the same terminal status a negotiated acceptance reaches from `NEGOTIATION`.

**Outcome `CLAIM_DENIED`.** The claim fails on its merits. The `seller_conceded` flag must still be false, or `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY`. The decision is written with `allowance_variant` `NONE`, `allowance_cents` 0 and `frivolous_fee_cents` 0, and transport is allocated entirely to the buyer. The case reaches `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW → DENIED`. What distinguishes this branch is what it does *not* do: the consignor's `lifetime_upheld_count` is not incremented and its scorecard is not recomputed, because a denial is not an upheld claim. There is no *standing* consequence for the consignor on this branch — no tier movement, no policy action, no settlement hold, and no refresh of the six stored scorecard figures either, which stay exactly as the last upheld decision left them and may therefore trail the consignor's actual sale count. What does still happen is the counter release every outcome shares: the consignor's `open_claim_count` falls by 1 and its `version` rises by 1.

**Outcome `FRIVOLOUS_DENIED`.** The claim fails and the buyer is billed for wasting the department's time. As with a plain denial the `seller_conceded` flag must be false, or `ERR_UNWIND_MANDATORY`; the decision carries `allowance_variant` `NONE` and `allowance_cents` 0; transport falls entirely on the buyer; and the case reaches `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW → DENIED`. Four hops then follow, each caused by the one before it:

- **Hop 1.** A `FrivolousFeeAssessment` row is created. It records its `case_id`, the charged `buyer_id`, the buyer's `frivolous_claim_count` as read immediately *before* this assessment as `prior_frivolous_count`, an `assessed_at_utc` from the logical clock, a `reason` that is a verbatim copy of the decision's `rationale` and is never supplied by the client, and a `fee_cents` computed as follows: the base fee of 25000 cents multiplied by one plus `prior_frivolous_count`, capped at 60000 cents, whichever is smaller. The ladder is therefore 25000 cents for a buyer's first frivolous claim, 50000 for the second, and 60000 for the third, since 75000 is clipped by the cap. The same figure is mirrored onto the decision as `frivolous_fee_cents`, which is 0 for every other outcome. A `FRIVOLOUS_FEE_ASSESSED` pair is posted, debiting the buyer the fee and crediting the auction house the same.
- **Hop 2.** Because the assessment row now exists, the buyer's `frivolous_claim_count` rises by 1 and its `frivolous_fee_balance_cents` rises by `fee_cents`.
- **Hop 3.** Because the balance is now greater than 0, the buyer's `claim_privilege_status` moves `ACTIVE → FEE_HOLD` — unless the incremented `frivolous_claim_count` has reached 3, in which case the buyer goes to `SUSPENDED` instead, arriving at the suspension in the same request. That suspension is reached from whatever privilege status the buyer held going in, not only from `FEE_HOLD`: a buyer who had paid its balance down and returned to `ACTIVE` before earning its third frivolous claim moves `ACTIVE → SUSPENDED` in one step, and the count test outranks the balance test, so the third assessment suspends even though the balance is what would otherwise have produced a fee hold.
- **Hop 4.** Because the buyer has reached `SUSPENDED`, every post-sale inspection still in `REQUESTED` on any sale whose buyer is this buyer is moved to `EXPIRED`, processed in ascending `created_at` order, each such inspection's `version` rising by 1.

The buyer's `version` rises by 1 across this cascade, as does the case's. Because the fee cap of 60000 sits below the third rung of the ladder and the third assessment is also the one that suspends the buyer, the cap is observable exactly once — and since a `SUSPENDED` buyer can file no further claims, no fourth assessment can ever exist.

Every outcome shares one closing set of effects: the sale's `arbitration_status` moves `ACTIVE → RESOLVED`, its `open_case_count` falls by 1, the buyer's `open_claim_count` falls by 1, the consignor's `open_claim_count` falls by 1, and the case, the sale, the buyer and the consignor each have their `version` incremented by 1. The two upheld outcomes — `UNWIND` and `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` — additionally increment the consignor's `lifetime_upheld_count`, recompute its scorecard, may append a `SellerPolicyAction`, and may set `settlement_hold` across that consignor's sales, exactly as described in §3.9. One thing no decision does is tidy the negotiation record: unlike escalation and unlike withdrawal, rendering a decision supersedes nothing, so a round left in `COUNTERED` by a consignor's dispute is still sitting in `COUNTERED` after the case has been decided, and no round's `version` moves on the decision request. The frivolous fee assessed on a case and the policy actions a case triggered can each be listed from the case, the latter being the discovery channel for rows that no client ever asked to create.

### 3.9. Consequences: Seller Standing, Buyer Privileges, Abandonment and the Deadline Sweep

Arbitration outcomes only change behaviour if they accumulate, so the department keeps a running scorecard on every consignor and a running conduct record on every buying dealer.

**The seller scorecard.** A consignor's arbitration rate is recomputed whenever an upheld decision is created against one of its sales — that is, whenever a decision with `outcome` `UNWIND` or `REPAIR_ALLOWANCE` comes into existence, whether it was rendered by an officer or minted by a buyer accepting an offer. Decisions with `outcome` `CLAIM_DENIED` or `FRIVOLOUS_DENIED` are excluded from the arithmetic entirely.

The recomputation produces two variants side by side. The rolling variant looks back exactly 90 days from the current logical clock: `rolling_window_sales_count` counts the consignor's sales whose `hammer_time_utc` falls strictly after the window start and at or before now, and `rolling_window_upheld_count` counts the upheld decisions against **any** of the consignor's sales whose `decided_at_utc` falls strictly after the window start and at or before now. The two counts are filtered on independent axes and are not nested: a decision is counted on its own `decided_at_utc` alone, so an upheld decision rendered inside the window against a sale hammered long before it opened raises `rolling_window_upheld_count` while contributing nothing to `rolling_window_sales_count` — which is precisely how a rolling rate above 10000 basis points can arise. The window is half-open on the left and inclusive on the right for both axes, so a sale whose `hammer_time_utc` lands exactly on the window start is **outside** the window while one landing exactly on the current clock is inside it. The lifetime variant applies no time filter at all: `lifetime_sales_count` counts every sale the consignor has run and `lifetime_upheld_count` every upheld decision against it. Each variant's rate is then 0 when its sales count is 0, and otherwise 10000 multiplied by the upheld count, divided by the sales count, rounded half up to a whole number of basis points — stored as `rolling_arbitration_rate_bps` and `lifetime_arbitration_rate_bps` respectively.

**Only the rolling variant moves the tier.** The lifetime figures are reported for context and never drive a consequence, so a consignor with a dreadful lifetime record and a clean recent quarter stays in `GOOD_STANDING`. The tier is a pure function of the rolling rate, evaluated in this order. If the current `policy_tier` is already `BLOCKED`, it does not change — `BLOCKED` is sticky and no recomputation can leave it. Otherwise, if `rolling_window_sales_count` is below 4 the tier is `GOOD_STANDING`, because a rate computed from a handful of sales says nothing: a consignor with 3 in-window sales and 1 upheld claim shows a rate of 3333 basis points and still stays in `GOOD_STANDING`. Otherwise the tier is `BLOCKED` when the rolling rate is at least 2500 basis points, `PROBATION` when it is at least 1500, `WATCH` when it is at least 800, and `GOOD_STANDING` below that. The ordinary escalations are therefore `GOOD_STANDING → WATCH`, `WATCH → PROBATION` and `PROBATION → BLOCKED`. Because the tier is a function rather than a one-step ladder, tier jumps are legitimate too: `GOOD_STANDING → BLOCKED` in a single recomputation is a lawful move whenever the rolling rate clears 2500 basis points from a standing start.

Each escalation appends a `SellerPolicyAction` row recording its `seller_id`, the `triggered_by_case_id` of the case whose decision moved the tier, an `action_type` of `WATCH_LIST` for a move into `WATCH`, `PROBATION` for a move into `PROBATION` or `BLOCK` for a move into `BLOCKED`, the `from_tier` and `to_tier` either side of the move, the `rolling_arbitration_rate_bps` at that instant as `rolling_rate_bps_at_action`, an `effective_at_utc` from the logical clock, and a server-generated `note` reading exactly `auto tier change at <rolling_rate_bps_at_action> bps` with the numeric rate substituted. Clients never supply the `note` on an automatic action. De-escalation is deliberately silent: a consignor whose rolling rate falls back below 800 basis points, or whose in-window sales count drops below 4, moves `WATCH → GOOD_STANDING` with **no** `SellerPolicyAction` appended at all. That silence belongs to `GOOD_STANDING` alone, though, because the row is keyed on the tier the consignor moved *into* rather than on the direction of travel: any move into `WATCH`, `PROBATION` or `BLOCKED` appends its action, so a consignor sliding back `PROBATION → WATCH` on an improving rate appends a `WATCH_LIST` action exactly as a consignor climbing `GOOD_STANDING → WATCH` does. A recomputation that leaves the tier where it already was appends nothing either, even though it still refreshes the six figures and still bumps the consignor's `version`.

Reaching `BLOCKED` carries two further effects. `blocked_at_utc` is stamped with the logical-clock instant of the block, and it is null at every other time. And the settlement hold cascade runs: every sale of that consignor whose `settlement_status` is `UNSETTLED` **and** whose `arbitration_status` is `NONE` has `settlement_hold` set to true, processed in ascending `created_at` order, each sale's `version` rising by 1. The predicate is narrow on purpose — sales already settled cannot be held and sales already under dispute have their own machinery — and its practical effect is that a blocked consignor's undisputed pipeline money stops moving. Both effects belong to the *transition* rather than to the tier: they fire only when a recomputation carries the consignor into `BLOCKED` from some other tier, so a further upheld decision against an already-blocked consignor refreshes the six figures and bumps its `version` while re-running neither the hold cascade nor the `blocked_at_utc` stamp. And because the deciding case's own sale has already moved `ACTIVE → RESOLVED` by the time the cascade walks the consignor's sales, that sale fails the `arbitration_status` `NONE` test and is never itself placed on hold — the block reaches the consignor's *other* pipeline, not the unit that caused it.

**Reinstatement.** Only an `AuctionAdmin` can release a blocked consignor, supplying a `reinstate_note` of at least 1 character that is not blank — no upper length bound applies to that field. The consignor's `policy_tier` must be `BLOCKED`, or the request is refused with `ERR_SELLER_NOT_BLOCKED`. Reinstatement moves the tier `BLOCKED → PROBATION` rather than all the way back to `GOOD_STANDING`, clears `blocked_at_utc` to null, increments `reinstated_count` by 1, appends a `SellerPolicyAction` with `action_type` `REINSTATE`, a `from_tier` of `BLOCKED` and a `to_tier` of `PROBATION`, a null `triggered_by_case_id` — no case caused it — a `rolling_rate_bps_at_action` copied straight from the consignor's *stored* `rolling_arbitration_rate_bps`, since reinstatement recomputes no scorecard and leaves every counter and rate on the record exactly as the last upheld decision left them, and the supplied `reinstate_note` copied verbatim into `note`, clears `settlement_hold` on every one of that consignor's sales in ascending `created_at` order while bumping each sale's `version`, and increments the consignor's own `version` by 1. This closes the only cycle out of `BLOCKED`: `PROBATION → BLOCKED → PROBATION`.

The scorecard can also be read directly. That read recomputes both variants against the current logical clock and answers with an object carrying `seller_id`, `rolling_window_sales_count`, `rolling_window_upheld_count`, `rolling_arbitration_rate_bps`, `lifetime_sales_count`, `lifetime_upheld_count`, `lifetime_arbitration_rate_bps`, the `policy_tier` those figures produce when they are fed to the same tier function the stored tier uses — so the consignor's currently stored `policy_tier` enters the answer only through the stickiness clause, a consignor already `BLOCKED` reading `BLOCKED` whatever the recomputed figures say, while from any other stored tier the reported tier is simply whatever the recomputed rolling figures produce, be that above or below the stored one — and a `computed_at_utc` stamp. It never mutates the consignor row — a read must not bump a `version` or move a tier — which means a scorecard read after the clock has advanced past a 90-day boundary can legitimately report figures that differ from the ones stored on the consignor. Every policy action recorded against a consignor can be listed in ascending `created_at` order.

**Buyer conduct and fee payment.** On the buyer side the conduct record is the frivolous-fee ledger of §3.8. A `BuyerRep` pays down the balance by supplying an `amount_cents` figure and the `expected_buyer_version` of §2.6. `amount_cents` is a non-negative integer, and 0 is a legal payment: it moves no balance and is not an error, yet like any other successful payment it still bumps the buyer's `version`. The buyer being paid for is the one named in the path, and this is the one buyer-facing write with **no** ownership check at all — any `BuyerRep` may pay down any buyer's balance, so the acting `X-User-ID` is never compared against the path buyer's `id` and no `ERR_NOT_SALE_BUYER` or `ERR_NOT_CASE_OWNER` can arise here. The stale-version check is evaluated first; then `amount_cents` must not exceed the outstanding `frivolous_fee_balance_cents`, or the payment is refused with `ERR_PAYMENT_EXCEEDS_BALANCE` — the department does not hold buyer credit. A successful payment reduces `frivolous_fee_balance_cents` by `amount_cents`, posts **no** `SettlementLedgerEntry` at all because the money never touches a sale's books, and increments the buyer's `version` by 1. When the balance reaches 0 and the buyer's `frivolous_claim_count` is still below 3, `claim_privilege_status` returns from `FEE_HOLD` to `ACTIVE`, closing the cycle `ACTIVE → FEE_HOLD → ACTIVE`. A buyer in `SUSPENDED` stays `SUSPENDED`: paying the balance down to zero does not restore claim privileges, because the suspension was earned by the third claim and not by the debt. Every fee assessed against a buyer can be listed in ascending `created_at` order.

**Abandonment by the buyer.** A `BuyerRep` may withdraw its own claim at any point before it is decided, supplying a `withdraw_reason`. Withdrawal is permitted from `SUBMITTED`, `UNDER_REVIEW`, `NEGOTIATION` and `COUNTERED`; from any terminal status it is refused with `ERR_INVALID_CASE_STATE`, and from another buyer's case with `ERR_NOT_CASE_OWNER`. The case moves to `WITHDRAWN` — `SUBMITTED → WITHDRAWN`, `UNDER_REVIEW → WITHDRAWN`, `NEGOTIATION → WITHDRAWN` or `COUNTERED → WITHDRAWN` according to where it stood; every round on it still in `OPEN` or `COUNTERED` becomes `SUPERSEDED` — `OPEN → SUPERSEDED` or `COUNTERED → SUPERSEDED` — processed in ascending `created_at` order; the sale's `open_case_count`, the buyer's `open_claim_count` and the consignor's `open_claim_count` each fall by 1; and when the sale's `open_case_count` reaches 0 and no `ArbitrationDecision` exists on any of that sale's cases, the sale's `arbitration_status` reverts `ACTIVE → NONE`. The case, the sale, the buyer and the consignor each have their `version` incremented by 1. This is the compensating half of the counter conservation of §2.9: filing and then withdrawing must leave all four aggregate conditions exactly where they started.

**The deadline sweep.** Because the logical clock only moves when an operator moves it, nothing expires by itself. An `AuctionAdmin` drives expiry with a sweep capability that takes no payload — any body sent with it is ignored rather than rejected — runs four phases against the current logical clock in a fixed order, and answers `200 OK` with a report object carrying exactly the four counts `{ inspections_expired, rounds_expired, seller_response_timeouts, cases_expired }` and nothing else: the report is a plain tally, not a resource, so it bears no `id`, no `created_at` and no list of the rows it moved. Within each phase rows are processed in ascending `created_at` order.

- **Phase 1 — inspections.** Every `PostSaleInspection` whose `status` is `REQUESTED` and whose `window_end_utc` the clock has reached or passed moves `REQUESTED → EXPIRED`, incrementing its `version` by 1. This boundary is **inclusive**: an inspection whose `window_end_utc` exactly equals the current clock expires. Expiring it frees the sale's inspection interval for a fresh purchase.
- **Phase 2 — lapsed offers.** Every `NegotiationRound` whose `status` is `OPEN` and whose `response_deadline_utc` the clock has passed **strictly** moves `OPEN → EXPIRED`, and its parent case moves `NEGOTIATION → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW` with `fault_party` set to `BUYER`, because the buyer is the party who let a standing offer lapse. Both rows have their `version` incremented by 1. A round whose deadline exactly equals the current clock does **not** expire.
- **Phase 3 — silent consignors.** Every `ArbitrationCase` whose `status` is `UNDER_REVIEW` and for which the clock has passed `review_opened_at_utc` plus the governing policy's `seller_response_hours` — **strictly** past, so a case sitting exactly on that instant does not time out and waits for a later sweep — moves `UNDER_REVIEW → ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`. A `NegotiationRound` is minted for it with `response_type` `TIMEOUT`, `seller_offer_cents` 0, `status` `CLOSED` and a server-generated `response_note` reading exactly `seller response timed out`, since no consignor supplied one; the case's `seller_response_timed_out` is set true and its `fault_party` to `SELLER`; its `current_round_index` rises by 1 and its `version` by 1. Setting `seller_response_timed_out` is also what unlocks escalation on a case that never reached the round limit.
- **Phase 4 — abandoned intakes.** Every `ArbitrationCase` still in `SUBMITTED` for which the clock has passed `claimed_at_utc` plus a 48-hour intake grace period — **strictly** past here too, so a case standing exactly 48 hours after `claimed_at_utc` survives this sweep — moves `SUBMITTED → EXPIRED`, releasing counters exactly as a withdrawal does: the sale's `open_case_count`, the buyer's `open_claim_count` and the consignor's `open_claim_count` each fall by 1, the sale's `arbitration_status` reverts `ACTIVE → NONE` when its `open_case_count` reaches 0 with no decision on the sale, and the case, sale, buyer and consignor each have their `version` incremented by 1.

The phase order is itself a business rule, not an implementation detail. Phase 2 runs before phase 3 so that a case whose open offer has lapsed is attributed to the `BUYER` rather than being swept up as a consignor timeout, and the difference is directly observable in the case's `fault_party`. Each phase feeds exactly one of the four counters and the counters never overlap: `rounds_expired` counts only the rounds phase 2 lapsed and not the round phase 3 mints, and `cases_expired` counts only the phase-4 abandonments and never the cases phases 2 and 3 pushed into `ARBITRATOR_REVIEW`, so a sweep that moves one lapsed offer and one silent consignor reports `rounds_expired` 1 and `seller_response_timeouts` 1 with `cases_expired` still 0. The sweep is idempotent: called a second time without advancing the clock, it moves nothing and reports 0 for all four counters.
